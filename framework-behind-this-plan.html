The framework behind this plan

Our plan isn’t ideology. It’s a design framework applied honestly to our home — and this page is that framework, shown plainly, with the mapping made explicit.

The framework is called Permanent Culture 3.0: a place-free, universal design system for how living things — ecosystems, organizations, cities — actually stay alive over time, built from what’s been learned across ecology, systems thinking, and the study of durable institutions. It carries no Toronto marks and asks nothing of any city. Read it on its own terms at Permanent Culture 3.0.

This movement is that framework pointed at one city, once. Here is the mapping, stated plainly rather than left implicit:

  • FLOOR ← guard the slow stores. A city’s people are its substrate, and substrate has to hold before anything is built on top of it — a floor under everyone is this project’s slow store, the thing that has to stay stable for everything else to work.
  • COMMONS ← index, don’t hoard; cooperation needs rules. What this project shares — the research, the tools, the plan itself — is governed the way any working commons has to be governed: real boundaries on who the group is, rules the group itself can shape, and a wall against any one party capturing what belongs to everyone.
  • PRACTICE ← feed the edge; let evidence rewire structure. The weekly conversation, the kit run in somebody’s own room, the block circle meeting because it chose to — that’s the fast layer, the place new information enters the system and the place structure gets rewritten when the evidence says to.
  • RECEIPTS ← instrument; build so the picture can be checked, including against yourself. The publishing discipline — sourced claims, labeled targets, public corrections — is the reflexive lens the framework calls for, actually built and actually running.

That mapping is not decoration. It means this project can say, truthfully: our plan isn’t ideology — it’s a design framework applied honestly to our home, and here’s the framework. The link runs one way, on purpose: this page points to Permanent Culture 3.0 as the thinking behind our plan; Permanent Culture 3.0 never points back to recruit anyone into ours. It stays what it is — a gift to think with, for any city, including ones that never hear of Toronto at all.

A Tuesday in the Practiced City

Here is that mapping again, shown rather than stated — a picture, not a promise, of one ordinary day in a Toronto that practiced this framework for a few years. It labels itself as exactly that:

Before you read this: it hasn’t happened. It might not. This is a picture of an ordinary Tuesday in a Toronto that practiced for a few years. Every image in it belongs to a real project with a public test it must pass — and when a project fails its test, we cross its image out, right here, in public. The ledger of what’s standing and what’s struck is at the foot of the page. Keep that in your pocket as you read.

Six-forty a.m., and the light comes up over a city that slept better than it used to, because fewer people were lying awake doing the rent-or-groceries math. The floor did that. Not riches — just the absence of freefall. A city sounds different when falling isn’t the background noise.

On the streetcar, a kid rides free with her benefit-linked pass and doesn’t think about it, which is the point — dignity that works is boring. She’s reading a book one grade too hard for her, because her block’s learning circle meets tonight and she wants to have opinions.

The morning crews are out — not an army, neighbours with a schedule. Small rotating teams that adopted their ravine two years back and know it the way you know a friend’s moods: where the water pools wrong, which slope the invasives start on, when the salmon are due. On the fence, a laminated card with a hand-drawn figure holding a small light: Cities aren’t saved. They’re practiced. Someone drew the figure themselves. It’s a bit crooked. Everyone’s is.

Late morning at the tool commons — the library annex where the pottery wheels, the guitars, the soldering irons and the good ladder live. A man returns a borrowed cello slightly late and slightly embarrassed; the librarian checking it in books him for open-studio night, because she saw him practicing in the stairwell on the camera feed he asked her to delete — and she deleted it, because the commons doesn’t keep what it doesn’t need. That’s in its founding rules. People checked.

Lunch is loud in three languages at the long tables outside the food depot — the buyers’ co-op that never took anything over, just made good food cheap by buying like a city instead of like ten thousand strangers. An elder teaches a newcomer the word for the eggplant dish; the newcomer teaches it back in her own language. Both get it wrong. Nobody minds.

Mid-afternoon, a ward assembly — second Tuesday, the room mixed by lottery so it never sorts into the same six voices. They’re arguing, really arguing, about the waterfront. But the receipts are on the table, the facilitator is paid and trained, and at the end the decision goes somewhere — everyone in the room can name where, and can check on it next month. Nobody calls it historic. It’s Tuesday.

Evening: the neighbourhood games. Forty-one adults playing something between soccer and tag, badly, on a permitted field, while the good players hang back and coach — that’s the custom now: the better you are, the more you give away. Nobody was picked last, because nobody was picked. You showed up, and that was the whole tryout. Somewhere downtown they’re still figuring out the massive dance party. They’ll get there.

And at the end of it — the receipts, glowing quietly on the public dashboard nobody visits on happy days, which is fine. It’s there for the other days. It says what improved, what didn’t, and what we stopped doing because it failed its own test — the failures in the same typeface as the wins. A city that can read its own scoreboard without flinching. That’s the whole trick. That was always the whole trick.
