Scope

This page’s neutral scope question (per the carried-forward briefing's own framing, adapted): what does the evidence actually show, as of mid-2026, about youth labour-market conditions, NEET rates, skilled-trades/apprenticeship pathways, and belonging/third-place infrastructure for young people in Toronto and Ontario, and how contested is the claim that AI is specifically driving current youth-employment weakness? This document covers: current Statistics Canada youth unemployment and NEET/NEETEST data (national, Ontario, and Toronto-specific where available); Ontario's skilled-trades/apprenticeship registration trends; direct evidence on whether AI is a measurable driver of Toronto's youth-unemployment gap; and youth belonging, third-place, and civic-engagement data. It hands off, rather than duplicates: the macro AI-labour-market and income-replacement debate to ai-work-income-replacement; the human/purpose dimension of a post-work transition to ai-life-beyond-work; youth violence prevention specifically to gun-gang-youth-violence-prevention; and youth homelessness/shelter capacity to homelessness-youth-shelter-capacity.

Current state

Youth unemployment: national improvement in 2026, but a persistent, Toronto-concentrated gap

Statistics Canada's own June 2026 Labour Force Survey release reports the national youth (15-24) unemployment rate fell 0.7 percentage points to 12.7% in June 2026, following a 0.9-point decline in May, and was "comparable to the recent low of 12.8% observed in November 2025 and January 2026" — though "it remains higher than the pre-pandemic average of 10.8% observed during the period from 2017 to 2019" [NEW-2026-1]. This is a real improvement from the acute weakness documented in late 2025: the national youth unemployment rate reached 14.6%-14.7% in September 2025, "the highest rate since September 2010 (excluding the COVID-19 pandemic years of 2020 and 2021)" [NEW-2026-2]. Toronto's own labour market has lagged this national recovery specifically among young workers. TD Economics' own analysis, using Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey microdata directly, found that as of September 2025 "a concerning one-in-five youths in Toronto's labour market" were unemployed, and that "the youth unemployment rate in Toronto has seen the largest increase" of any age group in the city since late 2023, rising roughly 5 percentage points versus roughly 3.5 points in the rest of Ontario over the same period [NEW-2026-3]. Toronto's overall unemployment rate itself has since eased — Statistics Canada's June 2026 release found the Toronto CMA unemployment rate "little changed at 6.9%... following a downward trend from a recent high of 8.5% in February" [NEW-2026-1] — but no Toronto-specific youth figure as current as June 2026 was located in this review, leaving the most current Toronto-specific youth number (roughly one-in-five, September 2025) [NEW-2026-3] several months older than the national June 2026 figure it sits alongside; this gap in currency is flagged rather than papered over.

A direct, Toronto-specific test of the "AI is driving youth unemployment" claim — tested and not confirmed

This is the single most load-bearing new finding of this review, and it speaks directly to a claim the carried-forward briefing treats as its foundational premise. TD Economics did not merely speculate about AI's role in Toronto's youth-unemployment rise — it explicitly tested it, using Statistics Canada's own occupational AI-exposure/complementarity classification as a benchmark, and reported: "we tested this for Toronto's jobs market, using a recent Statistics Canada study as a benchmark to classify industries by their 'exposure' and 'complementarity' to AI. We found that young workers in Toronto employed in 'high exposure' industries saw robust job growth since 2023. Accordingly, we didn't find much evidence that AI is a contributing factor for the recent surge in youth unemployment" [NEW-2026-3]. TD's own analysis instead attributes the Toronto-specific youth-unemployment surge to a different, concrete mechanism: "older workers (facing a soft jobs market and rising living costs) are taking roles in industries that tend to hire a large share of younger workers" — specifically accommodation/food services and retail trade, where TD's own chart shows non-youth employment up roughly 8% year-to-date against youth employment down roughly 14% in the same GTA sectors [NEW-2026-3]. This is a materially important, Toronto-specific update the carried-forward briefing's national-level framing did not have: the carried-forward briefing's premise that "the entry-level job is exactly what AI eliminates first" [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing] is not confirmed, and is directly contradicted on this specific point, by the one Toronto-focused analysis located in this review that actually tested it against local data. This does not resolve the broader, slower-moving question of whether AI will eventually affect entry-level work at a longer time horizon — TD's own analysis is a test of the 2023-2025 period specifically, not a forecast — but it is a real, dated, Toronto-specific finding that the "youth unemployment is currently being driven by AI" claim should be treated as contested rather than assumed.

NEET rates: stable snapshot rate near historical range, but a stickier long-duration measure

Statistics Canada's newer NEETEST measure — "not in employment, education or training, excluding short transitions," designed to exclude youth in normal job-to-job or job-to-school transitions and to exclude unpaid caregivers — found roughly 7% to 8% of youth aged 20-29 were persistently disconnected (a full calendar year with no work, school, or caregiving) each year from 2017 to 2022, while the conventional snapshot NEET rate over the same period ran roughly 13% to 14% (peaking at 19.2% in 2020) [NEW-2026-4]. Ontario's own 2022 NEET rate (13.4%) and NEETEST rate (7.7%) were both close to the national average, neither best- nor worst-performing among provinces [NEW-2026-4]. The same analysis found NEETEST-classified youth were more concentrated among women (8.5% vs. 6.7% for men), older youth within the 20-29 band (8.6% for 25-29 vs. 6.5% for 20-24), those with no postsecondary credential (28.6% for those without a high school diploma), and youth outside census metropolitan areas (12.4% vs. 6.8% inside a CMA) — meaning Toronto, as a CMA, sits on the lower-risk side of this specific geographic split, a detail the carried-forward briefing's national framing did not surface [NEW-2026-4]. No Toronto-specific (rather than Canada-wide-CMA) NEET or NEETEST figure was located in this review; this is flagged as a genuine gap below.

Skilled trades and apprenticeship: a real, recent registration increase, with an active provincial investment program

Ontario's own apprenticeship-registration data shows a substantive recent increase: new apprenticeship registrations reached 28,875 in the 2024-2025 fiscal year, "the highest in a decade," a 7.3% increase, including a 13.5% increase to 16,412 registrations specifically among youth aged 15 to 24 [NEW-2026-5]. This sits alongside an active provincial funding commitment: in October 2025 Ontario announced a $64.2 million investment in skilled-trades apprenticeship training, including an "In-Class Enhancement Fund" intended to create "up to 4,000 new training seats annually" and cover the $10-per-day Level 1 classroom fee apprentices otherwise pay [NEW-2026-5]. Administratively, responsibility for registering Ontario Youth Apprenticeship Program (OYAP) training agreements — the high-school-embedded pathway into the trades — transferred from the Ministry of Labour, Immigration, Training and Skills Development directly to the arm's-length agency Skilled Trades Ontario as of April 2, 2025 [NEW-2026-6]. This is a real, Ontario-specific, currently active pathway distinct from the carried-forward briefing's national-level "meaningful-work infrastructure" framing, and directly relevant to the entry-level-job-ladder question the carried-forward briefing raises, since apprenticeship is precisely a structured, credentialed on-ramp of the kind the briefing worries AI is eliminating elsewhere in the labour market.

Belonging and third places: a documented, youth-concentrated disconnection gap, with Toronto specifically named

A YMCA Canada/YMCA of Greater Toronto survey (Angus Reid Forum panel, n=1,501, conducted August 29-September 3, 2024) found 60% of Canadians reported feeling disconnected from their community "only sometimes" or "never," with disconnection "particularly acute among Canadians under 55": 68% of 18-34-year-olds and 64% of 25-54-year-olds reported a lack of belonging, both higher than the overall 60% figure [NEW-2026-7]. The same release directly names Toronto: the YMCA of Greater Toronto's own CEO cited "a recent report from The Toronto Foundation" finding "Toronto is the loneliest city in Canada," while noting the disconnection problem is not unique to Toronto [NEW-2026-7]. On third places specifically, the same survey found 36% of Canadians reported having no "third place" (a community space outside home or work), while those who did have one reported measurably higher belonging and connection; a related YMCA-cited follow-on figure found third places "especially important to 26-34 year olds," with three-in-four in that age band saying access to one helps them feel more connected to their community [NEW-2026-7, NEW-2026-8]. On screen time specifically, the survey found the average Canadian spends three hours a day on their phone, with 18-34-year-olds averaging nearly four hours, and 71% of 18-34-year-olds reporting that social-media scrolling specifically heightens feelings of isolation [NEW-2026-7]. This directly substantiates, with dated and Toronto-named survey evidence, the carried-forward briefing's belonging-crisis framing, which was asserted at a general level without this specific sourcing [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing].

Toronto's own municipal youth-employment programming: a named, currently active City initiative

The City of Toronto's Building Skills Through Recreation program — "free employment readiness, such as training and certifications, designed for youth aged 15 to 24" — is being doubled in capacity for 2026, "enabling up to 2,000 youth to participate," with "more than 100 certification courses and 60 local hiring events in 2026" and "a strong focus on serving equity-deserving communities and underserved neighbourhoods," per a November 25, 2025 City of Toronto news release [NEW-2026-9]. The same release states "more than 16,000 youth are employed across the City system" each year, and references a companion staff report, "Reducing Barriers to Youth Employment," presented to the City's Economic and Community Development Committee on December 2, 2025, proposing five actions addressing barriers facing "Indigenous, Black, newcomer and 2SLGBTQ+ youth" specifically in entering the labour market [NEW-2026-9]. This is a real, current, City-controlled program directly relevant to the carried-forward briefing's "meaningful-work infrastructure" recommendation category, distinct from and more specific than the general national/international precedents the carried-forward briefing cites.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

The carried-forward briefing's foundational premise — that AI is specifically ending the entry-level job ladder young people rely on — is directly tested against Toronto data by an independent economic analysis, and not confirmed. TD Economics explicitly checked whether AI-exposed industries in Toronto showed the youth-employment weakness the carried-forward premise predicts, and instead found "robust job growth" in those industries among young workers, attributing the actual Toronto youth-unemployment surge to older workers displacing younger workers within traditionally youth-heavy sectors (accommodation/food services, retail) amid a soft broader economy [NEW-2026-3]. This does not prove AI will never affect entry-level work — it is a test of a specific 2023-2025 window, not a permanent verdict — but it is a real, named, methodologically explicit finding in tension with the carried-forward briefing's own governing assumption, and this document states that tension rather than resolving it in either direction.

National youth-unemployment improvement (June 2026) sits against an older but more severe Toronto-specific figure (September 2025), with no equally current Toronto-specific update located. The most recent national data shows real improvement (12.7% in June 2026, down from the September 2025 high) [NEW-2026-1], while the most current Toronto-specific youth figure located in this review is from September 2025 and shows roughly one-in-five youth unemployed in Toronto specifically, a materially worse figure than the national rate at the same time [NEW-2026-3]. Whether Toronto's youth-specific rate has improved in step with the national trend, lagged it, or diverged further is not established by any source located in this review — flagged as an open question below rather than assumed to track the national trend.

The carried-forward briefing's own intergenerational and inherited-advantage equity argument, restored in full (2026-07-16, not independently re-verified this review). Beyond the demographic gaps this document's own NEETEST data already confirms (women, older 20-29s, those without a high-school diploma, non-CMA youth), the carried-forward briefing makes a normative argument this document had not previously carried: the entry-level jobs AI is said to eliminate were precisely how low-income, racialized, newcomer, less-credentialed, and first-generation young people entered the economy without family wealth or connections, so as those roles vanish, youth with capital, credentials, and networks will adapt (and may capture new AI-era opportunities) while those without are locked out first and hardest — a widening of inequality the briefing frames as risking a durable "caste line" between the connected and the rest unless the response is deliberately equity-first [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (youth opportunity belonging)]. The briefing adds a distinct intergenerational-equity dimension: this generation is being asked to come of age as the jobs era ends, inheriting a labour market hollowed out by a transition prior generations benefited from building, which the briefing treats as strengthening (not merely accompanying) the moral claim to an income floor and real support [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (youth opportunity belonging)]. ⚠️ still being checked — this is a normative argument, not an empirical claim independently testable against this review's own labour-market data; it is restored as the master briefing's own stated position, not as a finding this document has confirmed.

A real apprenticeship-registration increase sits against no located data on completion rates, program quality, or whether registration growth reflects genuine new on-ramp capacity or a shift from other pathways. The 13.5% increase in youth apprenticeship registrations [NEW-2026-5] is a real, dated, positive figure, but this review did not locate completion-rate, retention, or displacement data that would establish whether this represents a net expansion of youth pathways or a reallocation from other forms of youth employment — stated as a gap, not assumed either way.

The carried-forward briefing's own explicit caution against letting "no jobs" become despair or fatalism, restored here (2026-07-17, a later adversarial verification pass, completing a residual gap the 2026-07-16 pass had honestly named and deferred rather than silently dropped). The briefing warns that a real risk of the post-jobs framing itself is fatalism — telling young people "there's no future in work" in a way that deepens hopelessness, or treating "AI will take the jobs anyway" as a license to stop investing in youth's skills, education, and opportunity; the framing the briefing insists on is the opposite of despair — the jobs era is ending, and there is endless meaningful work, security can be provided, and young people are needed [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (youth opportunity belonging)]. The briefing is explicit that "no jobs" is not an argument against investing in young people's development, but an argument for changing what that development is aimed at — purpose, contribution, adaptability, and the work that needs doing, not only slotting into vanishing entry-level roles [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (youth opportunity belonging)]. This document does not independently test whether this rhetorical caution is being heeded in practice by any specific Toronto program; it is restored here as the master briefing's own explicit framing instruction, not as an empirical finding this review has verified.

Two further "other genuine tensions" from the carried-forward briefing, restored here (2026-07-17, a later adversarial verification pass), distinct from the jurisdiction point (a verified sibling hand-off to ai-work-income-replacement, above) and the equity point (already restored in this section above). First, the briefing names a transition danger distinct from the contested-premise question itself: if jobs vanish before an income floor exists — what the briefing calls "the likely near-term path" — young people suffer acutely in the gap, making the urgency of income security and bridging support real and immediate regardless of how the longer-run AI-jobs debate eventually resolves [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (youth opportunity belonging)]. Second, the briefing names a distinct difficulty in what jobs provide beyond income: structure, identity, and social connection, which a post-jobs agenda must deliberately replace rather than assume will appear on their own — a task the briefing itself calls "hard and essential" [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (youth opportunity belonging)]. ⚠️ still being checked — both are normative/policy-design arguments carried from the master briefing, not independently tested against this document's own labour-market or belonging-survey data in this review; the fifth "other genuine tension" (belonging and mental health are needed regardless of the jobs debate) is not separately restated here since this document's own directly-fetched belonging-survey findings (above) already substantively instantiate it.

What the evidence does and doesn't support

Well-supported:

  • The national youth (15-24) unemployment rate fell to 12.7% in June 2026, down from a 14.6%-14.7% peak in September 2025 — directly fetched from Statistics Canada's own June 2026 Labour Force Survey release [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2].
  • Toronto's youth-specific unemployment gap versus the rest of Ontario widened sharply from late 2023 through September 2025, reaching roughly one-in-five youth unemployed in the Toronto labour market — directly fetched from TD Economics' own named, dated analysis using Statistics Canada microdata [NEW-2026-3].
  • TD Economics explicitly tested and did not find evidence that AI-exposure is a contributing factor to Toronto's youth-unemployment surge in the 2023-2025 period specifically, instead attributing it to older-worker displacement into youth-heavy sectors amid a soft economy [NEW-2026-3] — this is a real, methodologically specific counter-finding to the carried-forward briefing's core premise, not a dismissal of AI's relevance generally or over a longer horizon.
  • Ontario youth apprenticeship registrations increased 13.5% to 16,412 in 2024-2025, alongside an active $64.2 million provincial training-seat investment — directly sourced to Ontario government and industry-press reporting on the announcement [NEW-2026-5, NEW-2026-6].
  • Youth (18-34) report measurably higher rates of community disconnection (68%) than the Canadian average (60%), and Toronto is specifically named in a cited report as the "loneliest city in Canada" — directly fetched from the YMCA's own named, dated survey release [NEW-2026-7].

Thin or contested:

  • The Statistics Canada NEETEST figures [NEW-2026-4] are the most current, methodologically rigorous NEET-adjacent data located, but the underlying data run only through 2022 — three to four years stale relative to this document's other 2025-2026 figures, and no more current NEETEST update was located in this review.
  • No Toronto-specific (as opposed to Canada-wide-CMA-aggregate) NEET or NEETEST rate was located in this review — the CMA-vs-non-CMA comparison in [NEW-2026-4] is suggestive but not a Toronto-specific figure, and should not be read as one.
  • The "Toronto is the loneliest city in Canada" claim [NEW-2026-7] is cited secondhand, via the YMCA release's own citation of a Toronto Foundation report; this review did not independently fetch and confirm the underlying Toronto Foundation report itself — flagged ⚠️ still being checked.
  • Whether Ontario's apprenticeship-registration increase reflects genuine net new youth pathway capacity, as opposed to reallocation or a short-term administrative artifact of the Skilled Trades Ontario transition, is not established by any source located in this review [NEW-2026-5, NEW-2026-6].
  • The carried-forward briefing's own core empirical claims (Canadian youth unemployment "hit ~20% in 2025," "~12% of 15-29s are NEET," anxiety "roughly tripled" and depression "doubled" in a decade) [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing] were not independently re-verified against primary sources in this review; this review's own NEET figures [NEW-2026-4] suggest the carried-forward briefing's ~12% NEET figure is broadly consistent with the snapshot NEET rate's historical 12-14% range, but the carried-forward briefing's specific mental-health trend figures remain at their original carried-forward status, unconfirmed by this review.

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (specifically Article 32's protection from economic exploitation and Article 6's right to development, though these are not youth-labour-market-specific instruments) and the International Labour Organization's Global Employment Trends for Youth reporting are the closest institutional touchpoints; this review did not locate a specific, precisely-named binding international instrument addressing youth labour-market entry or NEET status directly, and one is not manufactured here to fill the sub-part. The UN Sustainable Development Goal 8.6 (by 2020, substantially reduce the proportion of youth not in employment, education or training) is a real, named, numbered SDG target directly on point, though this review did not fetch the specific current Canadian progress reporting against it.

2. 2-3 best global comparators. Germany's dual vocational-education-and-training (VET) system is the most-cited, best-evidenced global comparator for structured youth labour-market entry: OECD's own comparative analysis found the employment rate among 25-34-year-olds with intermediate (vocational) qualifications in Germany is 88%, above the OECD average of 82%, and academic literature comparing Germany and the United States after the 2008 financial crisis found "no observable impact occurred for young adults with dual VET" in Germany even as youth unemployment rose sharply in the US, attributed to the dual system's structured employer-linkage [NEW-2026-10]. Germany's youth unemployment rate is cited as Europe's lowest, at 5.7% [NEW-2026-10] — a genuinely strong, well-evidenced comparator, distinct from and complementary to Ontario's own OYAP/Skilled Trades Ontario apprenticeship pathway [NEW-2026-5, NEW-2026-6], which is structurally similar in concept (school-embedded trades registration) but smaller in scale and newer in its current administrative form. This review's own live discovery did not surface a materially stronger second or third comparator specific to youth belonging/third-place infrastructure beyond the YMCA's own Canada-wide programming already cited above.

3. What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. The concrete, transferable mechanism from the Germany comparison is the dual system's direct, structured employer co-ownership of the training relationship (apprentices are simultaneously employees of a specific firm and students at a vocational school, with the employer bearing real training cost and commitment) — a stronger employer-linkage model than Ontario's current OYAP structure, which is more school-embedded with employer placement layered on top rather than co-owned from the outset [NEW-2026-5, NEW-2026-6, NEW-2026-10]. This is a structural design difference, not a funding-level difference, and is nameable as a specific transferable feature rather than a vague "Toronto should be more like Germany" gesture.

NEW-2026-1 — StatCan Labour Force Survey, June 2026: national youth unemployment and Toronto CMA figures.

"The unemployment rate for youth aged 15 to 24 fell 0.7 percentage points to 12.7% in June, following a 0.9 percentage point decline in May. However, it remains higher than the pre-pandemic average of 10.8% observed during the period from 2017 to 2019." Also: "In the Toronto CMA, the unemployment rate was little changed at 6.9% in June, following a downward trend from a recent high of 8.5% in February."

"the September 2025 unemployment rate for youth (those aged 15 to 24 years) was 14.6%— the highest rate since September 2010 (excluding the COVID-19 pandemic years of 2020 and 2021)."

Source: Statistics Canada, "The NEETEST youth: Not in employment, education or training, excluding short transitions," by Marc Frenette and Tomasz Handler, https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2026003/article/00001-eng.htm, released 2026-03-25. NEW-2026-3 — TD Economics, "Hogtown Blues: Toronto's Lagging Jobs Market."

"The widening gap between Toronto's unemployment rate and the rest of Ontario's has been particularly pronounced among young workers. At present, a concerning one-in-five youths in Toronto's labour market are unemployed." Also: "the youth unemployment rate in Toronto has seen the largest increase over the time [since late 2023]." Also, on AI specifically: "Much has been made of the potential impact of technological changes on employment, including AI... We tested this for Toronto's jobs market, using a recent Statistics Canada study as a benchmark to classify industries by their 'exposure' and 'complementarity' to AI. We found that young workers in Toronto employed in 'high exposure' industries saw robust job growth since 2023. Accordingly, we didn't find much evidence that AI is a contributing factor for the recent surge in youth unemployment." Also: "older workers (facing a soft jobs market and rising living costs) are taking roles in industries that tend to hire a large share of younger workers."

Source: Tarek Attia and Rishi Sondhi, TD Economics, "Hogtown Blues: Toronto's Lagging Jobs Market," https://economics.td.com/ca-toronto-lagging-jobs-market, published 2025-10-29. Accessed via direct fetch 2026-07-14. TD's own analysis cites, as its AI-exposure classification benchmark: Statistics Canada, "Experimental Estimates of Potential Artificial Intelligence Occupational Exposure in Canada," https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11f0019m/11f0019m2024005-eng.htm (September 2024), and Brynjolfsson et al., "Canaries in the Coal Mine?
