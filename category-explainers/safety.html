Safety & Emergency Readiness

A plain-language explainer · 8th-grade reading level · every figure below is sourced and checkable.

What’s wrong here

When you call 911 in Toronto, one of three services answers: police, fire, or paramedics. Each is under strain, but not the same way.

Start with paramedics. In 2019, Toronto had 29 moments with zero staffed ambulances available anywhere in the city. By 2023, that had jumped to more than 1,200 — a 40-fold increase.1 The main cause is not a shortage of ambulances. The city has 234 of them, but on a typical day only 126 are staffed and running.2 The bigger problem is hospitals. In 2023, a paramedic crew spent two hours on average handling a call — and 71 of those minutes were spent waiting inside a hospital to hand off the patient.3 For the most urgent calls — cardiac arrest, the highest-danger cases — response times got 6% to 14% slower between 2019 and 2023, even though the service still hit its overall targets about 80% of the time.4

Police face a different kind of strain. In 2019, average response time for the two most urgent call types was 19 and 50 minutes — against a 6-minute target the police board itself set back in 1995.5 Toronto’s 2026 police budget is $1.43 billion, up $93.8 million, funding 143 net new officers.6 But a peer-reviewed study of 20 Canadian cities, including Toronto, found no consistent link between rising police spending and falling crime rates.7

Gun violence is its own story. In 2024, shootings in Toronto rose 33.6% and gun deaths rose 48.3% — the worst year in over a decade, confirmed by both police records and Statistics Canada.8 Young people aged 12 to 29 made up 58% of those involved in shootings, though they are only 23% of the population; nearly half of all shootings happened in areas holding only 22% of the city’s residents.9

What’s been proven to work elsewhere

Scotland took a public-health approach to violence: treat it like a disease to prevent, not just punish. Weapon-carrying fell 69% from 2005 to 2016, and hospital admissions from sharp-object assaults fell 63% from 2008 to 2015.10 That success took more than a decade of steady funding, not a one-year pilot.

Closer to home, Toronto’s own Community Crisis Service — a mental-health response team, not police — has resolved 78% of transferred 911 mental-health calls without police involvement since it launched in 2022.11 That is a working example already running here, not a foreign import.

What we could choose together

The City directly runs and funds police services (through the police board and council), fire, paramedics, bylaw enforcement, and the community safety plan called SafeTO. These are municipal decisions and municipal money.

Some things are provincial. Ontario sets the rules police must follow under a 2019 law, and funds a new watchdog — the Inspectorate of Policing — which took in 505 complaints in its first nine months and issued zero formal enforcement actions, too new to judge yet either way.12 The province also funds hospital-side fixes for the paramedic problem, including nurses dedicated to receiving ambulance patients faster, and cost-shares gun-violence-prevention grants with the city.

Some things are federal. The Criminal Code and gun laws are written in Ottawa. Two federal grants pay for most of Toronto’s youth violence-prevention work — $16.6 million since 2023 — and both were set to run out by the end of March 2026, before Ottawa said it intended to renew one, without yet locking in money beyond short-term extensions.13

None of these programs — the paramedic staffing plan, the police budget, the violence-prevention grants — has been checked by an outside evaluator yet. Most success numbers so far come from the same government running the program.

One question to ask any candidate: “Toronto had more than 1,200 moments last year with zero staffed ambulances available anywhere in the city — up from 29 five years earlier. What specific step will bring that number down, and where can I check it myself?”
