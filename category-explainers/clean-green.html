Clean, Green & Resilient

A plain-language explainer · 8th-grade reading level · every figure below is sourced and checkable.

What’s wrong here

This category covers a lot: clean streets, garbage, parks, snow clearing, old buildings, accessible sidewalks, flooding, and greener buildings. Three threads have the clearest numbers.

Start with garbage. Toronto’s only landfill, Green Lane, is expected to be full by 2034 or 2035.1 About 30% of Ontario’s waste already gets shipped across the border to the US.2 Toronto recycled or composted 50.6% of household waste in 2025, but that hides a big gap: houses hit 60.3%, while apartment towers managed only 26.3%. No source explains why.3

Snow is a similar story of good plans, unevenly kept. The city spends $143.8 million a year clearing 14,700 lane-km of roads and 7,900 km of sidewalks.4 In February 2025, a major storm overwhelmed sidewalks and local streets even though main roads stayed clear; residents filed more than 25,000 winter-related 311 complaints, and cleanup cost about $20 million.5 That storm hit after years of the city’s own Auditor General flagging contract oversight gaps — as of a 2025 check, 9 of 30 recommendations from earlier audits still were not fully in place.6

Flooding is the third thread, and the most expensive one. A single storm in July 2024 caused roughly $899 million to $940 million in insured damage and flooded more than 1,000 basements — Toronto’s third “hundred-year storm” in a decade.7 Ontario’s own Auditor General found only 28% of municipalities with stormwater costs in 2020 collected any dedicated revenue to pay for it.8 Toronto considered a dedicated stormwater fee for over a decade, then Council directed staff to indefinitely suspend it.9

What’s been proven to work elsewhere

Seoul weighs household food waste electronically and, since December 2025, pays residents credit for cutting what they throw out by 10% or more.10

Ottawa built a $232-million storage tunnel that has already cut sewage overflows and reduced flood risk for about 7,000 homes.11 Toronto’s own comparable tunnel is still under construction and not due to finish until 2034.

What we could choose together

Most of this category is squarely the City of Toronto’s own job: garbage collection, parks, snow clearing, and street cleanliness are all direct municipal responsibilities under the Municipal Act. The city can fund a stronger winter-storm response on its own: its own consultants priced a fix at $24.0 million a season, which “can primarily be accommodated within the City’s current winter maintenance budget.”12 It can also revive a scoped stormwater fee for commercial and industrial properties, a version it already studied once before shelving the whole idea.13

Some of this needs the Province of Ontario. Flood-plain mapping and river flood control belong to provincial conservation authorities, not the city.14 The province also sets the Building Code standard for backwater valves that help prevent basement flooding — its own survey of 52 chief building officials found only 27% required them in all new homes, even though a valve costs about $250 to install versus roughly $43,000 to fix a flooded basement afterward.15 Accessible public spaces are governed by provincial accessibility law the city implements locally, and building-retrofit standards trace back to the same provincial Building Code.

The federal government mostly shows up through infrastructure funding and national flood insurance discussions, and rules affecting where garbage can go once landfills run out.

This category also includes heritage building protection and disabled residents’ access to sidewalks — real issues where the sources reviewed did not turn up figures strong enough to cite honestly here. That is a gap in this draft, not a judgment that those issues matter less.

One question to ask any candidate: “Toronto’s landfill is projected to be full by 2034 or 2035, and only 26.3% of apartment-tower waste gets diverted from it, compared to 60.3% for houses. What will you do to close that gap, and how can I check the diversion rate for my own building?”
