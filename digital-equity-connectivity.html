Scope

This page’s neutral scope question, drawn from and sharpened against its carried-forward documents: who in Toronto cannot get meaningfully online — because of cost, lack of a suitable device, or lack of digital skills — and what, if anything, the City can do about it, given that the deepest lever (affordability, set by a concentrated national telecom market and federal/CRTC regulation) sits mostly outside municipal authority. This document covers: the current (2025-2026) status of ConnectTO's public Wi-Fi rollout and its own reporting on the scale of Toronto's digital divide; the real, confirmed status of the OW/ODSP broadband subsidy the master briefing described as "exploring" (a significant correction to that framing, detailed below); fresher national and UK-comparator affordability-program data; and 2025-2026 CRTC and Competition Bureau action bearing on the federal affordability lever this page’s own inherited framing already identifies as the deepest fix.

This document does not cover broadband-as-physical-infrastructure — the dig-once/conduit policy, fibre build-out, right-of-way management, and the "should Toronto re-establish a City-owned fibre network" capital-infrastructure question. That angle belongs to the sibling leaf water-wastewater-broadband-infrastructure (that page's own internal records), named here explicitly per this page’s assignment. Current state

ConnectTO's public Wi-Fi rollout, updated directly against the City's own July 2025 and October 2025 primary sources

The master briefing's headline currency figures — 11,000 residents across 22 apartment buildings, rolling out to 280 community centres and TCHC buildings, ~7,600 daily connections in 2024 — are one to two years stale against this review's direct fetch of the City's own July 2, 2025 Executive Committee report and an October 6, 2025 news release. The July 2025 ConnectTO Program Update states plainly: "ConnectTO has successfully focused on expanding internet connectivity, growing the number of locations of public Wi-Fi from 100 sites exclusively in Toronto Public Library branches to a total of 252 locations across the City in various types of City facilities... By the end of 2025, the City will have approximately 300 public Wi-Fi locations" [NEW-2026-1]. As of the report's own snapshot date (May 2025), the breakdown was 152 non-library City-owned-and-operated locations (126 community recreation centres, 14 TCHC/Toronto Seniors Housing buildings, 4 outdoor squares, 4 civic centres, 1 community hub, 3 other City locations, plus 7 shelters) plus the pre-existing 100 TPL library branches [NEW-2026-1]. The subsequent October 6, 2025 news release confirms the milestone was reached and exceeded: "free, City-provided public Wi-Fi available in more than 250 locations across Toronto," with "89 per cent of these sites... located in Toronto's Neighbourhood Improvement Areas... and Emerging Neighbourhoods" [NEW-2026-2]. The July 2025 report's own daily-connection and usage figures (7,600 daily connections in 2024, 69% repeat connections, 4-hour average session length) are the same figures the master briefing already cites — this review found no more current (2025-2026) daily-connection count superseding the 2024 figure, which is stated as a gap rather than assumed unchanged.

The July 2025 report also provides a load-bearing scale figure not present in the master briefing at all: "Approximately 2% of Torontonians, or 58,000 people, currently lack affordable in-home internet access," attributed to Toronto Metropolitan University research (Angus Lockhart and Sam Andrey, "Toronto's Digital Divide," 2024) commissioned by the City itself [NEW-2026-1]. This is a materially different framing from the master briefing's own headline claim that "~40% of Toronto's lowest-income households lack home internet" — the two figures are not directly comparable (one is a citywide headcount of the disconnected across all income levels; the other is an income-quartile-specific access rate) but their coexistence in different City-facing documents is flagged here as a genuine reconciliation gap this review did not resolve, detailed further in "What the evidence does and doesn't support" below.

The OW/ODSP broadband subsidy: not launched — the City's own 2025-2026 record shows a pivot away from a City-administered subsidy toward promoting existing federal and private programs

This is the central live-verification finding of this review, and it corrects the master briefing's framing rather than merely updating it. The master briefing states Toronto "is exploring a household broadband subsidy for OW/ODSP recipients." This review's direct fetch of the City's own July 2, 2025 ConnectTO Program Update — the most current primary-source ConnectTO status document located — describes no such City-administered subsidy program, launched or in development. Instead, under the heading "Linking eligible families with digital subsidies through the Human Services Integration (HSI) office," the report states: "Technology Services and the Human Services Integration (HSI) Office in Social Development are partnered to promote enrollment in digital equity subsidy programs and supports available to all eligible Torontonians," listing as the HSI office's concrete 2025 activities: "Updated the Human Services website with new links to the Connecting Families Initiative and Connected for Success program" and "Developing a strategy for proactive services recommendations to ensure that eligible families who contact the City for other services are informed about the available subsidies" [NEW-2026-1]. The report's own "Key Themes and Actions" table, summarizing 2024 public consultation findings on affordability, likewise commits only to "sharing information on federal government subsidies for in-home internet through the Human Services Integration office" [NEW-2026-1] — not to creating or funding a City-administered subsidy.

The subsidy programs the City is now directing OW/ODSP recipients toward are pre-existing federal and private-sector programs, not new City initiatives: the federal Connecting Families Initiative (2.0, launched April 1, 2022, offering a minimum 200 GB/month at 50 Mbps download/10 Mbps upload for $20/month to households receiving the maximum Canada Child Benefit or Guaranteed Income Supplement) [NEW-2026-3], and Rogers' Connected for Success program, a private-carrier program explicitly open to ODSP, OW, and GIS recipients as well as subsidized-housing tenants and seniors, independently confirmed via a disability-advocacy organization's own public-policy page describing its partnership with Rogers on the program [NEW-2026-4]. Toronto Community Housing operates its own "Connected for Success" landing page for current tenants, confirming this is the channel TCHC uses for its own residents' internet-affordability referrals [search-confirmed, not independently fetched in full this review]. This document therefore corrects the master briefing's "exploring a subsidy" framing: as of this review's most current available source (July 2025, the most recent ConnectTO status report located), the City's own record shows no City-administered OW/ODSP broadband subsidy in development — the City's actual 2025-2026 posture is referral/promotion of existing federal and private-carrier programs, not subsidy creation. This is stated plainly as a correction, not a silent update, per this project's citation and correction-propagation discipline.

Fresher (2025) Canadian digital-divide statistics

This review located a more current income-quartile access figure than the master briefing's own cited 97.7%/58% split (StatsCan, vintage unstated in the master briefing but referencing pre-2025 data per its own Wikipedia-sourced citation). A 2025-referenced figure surfaced via this review's search states "95 per cent of Canadians in the highest income quartile are connected, just 62 per cent in the lowest income quartile have internet access" [NEW-2026-5] — narrower than the master briefing's 97.7%/58% split, suggesting some narrowing of the gap, but this figure was WebSearch-discovered via a secondary aggregator (OpenMedia) rather than independently fetched from a StatsCan primary table in this review, and its exact reference year was not confirmed — flagged ⚠️ still being checked below rather than presented as a confirmed update. Separately, StatsCan's own quality-of-access data (also not independently re-fetched from the primary StatsCan table in this review, cited via the same secondary source) finds that among Canadian households with children under 18, "24% of those in the lowest income quartile depend on mobile devices to access the Internet compared with 8% of those in the highest income quartile" [NEW-2026-5] — a device/quality-of-access finding the master briefing's own four-legs framing (access/affordability/devices/skills) anticipates but does not itself cite a specific figure for, making this a genuine strengthening addition to the "devices" leg specifically.

The master briefing's own foundational framework for this whole leaf — restated here because the claim-index appendix below already asserts it is "cited throughout as-is" but a direct check found it was not actually developed anywhere in this document's body text, a gap this restoration closes — treats the digital divide as four linked legs: access (is broadband available?), affordability (can people pay? — the binding leg in a city), devices (do they have a computer, not just a phone?), and skills/digital literacy (low-income people and seniors lag by approximately 30 percentage points in digital participation, per the master briefing's own figure, blocking access to jobs, education, and e-government). The master briefing also names Canada's affordability problem as structural: a concentrated telecom market produces some of the world's highest prices and lowest data caps, with caps "as low as 20 GB vs. 150 GB minimums elsewhere" — a comparator this document carries forward as-is, per this page’s inherited-citation rule, since this review's own live-discovery budget did not include an independent re-check of international data-cap comparators. Separately, the master briefing's own "Sources to verify" list names First Nations connectivity (~60% without reliable access) as a figure needing confirmation; this document restores that figure here, verbatim-hedged, rather than silently dropping it — it is carried as an unconfirmed, single-mention statistic from the master briefing's own text (the master briefing does not itself cite a source for this specific number), so it is treated as non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people)-tier at best (a bare demographic access-rate statistic, not a claim about what any First Nation or Indigenous person or organization needs, wants, or believes) and is flagged ⚠️ still being checked here rather than presented as confirmed.

Federal and CRTC action, 2025-2026: real movement on competition and consumer protection, not on OW/ODSP-specific affordability

The master briefing names CRTC regulation and Connecting Families as the deepest federal levers and frames both as "welcome but insufficient." This review's live discovery confirms substantial CRTC activity in the 2025-2026 window, though none of it is targeted at low-income or social-assistance-specific affordability the way the master briefing's own OW/ODSP framing implies is needed. In February 2025, the CRTC "began allowing competitors across Canada to use the largest telephone companies' fibre networks to sell a wide range of communications services, including home Internet, television, telephone, and smart home services," with competitive providers responding by announcing "plans to deliver new competitive choices for up to 8.5 million Canadian households" [NEW-2026-6]. On April 24, 2026, the CRTC finalized the wholesale access rates competitors pay for that fibre access, stating this "finalizes the CRTC's approach to increasing competition, which is already driving more choice of affordable Internet services for Canadians," per CRTC Chairperson and CEO Vicky Eatrides [NEW-2026-6, direct quote below]. The same April 2026 announcement references two other 2026 consumer-protection actions: the CRTC "eliminated extra fees on Internet and cellphone plans" (March 2026) and "improved how Canadians are notified about expiring contracts and discounts" (April 2026) [NEW-2026-6]. This review did not locate any 2025-2026 CRTC or federal action specifically expanding or reforming the Connecting Families program itself (e.g., raising its data cap, widening eligibility beyond CCB/GIS recipients to include OW/ODSP directly, or increasing its $20/month price point) — the program's eligibility and terms as described in this review's sources remain the same as the master briefing's own description, flagged as a gap rather than assumed unchanged.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

The OW/ODSP subsidy correction sharpens, rather than merely updates, the master briefing's own "the City has real levers" case. The master briefing's strongest case FOR treating connectivity as a basic service rests partly on the claim that Toronto "is exploring a broadband subsidy for OW/ODSP recipients" as evidence of an active municipal affordability agenda. This review's finding — that the City's actual 2025 record shows referral to existing federal/private programs, not subsidy creation — means the City's own affordability lever is narrower in practice than the master briefing's framing suggests. This is not presented as resolving whether referral-only is an adequate response (that is an AGAINST-brief question, out of scope for this document's own voice), only that the evidentiary basis for "the City is building a subsidy" specifically is not supported by the most current available primary source.

Two different City-cited scale figures for Toronto's digital divide — 58,000 people (~2% of Torontonians) versus a ~40%-of-lowest-income-households framing — sit in tension and are not reconciled in this review. Both trace to the same underlying TMU-commissioned research relationship (the Dais/TMU studies cited in both the master briefing and the July 2025 ConnectTO report), but the two figures answer different questions (an absolute citywide headcount of the disconnected, versus an income-quartile-specific access rate) and this document does not attempt to force them into a single number. This is named as a disclosed cross-source discrepancy per this project's citation discipline, not silently resolved by picking one.

Federal/CRTC 2025-2026 action is real and substantial on competition and consumer-protection mechanics, but not targeted at the specific low-income/social-assistance affordability gap this page’s inherited framing centres. The CRTC's February 2025 wholesale-fibre-access opening and April 2026 final-rate decision are framed by the CRTC itself as pro-competition, pro-affordability measures for the general market [NEW-2026-6] — but they are structurally different from, and not a substitute for, an income-targeted subsidy mechanism like Connecting Families, which this review found no evidence of being expanded or reformed in the same window. The City's own "advocate to Ottawa/CRTC" posture (per the master briefing) has, per this review's findings, a live regulatory process to advocate into — but this document does not assert that City advocacy caused, or is credited with, any of the 2025-2026 CRTC actions found.

Municipal broadband is hard, and Toronto's own experience is the master briefing's central cautionary case. The master briefing's own honest-complexities case is explicit that the boldest municipal lever — a City-owned broadband network — carries high capital cost, operational complexity, competition with incumbents, and political risk, and names Toronto's own cancelled ConnectTO network plan (2022) as the local example of that risk materializing, while noting municipal broadband has succeeded in some US cities and rural co-ops but failed or struggled in others. This document restores that argument here rather than letting the correction above (the OW/ODSP subsidy finding) stand in as the whole of this page’s "municipal broadband is hard" case, since the two are related but distinct points in the master briefing's own structure.

Public Wi-Fi is a bridge, not a substitute for home connectivity — the master briefing's own limiting principle on its headline Wi-Fi-rollout finding. The master briefing is explicit that free public Wi-Fi, while valuable, is "not private or reliable enough" for many essential tasks (telehealth, job interviews, banking, children's homework), and that people should not have to sit in a community centre to access government — so it frames public Wi-Fi as a bridge toward affordable home connectivity, not the end goal. This document's own "Well-supported" finding above (that ConnectTO's Wi-Fi rollout has grown substantially since the master briefing's writing) should be read against this limiting principle, which this document restores here rather than letting the rollout's scale stand uncontextualized.

Two further honest tensions the master briefing names, restored here. First, privacy and surveillance: the master briefing flags that free public Wi-Fi and City connectivity infrastructure raise data-collection and surveillance questions of their own — connectivity must be trustworthy, not a tracking vector, a concern this document notes is the direct province of the sibling data-governance briefing rather than developed further here. Second, digital-by-default exclusion: as governments move services online to cut costs, they risk stranding people who remain offline — so the master briefing insists digital services must always retain non-digital (phone, in-person) options so efficiency gains do not come at the cost of excluding the very people this page is about. Third, the master briefing cautions against over-techno-solutionizing: connectivity is necessary but not sufficient for inclusion — it sits alongside income, literacy, and language as compounding factors, so closing the digital divide does not by itself resolve the underlying poverty, literacy, or newcomer-settlement gaps that often accompany it.

The digital divide's own distributional pattern, restored from the master briefing's equity framing. The master briefing is explicit that the disconnected and under-skilled are disproportionately low-income households, seniors (who show the largest digital-skills gap), precariously-housed and public-housing residents, newcomers (facing both language and cost barriers), people without high-school completion, and disabled people (who may need both connectivity and accessible technology). It also names an intergenerational "homework gap": children in disconnected homes fall behind in school, entrenching disadvantage across generations. This document restores that distributional argument here because it is load-bearing for the page’s equity framing and was not otherwise carried in this document's own body text.

What the evidence does and doesn't support

Well-supported:

  • ConnectTO's public Wi-Fi rollout reached 252 City-owned-and-operated locations (plus 100 pre-existing library sites) as of May 2025, and exceeded 250 total non-library-plus-library combined sites by October 2025, per two independently fetched, dated City primary sources (a July 2, 2025 Executive Committee report and an October 6, 2025 news release) [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2].
  • The City's own 2025 record shows no City-administered OW/ODSP broadband subsidy in development; its actual affordability-related activity in this period is promotion of pre-existing federal (Connecting Families) and private-carrier (Rogers Connected for Success) programs through its Human Services Integration office [NEW-2026-1], independently corroborated by a disability-advocacy organization's own description of the Rogers partnership and eligibility criteria including ODSP/OW/GIS recipients [NEW-2026-4].
  • The CRTC undertook substantial, dated regulatory action on internet-market competition and consumer protection in the 2025-2026 window: opening competitor access to incumbent fibre networks (February 2025), finalizing wholesale access rates (April 24, 2026, with a direct quote from the CRTC Chairperson), eliminating switching fees (March 2026), and improving contract-expiry notifications (April 2026) [NEW-2026-6].
  • Canada's independent (non-incumbent) wholesale internet-provider market share fell from 8.4% in 2020 to 4.2% in 2024 — a halving in four years — a concentration trend directly relevant to this page’s inherited "concentrated telecom market" affordability framing [NEW-2026-7].

Thin or contested:

  • A narrower 2025-referenced income-quartile access gap (95%/62%, versus the master briefing's own 97.7%/58%) was located via a secondary source (OpenMedia) rather than independently fetched from a StatsCan primary table in this review; the exact reference year is unconfirmed. ⚠️ still being checked — a verification check should fetch the StatsCan table directly (www150.statcan.gc.ca, table 22-10-0007-01 or successor) before this figure is treated as superseding the master briefing's own figure.
  • The master briefing's "~40% of Toronto's lowest-income households lack home internet" figure and the City's own July 2025 report's "58,000 people, ~2% of Torontonians" figure were not reconciled in this review and may answer different questions (quartile-specific rate versus citywide absolute count) — both are cited here as-is rather than one being silently preferred.
  • No 2025-2026 daily-connection or usage figure superseding the master briefing's own 2024 figure (7,600 daily connections, 69% repeat) was located; the July 2025 report itself still cites the 2024 figure as current, suggesting no more recent measurement had been published as of that report's writing, but this review did not confirm whether a newer figure exists in a source not located.
  • Whether any 2025-2026 CRTC or federal action specifically reformed Connecting Families' eligibility, data cap, or price point (as opposed to general-market competition measures) was not confirmed in this review — genuinely unknown, not merely unsearched, given a direct search targeting this question in this review.

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. The UN Human Rights Council has adopted a recurring (bi-annual, since 2012) resolution on "the promotion, protection and enjoyment of human rights on the Internet," reaffirming that "the same rights that people have offline must also be protected online," with a 2021 iteration (adopted July 13, 2021, led by Brazil, Nigeria, Sweden, Tunisia and the United States, co-sponsored by 70 countries, passed 43-0 with 4 abstentions) that specifically "focuses on bridging digital divides and consolidates commitments on enhancing Internet accessibility and affordability" [NEW-2026-8]. This is a real, named, non-binding international framework directly on point for this page’s own subject matter — access and affordability specifically, not merely internet freedom of expression generally — and is cited here rather than the more commonly invoked but less precisely on-point ICESCR general provisions used elsewhere in this corpus for adjacent issues.

2. 2-3 best global comparators, plus two comparators restored from the master briefing's own precedents list. The master briefing itself names two real-world precedents this document had not otherwise carried in its body text: the federal Digital Literacy Exchange Program, a skills/literacy funding stream administered by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada, cited by the master briefing as part of the "helpful, insufficient" federal affordability-and-skills base alongside Connecting Families; and municipal broadband successes and failures in US cities and rural co-ops, which the master briefing cites as the evidence base for its own "municipal broadband can work, but must be approached with realism about cost, business model, and risk" argument — both restored here verbatim-hedged, per the master briefing's own framing, rather than re-derived or independently re-verified in this review. (a) San Jose, California's SJ Access program — a city-council-approved (April 2023), library-foundation-administered digital-inclusion initiative with a stated goal of connecting 50,000 households to universal device access and connectivity at 100 Mbps/20 Mbps. As of this review's search, the city's own reporting states approximately 41,000 households remain unconnected or under-connected and 25,500 are "digitally disadvantaged" (no device or smartphone-only), against program results of roughly 3,200 devices checked out through library rental (600 Chromebooks, 120 iPads added in one reporting period) and 12,800 free Wi-Fi hotspots distributed to K-12 students plus 3,000 to residents since program launch [NEW-2026-9]. This is cited with its own stated remaining-gap numbers rather than as an unqualified success — the program's own reporting shows the scale of need still outstripping delivered devices/hotspots by a wide margin, consistent with this template's instruction not to import an unearned success narrative. (b) The UK's Ofcom-regulated broadband and mobile "social tariffs" — discounted plans major UK providers (BT, Sky, Virgin Media O2, Vodafone, and smaller providers) are required or encouraged to offer benefits recipients, structurally similar to Canada's Connecting Families in being a national/regulatory-level rather than municipal affordability mechanism. Ofcom's own most recent uptake data found 532,000 UK customers on social tariffs as of June 2025 (up from 506,000 the prior year) — but this represents only about 8.6% of eligible households, with roughly 70% of eligible households reportedly unaware such tariffs exist, and Ofcom's own research separately found about 26% of UK households (roughly 6 million) struggled to afford their communications services as of May 2025 [search-confirmed, industry-press-sourced, not independently fetched from Ofcom's own primary release in this review — ⚠️ still being checked]. This comparator is cited specifically for its honest failure mode — a real, available, regulator-backed affordability mechanism with structurally low uptake driven by an awareness gap — rather than as a model to emulate uncritically. (c) The US federal Affordable Connectivity Program (ACP), referenced in this review's search results only as context (not independently verified in detail) for a contrasting failure mode: a federal subsidy that existed and then ended, leaving "nearly 51,000 households" in Boston alone without their prior monthly broadband subsidy per one municipal digital-equity assessment [NEW-2026-9, secondary reference only] — cited here as a cautionary comparator on subsidy-program durability, not as a program Toronto could import (it no longer exists).

3. What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. Two specific, nameable mechanisms are worth naming plainly. First, San Jose's model of routing device-lending and hotspot distribution through the public library system under a single named program brand (SJ Access) with published, specific household-level targets and gap-tracking (41,000 unconnected/under-connected, 25,500 device-disadvantaged, tracked as distinct categories) is a measurement discipline Toronto's own ConnectTO reporting does not currently appear to replicate at the same level of device/skills-specific granularity — ConnectTO's own July 2025 report is comprehensive on Wi-Fi site counts and usage but does not report a comparable device-gap or skills-gap headcount alongside its access figures. Second, Ofcom's social-tariff awareness failure is instructive precisely because Toronto's own ConnectTO report explicitly commits, as one of its stated 2025 actions, to "sharing information on federal government subsidies for in-home internet through the Human Services Integration office" [NEW-2026-1] — the UK's own regulator-documented ~70% awareness gap on a structurally similar mechanism suggests that a referral-only strategy, without a dedicated, measured awareness campaign, carries a known and documented risk of low uptake that Toronto's current approach does not yet appear to have built in a matching measurement mechanism for. Both observations are stated descriptively, per this document's own neutrality firewall; whether the City should adopt either a device-gap-tracking metric or a dedicated awareness-campaign measurement is a recommendation-shaped judgment reserved for the card layer.

Cui Bono — who profits from this problem persisting

This page’s own inherited framing already names the mechanism plainly: "a concentrated, high-priced telecom market" as the primary driver of the affordability gap that is the single biggest reason Torontonians lack home internet. This review's live discovery found a credible, ESTABLISHED-grade government source substantiating that structural claim at the entity-category (not named-individual-company) level, though it stops short of a single-company finding with a registered entity/claim pair this project's Accountability Observatory pipeline has not yet produced for this sector.

The Competition Bureau of Canada's own Market Study Notice on broadband competition states directly: "Most Canadian homes are served by two networks capable of providing broadband internet services: one owned by the local telephone company, and the other owned by the local cable company. This limited choice leads to obvious questions about competition; when consumers have only two options, can we be sure that market forces will deliver the low prices and high levels of innovation that are characteristic of competitive markets?" [NEW-2026-10]. The Bureau's study further documents that despite over 550 registered independent resellers, "as of 2016, 87% of retail internet subscriptions in Canada were purchased from a traditional telephone or cable company," even though resellers offered "seemingly comparable services at prices that can be as much as 30% lower" [NEW-2026-10, citing the CRTC's own 2017 Communications Monitoring Report and a 2015 Wall Communications price study]. This review's earlier search independently found a more current version of the same dynamic: independent wholesale-based internet providers' market share fell from 8.4% in 2020 to 4.2% in 2024 [NEW-2026-7] — coinciding with, though this document does not assert causation from, the CRTC's own multi-year process of setting and then finalizing (April 2026) the wholesale rates independent providers pay incumbents for network access [NEW-2026-6].

Disposition: this document treats the two-network incumbent duopoly (traditional telephone and cable companies, unnamed as specific corporate entities in the Bureau's own study language) as an ESTABLISHED-grade structural finding directly on point for this page’s own "concentrated telecom market" claim, per a government regulator's own published market study — not a REPORTED/contested allegation. It does not name specific incumbent telecom companies as individually profiting beneficiaries in a formal a registered entity/a registered accountability claim table row, because (a) this project's Accountability Observatory entity register has not yet produced a graded entity/claim pair for this sector as of this review (checked: no this library's internal records register/the accountability register's entities table or the claims register row was available to pull by ID within this review's scope), and (b) the Competition Bureau's own study, while clearly identifying the structural duopoly and its price/market-share effects, stops short of naming which specific companies benefit from which specific practice in the passages this review reviewed — the pointer-never-author discipline means this document does not extend the Bureau's own general "two-network" framing into a specific-company claim the Bureau itself has not made in the material reviewed here. NEW-2026-10 — Competition Bureau two-network duopoly framing (Cui Bono finding).

"Most Canadian homes are served by two networks capable of providing broadband internet services: one owned by the local telephone company, and the other owned by the local cable company. This limited choice leads to obvious questions about competition; when consumers have only two options, can we be sure that market forces will deliver the low prices and high levels of innovation that are characteristic of competitive markets? ... The latest CRTC Communications Monitoring Report notes that, as of 2016, 87% of retail internet subscriptions in Canada were purchased from a traditional telephone or cable company. This comes against the backdrop of resellers offering seemingly comparable services at prices that can be as much as 30% lower than those advertised by telephone and cable companies, as measured in a 2015 report by Wall Communications."

Source: Competition Bureau Canada, "Market Study Notice: Competition in Broadband Services," https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/how-we-foster-competition/promotion-and-advocacy/regulatory-adviceinterventions-competition-bureau/market-study-notice-competition-broadband-services, dated May 2018 (page last modified 2022-01-19; a companion "Broadband Market Study Update" page confirms the study was archived without this review locating a final published report — the study's ultimate disposition/final report was not confirmed in this review, flagged as a gap). Accessed via direct fetch 2026-07-14. The structural finding (two-network duopoly, 87% incumbent share, reseller price differential) is cited as the Bureau's own analysis; this document does not assert the Bureau's Study was ever completed with a final public report, since this review's search did not locate one — the Notice's own framing is cited as-is, on its own terms, as an ESTABLISHED government-regulator characterization of the market structure regardless of whether a later final report exists.
