REGENERATIVE TORONTO — JOURNALIST QUICK-START GUIDE

Toronto Homelessness & Housing Accountability | Primary-Sourced Briefing

The Campaign for a Regenerative Ontario | Regenerative Toronto | By The Unknown Soldier

Document ID: MAIN-DERIV-002

Who this is for: Journalists, editors, producers, podcast hosts, newsletter writers What this is: Every major claim is primary-sourced and independently verifiable Caveat: We are an advocacy organization. Check our sources. We want you to.

THE FIVE STORIES

STORY 1: THE AG RECOMMENDATION NOBODY IMPLEMENTS (5-YEAR FOLLOW)

The story: In December 2021, Ontario's Auditor General issued a landmark report with Recommendation 1: develop a provincial homelessness strategy with specific actions, targets, and timelines. In 2023, the AG's own follow-up found 40% of recommendations showing "little or no progress" — the strategy recommendation among them. Five years on, no provincial strategy exists.

What makes this newsworthy now: Ontario Big City Mayors declared a state of emergency on homelessness in December 2025 (SolvetheCrisis.ca). The Province responded by announcing supervised consumption site closures and passing the Safer Municipalities Act — neither of which constitutes a strategy.

The documents: - AG 2021 Homelessness Audit: https://www.auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en21/AR_Homelessness_en21.pdf - AG 2023 Follow-up (40% no progress): https://www.auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en23/1-07FU_homelessness_en23.pdf

The quote to use: Rec 1 exact language: "develop a provincial strategy with specific actions, targets, and timelines."

The ask for readers/voters: "Will you implement AG Recommendation 1? Yes or no?"

STORY 2: THE COHB SUSPENSION AND THE HOUSING EXIT COLLAPSE

The story: During an intergovernmental dispute, the Ford government suspended Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit payments for 7 months (April–October 2024). This caused the sharpest single-year drop in shelter-to-housing exits in Toronto's recorded history.

The numbers: - Shelter-to-housing exits 2023: 5,927 ✅ - Shelter-to-housing exits 2024: 4,344 ✅ - Drop: 1,583 people who would have been housed remained in shelter - Cost at $136/night × 365: approximately tens of millions in avoidable shelter costs for that cohort alone - Source: TSSS 2026 Budget Notes, backgroundfile-261532.pdf ✅

What makes this newsworthy: The COHB suspension is the clearest single example of a provincial government decision causing measurable harm to homeless Torontonians. The numbers come from the City's own budget documents.

The documents: - TSSS 2026 Budget Notes: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/bu/bgrd/backgroundfile-261532.pdf - Toronto AG 2025 Shelter Audit (confirms $136/night): https://www.torontoauditor.ca/report/audit-of-toronto-shelter-and-support-services-warming-centres-and-winter-respite-sites-understanding-and-addressing-demand-while-improving-financial-accountability-to-stretch-dollars-furthe/

STORY 3: THE SHELTER SYSTEM'S OWN VERDICT ON ITSELF

The story: In 2024, Toronto Shelter and Support Services conducted a major stakeholder consultation for its 2025-2030 Strategic Plan. The results of that consultation — from the City's own report — are damning: only 6% of 176 surveyed stakeholders — residents' associations (27%), health and homelessness organizations (20%), homelessness providers (17%), and people with lived experience — said services have improved in the past five years. 66% said worse. (What We Heard, App. A, p.58)

The numbers: - 6% of 176 stakeholders (not solely shelter staff — includes residents' associations, health orgs, lived experience) said services improved ✅ - 66% said they have gotten worse ✅ - 47% identified reducing chronic homelessness as the top system priority ✅ - Source: What We Heard: 2025-2030 Strategic Plan Stakeholder Engagement Report, April 2025

What makes this remarkable: This is a cross-sector consultation — not a poll of shelter staff alone. The 176 respondents include residents' associations (27%), health and homelessness organizations (20%), homelessness providers (17%), housing advocates, and people with lived experience — a cross-sector survey, not a poll of shelter workers alone. That breadth makes the finding more credible, not less.

The kicker: The plan this consultation was supposed to produce was promised to Council in 2025. It slipped to 2026. It's now been pushed to 2027 — while the system these stakeholders assessed as "getting worse" continues to operate without a plan.

The document: - What We Heard full report: https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/979d-Strategic-Plan-Stakeholder-Engagement-Report-FULL.pdf - TSSS 2026 Budget (confirms 2027 delay): https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/8eba-2026-Public-Book-TSSS-V1.pdf

STORY 4: THE IHAP CLIFF — $261M TO $97M IN ONE YEAR

The story: For three years, the federal Interim Housing Assistance Program funded Toronto's refugee claimant shelter response. In 2024, that funding was $261.87M — fully 33% of Toronto's entire shelter operating budget. In 2026, it dropped to $97M. The City's shelter system is absorbing a $165M year-over-year funding cut while serving the same population.

The numbers (all from primary sources): - TSSS 2024 total operating funds: $796.40M ✅ [TSSS 2024 Annual Report: https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/903c-2024-TSSS-Annual-ReportAODA.pdf] - IHAP share in 2024: $261.87M (33%) ✅ - IHAP in 2026: $97M ✅ [TSSS 2026 Budget Notes: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/bu/bgrd/backgroundfile-261532.pdf] - Drop: $164.87M reduction in one year from one source - Average nightly shelter users 2024: 9,700 people ✅ - Unique individuals served 2024: 24,200 (including 12,600+ refugee claimants) ✅

Why this matters: Toronto's shelter system was structurally dependent on emergency federal refugee funding that was never designed to be permanent. The cliff is hitting right as the system faces record chronic homelessness, zero HSCIS sites open, and a delayed strategic plan.

The documents: - TSSS 2024 Annual Report: https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/903c-2024-TSSS-Annual-ReportAODA.pdf - TSSS 2026 Budget Notes: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/bu/bgrd/backgroundfile-261532.pdf

STORY 5: THE HOUSING EXIT MATH — WHY $900M BUYS SO LITTLE

The story: Toronto's shelter system costs $897.957M gross per year. Each person who moves into Housing First costs approximately $6,311/year net after housing subsidies offset costs. The system saves $43,329 per person per year by housing someone instead of sheltering them. But only 4,344 people were housed in 2024 — down from 5,927 in 2023. The math shows a system spending nearly $900M annually that is getting worse at its core mission.

The numbers: - Gross TSSS budget 2025: $897.957M ✅ [Budget Committee January 2025: https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/budget-finances/city-budget/] - Shelter cost per person per year: $49,640 ($136/night) ✅ [Toronto AG 2025: https://www.torontoauditor.ca/report/audit-of-toronto-shelter-and-support-services-warming-centres-and-winter-respite-sites-understanding-and-addressing-demand-while-improving-financial-accountability-to-stretch-dollars-furthe/] - Housing First net cost per year: $6,311 ✅ [Latimer et al. 2020: https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ps.202000029] - Savings per person housed: $43,329/year ✅ - Housing exits 2024: 4,344 (down 26.7% from 2023) ✅ - A shelter bed in 2024 served half as many people as historically — people are staying longer ✅ [TSSS 2024 Annual Report: https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/903c-2024-TSSS-Annual-ReportAODA.pdf]

The comparison that lands: For what Toronto spends keeping one person in shelter for a year ($49,640), they could fund 7.9 years of Housing First ($6,311). The system is structured to spend money at the wrong end of the problem.

The academic backing: - Hwang et al. 2013: homeless people use the ED 8.48× more than low-income housed controls (DOI:10.2105/AJPH.2013.301369) ✅ - Hwang et al. 2009: mortality 3.5× higher than lowest income quintile (DOI:10.1136/bmj.b4036) ✅

STORY 6: THE $897.957M FIGURE ISN'T THE WHOLE STORY (Added July 2026)

The story: Every figure in Story 5 above is accurate — and it's also incomplete. The TTC and Toronto Public Library have quietly built substantial homelessness-response infrastructure of their own over the past several years, funded entirely outside TSSS's budget, because people experiencing homelessness have nowhere else to go. This is the natural follow-up angle to Story 5, and it's the question any careful reader of this campaign's own materials would eventually ask: "is $897.957M really the total?" It isn't — and this campaign found that out and said so before being asked.

The numbers: - TTC's own 2026 budget itemizes $26.2M for "safety, security and wellbeing" in a single year ✅ [TTC 2026 Budget Presentation: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/bu/bgrd/backgroundfile-261647.pdf] - TTC's own spokesperson has stated the agency has increased spending by "tens of millions of dollars each year, strictly focused on health and safety response" over the last four to five years — an on-record admission, not this campaign's estimate ✅ [The Local: https://thelocal.to/ttc-mental-health-issues-homelessness-public-space/] - 51% of subway delays in the month examined were attributed to "external factors," including disorderly patrons and unauthorized persons at track level ✅ [The Local: https://thelocal.to/ttc-mental-health-issues-homelessness-public-space/] - Toronto Public Library's Social and Crisis Support Services program has assisted 8,000+ people since 2023, graduating from a philanthropic pilot to $1.13M/year in permanent City funding starting 2026 ✅ [TPL 2025 Budget Notes: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/bu/bgrd/backgroundfile-252532.pdf ; CBC News, Sept 16 2025: https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/toronto-library-crisis-services-1.7634871] - Parks, Forestry and Recreation confirms $3.698M in federal encampment funding and a dedicated 5-position cleaning crew, also sitting outside TSSS's budget ✅ [PFR 2025 Budget Notes: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/bu/bgrd/backgroundfile-252540.pdf]

Why this is a good story, not a bad one, for this campaign: most political messaging tries to minimize or bury inconvenient complexity. This campaign's own research surfaced this gap and is naming it directly — a documented example of the transparency standard this platform's own recommendations (REC M-07, PS-01) ask the City to apply to itself. A journalist testing this campaign's credibility should find that it holds up to its own standard.

The quotable line: "Toronto doesn't have one homelessness budget — it has at least three, and only one of them gets talked about publicly."

RED-TEAM NOTE: ON OPIOID DEATHS

The government's counter-claim: Opioid deaths fell province-wide to 8.5/100,000 in 2025 — half the 2021 peak. This is true. Do not imply deaths rose after SCS closures.

The defensible ground: Non-fatal overdose calls rose ~119%. Public-health experts flagged this as the early-warning signal. No independent evaluation of HART hub outcomes has been published. The mortality trend and the call data can both be true simultaneously — and both should be reported accurately.
