REGENERATIVE TORONTO

Wave 5: The Broken Promises File

Six Ford Government Commitments vs. Documented Outcomes

Source: Ontario Auditor General | Financial Accountability Office | Integrity Commissioner | RCMP

The Campaign for a Regenerative Ontario | Regenerative Toronto | By The Unknown Soldier

Document ID: MAIN-DERIV-007 Date: June 29, 2026 Author: The Unknown Soldier Status: All documented outcomes traceable to independent watchdog bodies or primary government data. No opinion. No characterization. Only the record. Source-check status: PASS on all six items. No ⚠️ items in main claims.

THE RECORD

PROMISE #1: "MORE HOMES BUILT FASTER"

When: November 2022 | Source: Ontario News Release, "More Homes Built Faster Act, 2022"

The commitment: Bill 23 would create 1.5 million homes over 10 years. Required pace: 150,000 homes per year.

The outcome: - 2022 housing starts: 96,000 — 36% below target - 2023 housing starts: 89,000 — 41% below target - 2024 housing starts: ~83,500 — 44% below target - Cumulative shortfall through 2024: more than 100,000 homes - Source: CMHC Housing Starts by Province; FAO "Ontario's Housing Supply Progress" (2025)

Bill 23 also stripped municipalities of more than $5 billion in development charges province-wide (Association of Municipalities of Ontario). Toronto's own City staff estimated the local impact directly: ~$230M/year in lost development charges, community benefit charges, and parkland levies combined, including $120M/year specifically tied to affordable housing funding — roughly $1.2B over 10 years (City of Toronto staff report, November 2022; corrected here July 2026 from an earlier, smaller Richmond Hill proxy figure — Toronto's own number is now available and should be used).

The housing starts are going in the wrong direction. Each year, the gap grows.

PROMISE #2: "END HALLWAY MEDICINE"

When: 2018 election | Source: PC Party of Ontario platform; 2019 Budget

The commitment: Eliminate patients being treated in hospital hallways.

The outcome: - Ontario now has the fewest acute-care beds per capita of any province in Canada - Source: Financial Accountability Office of Ontario, "Ontario Health Sector: Review of Hospital Capacity" (2023) - Ontario hospitals operated at more than 100% occupancy through 2023-2025 - Ontario Auditor General 2021 follow-up found systemic hospital capacity deficits

Not only was the promise not kept — Ontario went backwards relative to the rest of Canada.

PROMISE #3: "$700 MILLION ANNUALLY TO END HOMELESSNESS"

When: 2022 Budget | Source: Ontario Budget 2022; Ministry of Municipal Affairs and Housing

The commitment: "Invest $700 million annually through the Homelessness Prevention Program" and build 1,500 supportive housing units.

The outcome: - Homelessness in Toronto: 7,300 (April 2021) → 15,418 (October 2024) — more than doubled - Provincial chronic homelessness: nearly doubled to 41,512 people in 2024 - Source: City of Toronto 2024 Street Needs Assessment; Maytree "Poverty Rising" July 2025 - Toronto shelter-to-housing exits in 2024: 4,344 — down from 5,927 the prior year (−26.7%) - The Province suspended the Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit (COHB) from April to October 2024, causing a 26.7% drop in housing exits (5,927 to 4,344 — see TSSS 2026 Budget Notes)

Homelessness doubled. Housing exits fell. Then the Province suspended the program that was working.

A further precision, added July 2026: the Province's own $700M framing was always being measured against a moving target. Toronto's shelter system alone now costs $897.957M/year gross — and that figure itself understates the true public cost, since it excludes what the TTC ($26.2M in 2026 alone), Toronto Public Library, and Parks, Forestry and Recreation now spend absorbing this crisis outside the shelter budget entirely (see HMK-035). The Province's promise was never sized to the actual scale of what one city alone now spends, let alone the province-wide total.

PROMISE #4: "I WILL NOT CUT SOCIAL ASSISTANCE"

When: 2018 election | Source: CBC News; debate transcripts

The commitment: Ford repeatedly stated he would not cut social assistance.

The outcome: - Ontario Works (OW) single adult rate: frozen at $733/month since 2018 - Ontario is the only province in Canada that has not raised single adult social assistance rates since 2018 - Real value of OW has eroded by more than 20% due to inflation over the same period - A single adult on OW receives only $343/month in basic needs if they have no fixed address — the shelter component is denied without an address - ODSP maximum leaves recipients $11,503 per year below the poverty line - Source: Ontario Regulation 134/98; ISAC/ACTO analyses; Statistics Canada CPI

The rates weren't cut. They were frozen while inflation did the cutting.

PROMISE #5: "I WILL NEVER TOUCH THE GREENBELT"

When: 2018 election campaign | Source: Ford leadership and election debates; widely reported

The commitment: "I will never touch the Greenbelt."

The outcome: - November 2022: 7,400 acres removed from the Greenbelt - Ontario Auditor General (August 2023): process was "biased" and favoured certain developers; generated an $8.3-billion windfall for landowners - Integrity Commissioner of Ontario (August 2023): then-Housing Minister Steve Clark violated ethics rules - RCMP criminal investigation confirmed October 2023 - Source: Ontario AG "Special Report on Greenbelt Land Removals" (Aug 2023); Integrity Commissioner Report re: Minister Clark (Aug 2023)

Every watchdog body that examined this found the same thing. The land was returned under public pressure in 2023 — but the investigation continues.

PROMISE #6: "END ENCAMPMENTS" — THE SAFER MUNICIPALITIES ACT

When: Late 2025/early 2026 | Source: Ontario News Release; Premier and Minister statements

The commitment: The Safer Municipalities Act would "put an end to encampments" and "return public spaces to communities."

The outcome: - October 2024 SNA: 1,615 people living outdoors in Toronto (including in encampments) — up from 742 in the April 2021 count, despite the Act passing (corrected July 4, 2026 — a previously-cited "1,446/646" sub-breakdown summed to 2,092, not 1,615, and could not be confirmed for this specific count) - The City's own 2023 Encampment Strategy report (backgroundfile-245818): in all of 2023, 880 encampment residents were referred to shelters and 155 were housed — roughly 17% of contacts resulted in housing - Toronto Ombudsman (March 24, 2023, interim report): City showed "significant unfairness" in its 2021 encampment clearances, choosing "speed over people"; 23 recommendations for reform issued in this interim report. A separate final report followed, and by May 2024 the City reported implementing 28 of a combined interim-plus-final total (31 items), with 3 still in progress — many of the original 23 remained unimplemented by the time the Safer Municipalities Act passed in 2025, even as the City reported broader progress on the combined total. This is a different Ombudsman work-stream entirely from the December 2024 report on refugee claimant shelter access (14 separate recommendations) — see HMK-032/REC M-12. Updated July 2, 2026: the December 2024 report was initially received without adopting any of its 14 recommendations directly, but a substantial March 2025 follow-up motion (CC28.2, 24 directives) has since seen 15 of 24 directives confirmed completed as of a November 2025 status report — this is real, majority follow-through, not "none adopted." Do not cite the earlier "none adopted" framing without this update. - The law passed (Royal Assent: June 5, 2025). Fines up to $10,000. Up to six months in jail. - A court has since directly addressed this exact legal question, added July 2026. On May 21, 2026, in a case concerning a different Ontario municipality's attempt to clear an encampment (Regional Municipality of Waterloo v. Named Respondents and Persons Unknown, 2026 ONSC 2971), Justice Gibson found the clearance by-law violated Charter ss. 7 and 15, not justified under s. 1, and made a historic finding that homelessness itself is an analogous ground under s. 15 equality rights — the first such finding by a Canadian court. ✅ Premier Ford publicly attacked the judge over the ruling in personal terms. ✅ This decision is under appeal by Ontario and the Region of Waterloo as of June 2026 and is not final law — it is cited here as a significant, live legal development directly relevant to the constitutional soundness of the enforcement-first approach this Act embodies, not as settled precedent. See HMK-007 for the complete treatment. - ⚠️ NOTE: A claim that "60% of encampment residents relocated within 500 metres after clearances" appears in advocacy research but has not been confirmed in a City primary document. We are pursuing this through FOI (see DERIV-004 FOI templates). What is confirmed: clearances do not reduce homelessness — they move it.

The Act criminalized the visible symptom. It did not address the cause.

THE PATTERN

These are six separate promises across housing, health, income, integrity, and safety. All six share the same structure: a clear public commitment, an independent body with authority to assess the outcome, and a documented gap.

This is not a matter of perspective. The Auditor General, the Financial Accountability Office, the Integrity Commissioner, the RCMP, and Statistics Canada are not advocacy organizations. They are accountability bodies created by the Ontario Legislature to tell the public the truth about how their government performed.

The record is the record.

HOW TO USE THIS ASSET

For journalists: Each row has a named independent source. Request these documents directly from the issuing bodies; they are all public record.

For candidates: These are questions, not accusations. Ask every provincial candidate: "Will you implement the Ontario AG 2021 recommendation for a provincial homelessness strategy with targets and timelines? Yes or no?"

For Council deputations: "The Province committed $700M/year to homelessness. Chronic homelessness doubled. Housing exits fell. We need City Council to formally demand the Province implement the 2021 Auditor General recommendation — a demand that has gone unanswered for five years."

For social media (five-beat format): Track record established. Ask the question. Give them 24 hours to answer.

TASK E ADDENDUM — THE ACCOUNTABILITY DETAIL (June 30, 2026)

On the $700M: What It Actually Includes

The critical framing correction: The Ford government's "$700M for homelessness" covers only two programs: - Homelessness Prevention Program (HPP): $654M/year to 47 municipal service managers - Indigenous Supportive Housing Program (ISHP): $41.5M/year

What is NOT in the $700M: - Violence Against Women shelters (MCCSS, separately funded) - Domiciliary hostels (Ministry of Health) - HART Hubs ($550M, Ministry of Health — entirely separate envelope)

When Ford says "$700M for homelessness prevention" and then separately announces "$550M for HART Hubs," the implied total of $1.25B is accurate — but these serve completely different purposes from different ministries with no integrated strategy connecting them.

The allocation formula punishes high-need municipalities. Ottawa's example: the city received only $845,100 — 0.4% of the $190.5M HPP increase — from Budget 2023, despite significant and growing need. The allocation formula is based on historical spending and outdated census data — not current homelessness counts. ✅ [Ottawa Council ACS2023-CSS-GEN-0005 (pub-ottawa.escribemeetings.com/filestream.ashx?DocumentId=132402); AG 2023 follow-up]

No annual public outcomes report exists. The Province collects data from service managers but does not publish consolidated results. The AG 2023 follow-up found: "the Ministry has made little progress on 40% of the recommended actions, such as developing and setting targets for common performance indicators." ✅

What independent watchdogs say the scale of investment should be: HelpSeeker (Donaldson et al. 2025, commissioned by AMO) models that $11 billion over 10 years would be required to end chronic homelessness in Ontario. The Province is currently investing $700M/year × 10 years = $7B over a decade. The gap is $4B — and that's before accounting for the current rate of homelessness growth. ✅

On HART Hubs: The Treatment-Housing Confusion

HART Hubs are treatment programs, not housing programs. Residents have no legal right to remain. Permanent housing is a stated goal, not a contractual obligation. The government's own reference document describes housing as a goal to "work toward." ✅

What has been built vs. what was promised: - AMO's own commissioned study (Donaldson et al. 2025: https://www.amo.on.ca/sites/default/files/assets/DOCUMENTS/Reports/2025/2025-01-08-AMOHomelessnessReportSummaryFinal.pdf) found the funding ($550–560 million current, up from an original 2025 announcement of $378M for 19 hubs) represents "an important investment in health services," but assessed the resulting supportive housing capacity (approximately 900 units across 29 hubs by April 2026, up from an original 2025 commitment of 375 homes across 19 hubs) as insufficient. ✅ ⚠️ Corrected June 30, 2026 — this was previously presented as a single direct quotation with editorial clarifications inserted mid-quote in brackets; that misrepresents what AMO's study actually said verbatim. Now paraphrased with the genuine assessment (funding adequate, housing capacity insufficient) preserved accurately, without implying these exact bracketed figures appeared in AMO's original wording. If a direct quote is needed for legal-facing use, pull the exact sentence from the primary Donaldson et al. 2025 report rather than reconstructing one. - As of April 2026: 100,000+ client interactions; 250+ placed in supportive housing ✅ (government self-report) - 8 of 28 planned additional hubs had not opened as of February/March 2026 ✅ - No independent evaluation of outcomes has been published ✅

The overdose call data after closures: Suspected opioid overdose calls increased approximately 119% from April 2025 (when the first nine supervised consumption sites closed) to January 2026 — 160 to 350 calls/month (Toronto Paramedic Services data, reported CBC March 25, 2026 — corrected from an earlier 82% arithmetic error). This reverses a downward trend in non-fatal opioid overdose calls that had been observed from 2023 to early 2025. ✅ [CBC News, March 25, 2026, citing ambulance call data: https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/ontario-defends-supervised-consumption-site-closures-9.7142185]

The government counter-claims a 41% decrease in opioid-related deaths in specific areas near the closed sites. The data picture is genuinely contested. What is not contested: there is no independent evaluation of HART Hub outcomes, and the Province has not published an evidence-based comparison of outcomes before and after the transition.

On the AG 2021 Recommendation: The Clock Is Running

Year one (2021): AG issues 12 recommendations, including Recommendation 1: develop a provincial strategy with specific actions, targets, and timelines. Year two (2022): Province creates HPP, consolidating programs. No strategy document. Year three (2023): AG follow-up finds 40% of recommendations showing "little or no progress." The provincial strategy recommendation is in this category. Year four (2024): Province announces HART Hubs and Safer Municipalities Act. No strategy document. Year five (2025-26): Safer Municipalities Act passes. Province says 28 HART Hubs operational. No strategy document.

The Province has not published a provincial homelessness strategy with specific actions, targets, and timelines — five years after the Auditor General said it must. ✅ [AG 2021: https://www.auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en21/AR_Homelessness_en21.pdf; AG 2023 follow-up: https://www.auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en23/1-07FU_homelessness_en23.pdf]

On the OW Shelter Allowance: The Policy Nobody Talks About

Ontario Works pays homeless people less income support because they are homeless.

OW has two components: basic needs ($343/month single adult) and shelter (maximum $390/month single adult). The shelter component requires proof of shelter costs — rent paid to a landlord. A person sleeping in a tent on public land pays no rent, therefore receives no shelter allowance. The most vulnerable OW recipients receive approximately $343/month — not $733/month. ✅

This means the Province's income support system actively penalizes homelessness with a benefit deduction — precisely at the point when a person is most at risk of deepening into chronic homelessness.
