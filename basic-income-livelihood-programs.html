Scope

This backgrounder's neutral scope question, per research/issue-map.md row G2: "What basic-income, guaranteed-livelihood, or income-security pilots and proposals exist or have been studied for this region, and what evidence exists on their outcomes?" Per the same row, income security sits outside municipal jurisdiction (federal/provincial owner); the municipal role is limited to advocacy, pilot-hosting, and poverty-reduction strategy.

This document covers: the Ontario Basic Income Pilot's origins, consultation process, and design; the design literature that shaped it (the "Finding a Better Way" discussion paper and its Mowat Centre companion); the peer-reviewed and journalistic research produced both during and after the pilot, including its early cancellation; federal costing analyses of a national guaranteed income modelled on the pilot's own parameters; Canada's existing federal income-security programs; the civil-society/advocacy organization landscape and the municipal-advocacy angle; and Ontario's Poverty Reduction Strategy as the provincial policy backdrop these proposals sit against.

This document does not cover, and hands off by name: the deeper UBI/UBS evidence base (design variants, the Finland/Stockton/GiveDirectly-Kenya international pilot literature, Canada's Child Benefit/GIS as de-facto basic incomes) already synthesized in basic-income-universal-services.md — a different, non-ledger-driven document under a leaf taxonomy outside this repo's 39-slug issue-map.md registry; this document's own claim set overlaps that territory only at the margins (see cross-references below) and does not re-derive its synthesis. The exact Ontario Works/ODSP rate history, freeze periods, and erosion relative to the poverty line belong to ontario-works-rate-history-erosion.md; the deep-poverty-as-upstream-cause-of-homelessness framing belongs to deep-poverty-income-security.md — this document cites current-baseline social-assistance figures only as context for why income-security proposals are being made, not as a rate-history analysis in its own right. The broader LIVELIHOOD program family's movement/visionary material lives in research/livelihood/ (per issue-map.md's own wave-4 note, that directory remains the source of record for the program's full visionary scope); this document covers only the concrete, institutionally-checkable facts the ledger has verified, not that broader mission.

Current state

The Basic Income Earth Network's 2026 Toronto Congress

The Basic Income Earth Network (BIEN), a global basic-income research and advocacy network, will hold its 2026 Congress in Toronto. Per BIEN's own official Congress website, the event bills itself as "The 25th BIEN Congress," running 19–22 August 2026 under the theme "Basic Income and the Polycrisis: The Key to Unlocking the Puzzle" [CL-100058]. Main programming runs Thursday, August 20 through Saturday, August 22, 2026, entirely at one Toronto location, with a separate pre-Congress meeting on "movement building in Canada" scheduled for Wednesday afternoon, August 19, at a different Toronto address [CL-100059]. The Congress's stated theme frames basic income as a response to a "polycrisis" spanning climate disruption, economic inflation and inequality, eroding political trust, and strain on the rights-based international order, describing basic income as "gaining renewed attention not only as an anti-poverty measure, but as a foundational policy for strengthening social inclusion and solidarity" [CL-100060]. A separate ledger row on this same event, CL-120012, carries a DISPUTED trust status and is not relied on for any of the facts above — see "What the evidence does and doesn't support" below for the full discrepancy and correction history.

The Ontario Basic Income Pilot (2017–2019): consultation and design

Ontario's 2016–17 Basic Income Pilot consultations (November 3, 2016 to January 31, 2017) drew 32,870 responses to a public online survey, 1,193 attendees at in-person public meetings, 1,213 responses to a separate expert/sector survey, and more than 450 written submissions from private citizens plus 87 from community groups [CL-100051]. The consultations' Discussion Paper proposed setting benefit levels at 75 or 100 percent of the Low Income Measure (LIM); using 2016 estimates, 75 percent of LIM would equal $16,989 annually for a single person or $24,027 for a couple, with participants with disabilities recommended to receive an additional $500 per month on top of that amount [CL-100052]. The consultations tested support for a Negative Income Tax (NIT) model — also described as a "refundable tax credit" — while the underlying Discussion Paper specifically recommended against a Universal Basic Income ("demogrant") model in which all adults would receive the same payment regardless of income, citing prohibitive cost [CL-100053]. Formal written submissions came from multiple Ontario municipalities and regional governments, including the Regional Municipality of York, the Regional Municipality of Durham, the Region of Peel, Halton Region, and Toronto Public Health [CL-100054] — an early instance of the municipal-advocacy role the issue-map row's own scope column names as this issue's municipal lever.

The resulting Ontario Basic Income Pilot (OBIP) tested a single Negative Income Tax reduction (clawback) rate of 50 percent: for every dollar of earned income, a participant's benefit was reduced by 50 cents, so a recipient with the $16,989 individual benefit would have needed earned income to reach $33,978 before the benefit was reduced to zero [CL-100055]. OBIP's Lindsay site was intended to function as a "saturation site" analogous to the 1970s Manitoba Mincome experiment's Dauphin site (where the entire population was eligible), but per a 2019 retrospective, Lindsay did not become a true saturation site: enrollment was cut off after an initial period, excluding residents who failed to enroll or qualify at that time for the remainder of the pilot [CL-100056]. The same retrospective states that the Ontario government did not provide a public explanation for why the Lindsay site fell short of a true saturation site, despite continuing to officially refer to it as "the saturation site" [CL-100057].

Design literature behind the pilot: the Segal report and its Mowat Centre companion

Ontario's 2016 discussion paper "Finding a Better Way: A Basic Income Pilot Project for Ontario," authored by government Special Advisor Hugh D. Segal, was commissioned following a basic income pilot commitment announced in the March 2016 Ontario Budget [CL-140630]. The 101-page paper is organized into sections covering why poverty matters, similar activities in other jurisdictions, the pilot's proposed organization, what the pilot should and should not test, and its implementation, and is explicitly framed by its author as recommending constructive options for pilot design rather than a single fixed proposal [CL-140631]. A companion report, "Pilot Lessons: How to Design a Basic Income Pilot Project for Ontario" (2016), co-authored by Evelyn Forget, Dylan Marando, Tonya Surman, and Michael Crawford Urban and published by the Mowat Centre at the University of Toronto's School of Public Policy and Governance, drew on comparative basic-income research to inform the design of the then-forthcoming pilot [CL-140632].

Research findings during and after the pilot, and its cancellation

A peer-reviewed study of OBIP participants in the Hamilton region, published in Basic Income Studies (De Gruyter, vol. 16, issue 2, December 2021), found "a small decline in labour market participation, but improvements on a variety of quality-of-life measurements," comparing OBIP against basic-income randomized control experiments conducted in high-income countries between 1968 and 2019 [CL-100077]. The same study hypothesizes that OBIP's comparatively positive well-being results can be attributed to its generous benefit rates relative to social assistance rates, its 50 percent clawback rate, its unconditionality, and its broad well-being-focused design [CL-100078].

A separate, McMaster/Ryerson(Toronto Metropolitan University)-affiliated study, "Southern Ontario's Basic Income Experience," conducted in partnership with the Hamilton Roundtable for Poverty Reduction, surveyed 217 former OBIP recipients in Hamilton, Brantford, and Brant County via a 70-question anonymous online survey, plus 40 in-depth interviews completed in July 2019; nearly three-quarters of respondents who were working when the pilot began reported that they kept working while receiving the benefit [CL-100079]. Per the same survey as reported by CBC News, nearly 80 percent of respondents reported better overall health while on the pilot, 83 percent described feeling stressed or anxious less often, and 81 percent said they felt more self-confident [CL-100080]. OBIP was a three-year, $150-million program that the province's government cancelled before completing its planned three-year run; a member of the research team behind the Southern Ontario study described the early cancellation as "the tragedy of the pilot not running for three years" [CL-100081] — a characterization from within the research team, not from an independent oversight body (see "What the evidence does and doesn't support" below).

Two more recent qualitative studies extend this record. A 2024 study in Social Policy and Society (Cambridge University Press) found that the pilot's reduced surveillance and unconditional payments helped participants feel "rehumanised as full members of society" relative to their experience of Ontario Works and ODSP [CL-140633], and the same study found that the pilot's premature 2018 cancellation "severely damaged participants' trust in government institutions" [CL-140634]. "Life on Basic Income: Stories from Southern Ontario" (2023), authored by Mohammad Ferdosi, Tom McDowell, Amy Ma, Kendal David, Rebekah Ederer, and Beth Martin with funding from the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council and Carleton University, was produced in partnership with the Hamilton Roundtable for Poverty Reduction, building on the same authors' original OBIP research project [CL-140635].

Federal costing analyses of a national guaranteed income

The Office of the Parliamentary Budget Officer (PBO) has repeatedly costed a national guaranteed basic income modelled explicitly on OBIP's own parameters. Its 2018 report estimated a gross cost of $76.0 billion in 2018–19, rising to nearly $79.5 billion by 2022–23, netting to approximately $43 billion after existing federal benefits are accounted for [CL-140620], and estimated the program would benefit approximately 7.7 million people in its base year [CL-140621]. A follow-up April 2021 PBO report examined the same design's distributional impact across income quintiles, family types, and gender, and identified the net federal revenue increase that would be required to offset its net cost, incorporating estimated behavioural responses [CL-140622]. A February 2025 PBO update estimated the gross cost at $107 billion in 2025 using a nuclear-family unit definition of eligibility, or $53 billion using a broader economic-family definition [CL-140623], and estimated the same program would reduce poverty (per Canada's Market Basket Measure) by 34 percent under the nuclear-family definition or 40 percent under the economic-family definition, with behavioural costs from labour-supply changes adding a further $5 billion or $3.6 billion respectively [CL-140624].

Canada's existing federal income-security programs

Beyond pilots and proposals, Canada already runs targeted federal income-security programs relevant to this issue's scope. The Canada Disability Benefit Act received Royal Assent on June 22, 2023, and came into force on June 22, 2024, establishing the Canada Disability Benefit to reduce poverty and support the financial security of low-income working-age persons with disabilities [CL-140625]. The Guaranteed Income Supplement (GIS) is a monthly, non-taxable federal benefit paid to low-income seniors aged 65 and older already receiving Old Age Security, with benefit amounts recalculated each July based on the recipient's net income from the prior calendar year [CL-140627]. The National Advisory Council on Poverty's 2024 report, "A Time for Urgent Action," tabled in Parliament on October 28, 2024, highlighted concerns about the availability and affordability of safe and suitable housing, the increasing cost of feeding families, and long hours combined with low wages of work [CL-140626].

Civil-society organizations and the municipal-advocacy angle

Several advocacy organizations are active in this space. The Ontario Basic Income Network (OBIN) describes itself as "a coalition committed to seeing Basic Income implemented in Ontario," and states that in February 2023 it developed and approved, together with a national basic income movement, a "Consensus Statement on a Basic Income Guarantee in Canada" [CL-100061]. OBIN's website describes a "Basic Income Ontario Municipal Campaign" under which, per an October 2024 post, "resolutions were passed at many of the municipal councils" in Ontario in support of basic income, with OBIN hosting a forum on the campaign's outcomes featuring what it terms "municipal champions" [CL-100062] — this is the clearest documented instance in this claim set of the municipal advocacy/pilot-hosting role the issue-map row's scope column names, since income security itself sits outside municipal authority.

Basic Income Canada Network (BICN) describes itself as advancing "human-rights based public policy that ensures everyone has enough income to meet basic needs, live with dignity, and participate in society and the economy" [CL-100063]. BICN's May 2024 Basic Income Guarantee (BIG) Forum, held in Ottawa, drew 334 participants — 223 in person and 111 virtually — from diverse organizations across Canada and beyond [CL-100064]. BICN's website also describes a report, "Basic Income: Some Policy Options for Canada," modelling "fair, effective and feasible options" for how Canada could design and fund a basic income program [CL-100065]. Coalition Canada: Basic Income – Revenu de base describes itself as a cross-country alliance of basic-income advocacy groups established in 2019, operating as a non-partisan volunteer group [CL-140628], with stated objectives that include recommending the federal government design and implement a national livable Basic Income Guarantee, working alongside partner organizations including BICN [CL-140629].

Ontario's Poverty Reduction Strategy and the current social-assistance baseline

Ontario's 2025 annual report on its Poverty Reduction Strategy states that annual exits from social assistance to employment reached 43,425 in 2025 — a 24 percent increase over 2024 — up from a 2019 baseline of 35,985, though still short of the strategy's stated target of 60,000 annual exits [CL-100066]. The same report states the government received "more than 3,600 responses" during public consultations on the province's next five-year strategy, from individuals with lived experience of poverty as well as non-profit, Indigenous, and public-sector organizations and employers [CL-100067]. It also reports the general-population (15–64) employment rate at 72.5 percent in 2025, down from a peak of 74.8 percent in 2023, alongside a cumulative 20 percent increase in ODSP/ACSD rates since September 2022, including a 2.8 percent increase in July 2025 [CL-100068]. Ontario's minimum wage increased from $17.20 to $17.60 per hour effective October 1, 2025 [CL-100069].

Civil-society reporting describes a starker baseline. A November 2025 joint submission by the Income Security Advocacy Centre (ISAC) and Advocacy Centre for Tenants Ontario (ACTO) states that Ontario Works (OW) rates for single employable adults — 30 percent of individual social assistance cases as of June 2025 — have been frozen at $733 per month since 2018 [CL-100070], and that OW's earnings exemption has remained frozen at $200 per month since 2013, after which a 50 percent deduction applies to every additional dollar earned — meaning, at the October 2025 minimum wage, a recipient begins losing 50 percent of earnings after just 11.2 hours of work per month [CL-100071]. The same submission states that ODSP's 2.8 percent inflation adjustment in July 2025 raised the monthly rate for a single recipient by $40, to $1,408 [CL-100072], and cites a 2025 HelpSeeker/Association of Municipalities of Ontario report finding at least 81,515 Ontarians experienced homelessness in 2024, with homelessness in Northern Ontario communities specifically up 70 percent over five years and more than 200 percent since 2016 [CL-100073]. Food Banks Canada's 2026 Poverty Report Card for Ontario states that OW rates have been frozen since 2018 and that social assistance covers just 32.7 percent of the poverty line in Ontario — the second-lowest proportion of any province — with about 71 percent of surveyed support recipients saying benefits are insufficient, the highest share in the country [CL-100074]. The same report states homelessness in the province grew nearly 8 percent since 2024 to approximately 85,000 people unhoused, including an estimated 23 percent who are youth and children, projecting this could reach 300,000 by 2035 without intervention [CL-100075], and that average Ontario rents climbed 54.5 percent between 2014 and 2023 — more than three times the legally permitted annual rent-increase guideline — while units built after 2018 carry no rent-control protections [CL-100076]. This baseline material is cited here only as context for why income-security proposals are being made; the exact OW/ODSP rate history and freeze trajectory over time is ontario-works-rate-history-erosion.md's own dedicated territory, not re-derived here.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

Fiscal scale of a national guaranteed income versus advocacy positions calling for full implementation. The PBO's own repeated costing exercises put a national guaranteed basic income modelled on OBIP's parameters at tens of billions of dollars net, even after existing federal benefits are accounted for — $43 billion net in 2018–19 terms [CL-140620], rising to a $107 billion/$53 billion gross range by 2025 depending on family-unit definition [CL-140623]. Set against that fiscal-scale evidence, both BICN and Coalition Canada state organizational positions calling for a full national "livable" Basic Income Guarantee [CL-100063, CL-140629], and OBIN's own discussion-paper design record explicitly rejected a universal ("demogrant") model specifically because "financial costs would be prohibitive" [CL-100053]. This document does not have a ledger claim reconciling the PBO's dollar figures against any advocacy organization's own costed proposal for how a program at that guaranteed-income design would be paid for — the two sides are stated here side by side, not adjudicated.

The pilot's own stated design intent versus its documented implementation shortfall. OBIP's Lindsay site was designed to replicate the Manitoba Mincome experiment's "saturation site" model, where the entire population would be eligible throughout the pilot [CL-100056]. The pilot fell short of that design in execution — enrollment was cut off after an initial period — and the same source that documents this also states the government provided no public explanation for the gap, while continuing to refer to Lindsay as "the saturation site" [CL-100057]. Both halves of this tension trace to the same single 2019 retrospective source; no second, independent account of the Lindsay shortfall sits in this claim set (see "What the evidence does and doesn't support" below).

The province's own progress framing versus civil-society's inadequacy framing on the current social-assistance baseline. Ontario's own 2025 Poverty Reduction Strategy annual report frames a 24 percent year-over-year increase in social-assistance-to-employment exits as "substantial progress," while acknowledging it fell short of the strategy's own 60,000-exit target [CL-100066]. Civil-society reporting from the same period states the underlying OW rate has been frozen at $733/month since 2018 and covers only 32.7 percent of the poverty line [CL-100070, CL-100074]. Both figures are independently sourced (one from the province's own annual report, two from independent civil-society organizations) and are not in factual conflict with each other — they describe different indicators (employment-exit flow versus benefit-level adequacy) — but they sit in genuine interpretive tension about how much "progress" the current strategy represents, which this document surfaces without adjudicating.

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. This issue engages a real, precisely pinned international-law connection distinct from the housing-specific ICESCR anchor other backgrounders in this corpus use: Article 9 of the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), which protects "the right of everyone to social security, including social insurance" — the Covenant's dedicated income-security provision, as distinct from Article 11's adequate-standard-of-living/housing language. Canada ratified the ICESCR in 1976 and remains a state party. The UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights' General Comment No. 19 elaborates that this right covers access to and maintenance of benefits (cash or in-kind) protecting against lack of work-related income from unemployment, old age, disability, and similar contingencies. No claim in this project's own ledger yet traces how Canada's specific programs above (GIS, the Canada Disability Benefit, OW/ODSP) are formally assessed against this obligation by any Canadian or international body — this is a real treaty connection, stated as such, not one this claim set has independently verified being applied to these specific programs.

2. 2–3 best global comparators. This document's own claim set does not itself contain new international pilot-evidence claims — the deep international literature (Finland's 2017–18 basic income experiment, Stockton's SEED program, GiveDirectly's Kenya study) is already covered in full by basic-income-universal-services.md, which this document points to by name rather than re-deriving. One genuinely new comparator surfaced by a live discovery search for this pass: Wales' Basic Income for Care Leavers pilot provided 644 care-experienced young people (97% of those eligible) with £1,600/month, pre-tax, for up to 24 months after their 18th birthday, with signup open from July 2022 to June 2023; delivery has concluded but evaluation activities continue through a final report expected in 2027 (Welsh Government, "Basic income for care leavers in Wales pilot evaluation: annual report 2024 to 2025," gov.wales, accessed 2026-07-22). Unlike Finland's or Stockton's general-population pilots, Wales targeted one specific, clearly bounded vulnerable population — a design worth noting precisely because it differs structurally from the universal/broad-population pilots already covered elsewhere in this corpus.

3. What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. The Wales pilot's population-specific design — a guaranteed income targeted at one clearly bounded, high-need population (care leavers) rather than a general-population trial — is the specific, nameable structural feature most transferable to this issue's own municipal lever: the issue-map row's own scope column names "pilot-hosting" as a municipal role even where full income-security authority sits with senior governments, and Wales' model shows what a bounded, population-specific guaranteed-income pilot can look like in practice at a sub-national government's own initiative, without requiring a full national program first. This is stated descriptively, as a fact about how that comparator is designed — not as a recommendation that Toronto or Ontario adopt it.

What do Torontonians & Ontarians think?

No Toronto-specific or Ontario-specific public polling on basic income or guaranteed income was located in a bounded search for this pass. The nearest available measurement is Canada-wide, not Ontario- or Toronto-specific, and dated rather than current. The Angus Reid Institute (ARI), using its online Angus Reid Forum panel, has measured Canadian support for a guaranteed/basic income at least twice: in 2016 (a poll ARI describes as drawing on its representative panel, methodology corroborated to a sample of 1,516 Canadian adults for that release) it found 67 percent support for a guaranteed income of $30,000 annually, conditional on the payment replacing most or all other government assistance, alongside 66 percent unwilling to pay more in taxes to fund it; and again on June 18, 2020 (during the COVID-19 CERB extension period), it found support "relatively unchanged" from 2016 — approximately 60 percent support at each of $10,000, $20,000, and $30,000 proposed annual levels, alongside roughly two-thirds resistance to funding it through personal tax increases and majority support (61%) for funding it through taxes on the wealthy instead. Exact sample size and margin of error for the 2020 release specifically were not available on ARI's own public page for this pass; this document states that gap explicitly rather than inventing a number.

Both measurements are Canada-wide (not Ontario- or Toronto-specific) and dated (2016 and 2020, not 2025–2026) — a real recency and jurisdiction gap, stated plainly per this section's own discipline rather than stretched to stand in for a current Ontario-specific measurement. No poll specific to this issue and to Ontario or Toronto, and no measurement more recent than 2020, was located in this pass's search.
