Scope

Neutral scope question for this leaf: what does Toronto's centralized Rent-Geared-to-Income (RGI) waitlist actually measure, how has it grown, and where do published figures require terminology care before being cited? This document covers: the size and five-plus-year growth trend of Toronto's centralized RGI waiting list; what the published number does and does not represent (households vs. individuals, active vs. total applications, centralized vs. program-specific lists); wait times and who gets housed, by household type and priority category; and how the waitlist trend compares to the pace of new RGI/social housing supply. It hands off, by name, questions of TCHC's own building-level vacancy and capital backlog to tchc-vacancy-capital-backlog; shelter-system capacity and flow to shelter-system-capacity-strain; and the broader housing-supply/affordability picture (zoning, land use, market rents) to housing-supply-affordability. It does not take a position on whether the City's waitlist administration is adequate — that is a card-level (L6) and brief-level (L5) question, addressed downstream of this document, not in it.

Current state

The trend, quarter by quarter, from the City's own primary table

Toronto's centralized RGI waiting list — administered through Access to Housing, the City's intake and application-management function for subsidized housing — grew from 81,664 households at year-end 2020 to 105,115 households at year-end 2025, an increase of just under 29% over five years [INHERITED, promoted growth doc; City of Toronto, Social Housing Waiting List Reports]. The year-by-year path was 81,664 (2020) → 78,879 (2021) → 84,282 (2022) → 85,097 (2023) → 100,361 (2024) → 105,115 (2025) [INHERITED]. The single largest jump in this series is 2023→2024 (+15,264, +18% in one year) — larger than any other year-over-year move in the table — and it predates the 2026 reporting-methodology change described below, which makes it the cleanest "before" figure to cite when illustrating the scale of recent growth [INHERITED].

NEW, direct primary-source check (2026-07-13): this document independently re-fetched the City's live Social Housing Waiting List Reports page rather than relying on the promoted docs' characterization of it. The page (https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/data-research-maps/research-reports/housing-and-homelessness-research-and-reports/social-housing-waiting-list-reports/, page metadata "Date modified: May 13, 2026," fetched 2026-07-13) shows the following quarterly path, quoted directly from its own tables:

  • 2024 (confirms the terminology-trap finding below): Q1 86,959 → Q2 89,838 → Q3 92,965 → Q4 100,361.
  • 2025: Q1 104,239 → Q2 104,585 → Q3 104,904 → Q4 105,115. Full-year 2025 activity: "New" 21,538; "Housed" 2,838; "Removed from Waiting list" 6,241.
  • 2026 (current, as published): Q1 104,851 (New 4,361; Housed 750; Removed from Waiting List 2,282). Q2, Q3, and Q4 2026 cells are blank on the live page as of this access — the site's own header states "Quarterly information will be added as it becomes available; empty cells indicate data is not yet released."

This is a direct reconfirmation, not new information contradicting the promoted docs: Q1 2026's 104,851 is still the most current published figure as of 2026-07-13, exactly matching what this library's prior synthesis document (Social Housing Waitlist Data) Part 6 already found on July 4, 2026. The one-quarter-stale risk flagged for this leaf did not materialize this session — no newer quarter has been posted in the ten days between that prior check and this one.

What the number counts, and what it does not: three terminology traps, named explicitly

Trap 1 — households vs. individuals. The single most consequential terminology error this leaf's promoted documents already corrected: CBC's January 2025 reporting described "92,965 people on the city's waiting list for social housing" [INHERITED, quoting CBC via the promoted HMK-025 doc]. The City's own quarterly table defines that exact figure as a household/application count, not a headcount of people: Q3 2024's "Total Active Applications" breaks into household-no-dependents (33,145) + household-with-dependents (23,659) + seniors (36,161) = 92,965 — an applications/household total, confirmed exactly against the live table this session [NEW, City of Toronto Social Housing Waiting List Reports, fetched 2026-07-13: Q3 2024 row shows "Household no dependents 33,145 / Household with dependents 23,659 / Senior 36,161 / Total 92,965"]. Because one household application can represent more than one person, the true number of individuals waiting is higher than every headline figure in this document — a distinction worth stating plainly every time a figure is cited, not just once.

Trap 2 — active applications vs. total/new/removed activity. The City's table tracks four distinct quantities per quarter that are easy to conflate: "New" applications received, "Housed" (applications that resulted in a move-in), "Removed from Waiting List" (applications that exit without being housed), and "Total Active Waiting List" (the standing stock at quarter-end). A reader citing "21,538" (2025's New applications) alongside "2,838" (2025's Housed) is comparing flow-into vs. flow-out-via-housing; neither is the same quantity as "105,115" (the year-end stock). The City's own 2026 reporting-methodology note makes this sharper still: "Reactivated applications were previously counted with new applications. Reactivated is now counted as part of the Total Active Waiting List; Cancelled Applications is now termed as Removed from Waiting List, which is defined as removed due to Single Offer Rule, no longer RGI eligible, or self-requested removal" [NEW, quoted verbatim from the City's live page, fetched 2026-07-13]. This means the pre-2025 and 2025-onward "New" and "Total Active Waiting List" lines are not built on an identical counting rule — a genuine methodology seam, not a data error, but one that should be disclosed whenever a multi-year trend line crosses it.

Trap 3 — centralized waitlist vs. program-specific lists. The figures in this document describe Toronto's single Centralized Waiting List for RGI housing, administered via Access to Housing: "Eligible applicants join the Centralized Waiting List for RGI housing" [NEW, quoted verbatim from https://www.toronto.ca/community-people/housing-shelter/access-community-housing/rent-geared-to-income-subsidy/, fetched 2026-07-13]. This is distinct from any individual housing provider's own supplementary or internal list (for example, a co-operative's internal transfer list, or a specific building's direct-application process outside the centralized RGI system) and distinct from portable rent-assistance programs such as the Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit (COHB), which the leaf's promoted documents flag — without fully resolving — as a program whose relationship to the centralized-list figures (mutually exclusive enrolment, or something else) was not confirmed in a prior research pass [INHERITED, this library's prior synthesis document (..) Part 3, HMK-008 cross-reference]. ⚠️ VERIFY: this document does not resolve the COHB/centralized-list relationship either — it is named here as an open question, not answered.

⚠️ VERIFY (entity naming): live search results this session describe Toronto's centralized-list administrator under two names — "Access to Housing" (the name used throughout the City's current live pages, confirmed directly) and "Housing Connections" (described in secondary sources — a CLEO Connect legal-resource listing and a corporate-data aggregator — as a predecessor name or a formally separate Toronto Community Housing subsidiary that historically ran the same function) [NEW, secondary sources only, not independently confirmed against a primary City or TCHC document this session]. Anyone citing older material that uses "Housing Connections" should treat it as potentially the same function under a different or prior name, not assume it is confirmed identical, until a primary source is checked directly.

Wait times, who gets housed, and by what rule

Two independent, non-City sources corroborate that waits are measured in years, not months. The Ontario Human Rights Commission's "Policy on Human Rights and Rental Housing" states TCHC-specific wait times of 7 to 8 years [INHERITED, OHRC, cited in this library's prior synthesis document (..) Part 2]. Separately, the City's own 2019 Auditor General report on the waitlist — a primary City oversight document distinct from the quarterly activity tables — found: "5% of applicants are prioritized, and they wait a significantly shorter period of time for housing. All other applicants are managed on a first-come, first-served basis. People can wait more than 10 years for subsidized housing." [NEW, quoted verbatim, Toronto Auditor General, "Opening Doors to Stable Housing: An Effective Waiting List and Reduced Vacancy Rates Will Help More People Access Housing," June 21, 2019 (report modified February 25, 2020), https://www.torontoauditor.ca/report/opening-doors-to-stable-housing-an-effective-waiting-list-and-reduced-vacancy-rates-will-help-more-people-access-housing/, fetched 2026-07-13]. The same report also found housing-unit underuse contributing to the delay: "$7 million in vacancy losses," and that "a 50% improvement in these areas would result in 2,200 more people getting RGI housing assistance" without adding a single new unit [NEW, same source, same access].

That 2019 baseline — first-come-first-served for 95% of applicants — is the system the City's subsequent queue reform was built to change. Council adopted a new allocation model in July 2022: 50% of units in buildings with more than 10 units go through a randomized lottery, and the other 50% go to applicants who have waited longest, with implementation described as targeted for "mid to late 2025" as of CBC's January 2025 reporting [INHERITED, CBC News, January 6, 2025, citing Doug Rollins, Director of Housing Stability Services, Housing Secretariat, per this library's prior synthesis document (..) Part 2]. Seven priority categories skip the general queue entirely, ranked (highest first): survivors of domestic/family abuse and human trafficking (Ontario's Special Priority Policy); households in existing subsidized units downsizing; terminally ill applicants; households exiting supportive housing; applicants experiencing homelessness; 16–17-year-old heads of household; Indigenous applicants [INHERITED, same source]. Whether the lottery/portal reform is now operationally live, rather than still a described framework, remained unconfirmed as of the promoted doc's own last check (October 2025 CBC coverage) [INHERITED] — this session's discovery pass did not find a more current confirmation either and flags it as still open, not resolved.

The City's Q1 2026 "Housed by Applicant Category" breakdown gives a live, current-quarter snapshot of how the priority queue actually distributes outcomes: of 750 households housed, 220 were Special Priority, 24 terminally ill, 11 over-housed (downsizing), 44 exiting supportive housing (HESH), 127 homeless/youth, 18 Indigenous, and 306 from the "General list" [NEW, City of Toronto Social Housing Waiting List Reports, Q1 2026 table, fetched 2026-07-13]. The General list (the ordinary, non-priority queue) still accounts for the largest single share (306 of 750, 41%) of who actually moved into housing in the current quarter, alongside the 444 households housed through a named priority category — a fact worth citing whenever a claim is made about whether priority categories have "taken over" the queue outcome.

Comparison to the supply pipeline

The gap between demand entering the list and the pace of housing placements is large and directly quantifiable from the City's own 2025 full-year figures: 21,538 new applications against 2,838 households actually housed — an inflow-to-housed ratio of roughly 7.6 to 1, and a 2.7% annual exit-to-housing rate measured against the 105,115 year-end list [INHERITED, this library's inherited source document (social housing waitlist growth)]. This document does not attempt to model why the exit rate is this low (funding, unit supply, eligibility-processing capacity, vacancy/turnover inefficiency, or some combination) — that causal question belongs to housing-supply-affordability and tchc-vacancy-capital-backlog, which this document hands off to by name rather than answering here. What this document does state, citably: the 2019 Auditor General finding above shows unit-utilization inefficiency ($7 million in vacancy losses, an estimated 2,200 additional people housable through better use of existing stock without new construction) was a documented, quantified part of the picture as of 2019 [NEW]; whether that specific inefficiency has since been resolved is not confirmed in this pass.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

  • A queue reform framed as fairness vs. a queue that, in its most current quarter, still houses more people off the plain seniority list than any single priority category. The 2022-adopted lottery/seniority-blend reform was itself framed by the City as correcting first-come-first-served's failure to serve urgent, recently-arrived need [INHERITED]. Yet the Q1 2026 outcome data shows the ordinary ("General list") queue still produced the single largest housed cohort (306 of 750) [NEW]. This is not necessarily a contradiction — a reform can be real and still leave the general queue as the largest single category by volume, especially before the lottery is confirmed fully operational — but it is a genuine tension between the reform's framing and the most current outcome breakdown, cited on both sides here rather than resolved.
  • A 2026 reporting-methodology change that improves definitional clarity but complicates trend continuity. Folding reactivated applications into the Total Active Waiting List, and precisely defining "Removed from Waiting List," is a genuine improvement in what the number means [NEW, quoted methodology note above] — but it also means the pre-2025 and 2025-onward series are not on an identical basis, a fact that would be lost if the trend were cited as one unbroken line without disclosure [INHERITED + NEW, both].

Well-supported: the overall five-year growth trend (81,664 → 105,115, 2020–2025) is directly confirmed against the City's own primary quarterly table, independently re-fetched this session and matching the promoted docs' figures exactly [INHERITED + NEW, cross-checked]. The households-not-individuals terminology correction is similarly well-supported: the exact arithmetic match (33,145 + 23,659 + 36,161 = 92,965) was independently re-derived from the live table this session, not merely taken on the promoted doc's word [NEW]. TCHC-specific 7-8 year wait times draw on an independent non-City source (OHRC) consistent with the scale implied by the overall list size [INHERITED].

Thin or contested: whether "Housing Connections" and "Access to Housing" name the same function historically or a formally distinct predecessor entity rests on secondary sources only, not a primary City/TCHC confirmation, and is flagged ⚠️ VERIFY above [NEW, unresolved]. The COHB/centralized-list relationship is flagged, not resolved, by the leaf's own promoted documents and remains open here [INHERITED, unresolved]. Whether the 2022-adopted lottery/seniority queue reform is now fully operational, versus still a described framework, is unconfirmed in both the promoted docs (as of October 2025) and this session's discovery pass (as of 2026-07-13) [INHERITED + NEW, both unresolved].

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. This leaf engages the same genuine, precisely pinned international-law connection as its sibling leaves in this corpus: the right to adequate housing under Article 11(1) of the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), domesticated via the National Housing Strategy Act (S.C. 2019, c. 29) — see homelessness-federal-funding-architecture.md's International context section for the Act's own statutory text. This leaf's own specific point of engagement is narrower and directly load-bearing for its own subject matter: the UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights' General Comment No. 4 (1991) states that satisfying Article 11(1) requires a State party to "demonstrate that it has taken whatever steps are necessary... to ascertain the full extent of homelessness and inadequate housing within its jurisdiction" [cited per homelessness-international-comparators.md's own citation of the same General Comment, WebSearch, accessed 2026-07-14] — a measurement obligation this entire leaf is, in effect, a case study of one specific system failing to meet cleanly: this document's own "three terminology traps" (households-vs-individuals, methodology-seam discontinuity, centralized-vs-program-specific list confusion) are exactly the kind of measurement-integrity gap the General Comment's "ascertain the full extent" language contemplates. This is not independently re-verified against the UN's own primary treaty archive in this pass, flagged the same ⚠️ VERIFY way the international-comparators leaf flags it.

2. Best global comparators. This leaf's own subject — a centralized rent-geared-to-income waitlist's measurement and reporting integrity — is not the kind of policy-outcome question homelessness-international-comparators.md covers in depth (that leaf's own scope is comparative Point-in-Time counts, program effectiveness, and treaty grounding, not waitlist-administration mechanics specifically), and this document does not force a comparator that leaf's own evidence base doesn't actually support. The one genuinely relevant comparator available in this corpus: the UK's Rough Sleeping Initiative evaluation, which homelessness-international-comparators.md documents as "one of the few homelessness interventions with a peer-reviewed, regression-adjusted impact evaluation anywhere in this leaf's evidence base" — a Difference-in-Differences design, methodologically more rigorous than the before/after or point-in-time comparisons most of this corpus's evidence rests on. This is relevant here specifically because it illustrates what a rigorously-measured social intervention looks like, in useful contrast to this leaf's own documented methodology seams (the 2026 reactivated-applications counting-rule change, the households-vs-individuals conflation) — named here as a contrast in measurement rigor, not as a directly comparable waitlist-administration program.

3. What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. Stated descriptively, not as a recommendation: the specific, nameable methodological practice worth naming is the UK evaluation's own Difference-in-Differences design — a measurement approach that produces an attributable causal estimate rather than a raw stock/flow count — applied here by analogy to this leaf's own subject: nothing in this leaf's evidence base suggests Toronto's own waitlist reporting has ever been evaluated for whether its queue-allocation reform (the 2022-adopted 50% lottery/50% seniority blend) causally changed housing-placement outcomes, as distinct from simply reporting the raw "Housed by Applicant Category" breakdown this document already cites. This is the same transferable methodology-gap point shelter-system-capacity-strain.md's own International context section makes about Toronto's shelter-system metrics generally — restated here because it applies with equal, and arguably greater, force to a waitlist-allocation reform whose own operational status this document could not confirm.
