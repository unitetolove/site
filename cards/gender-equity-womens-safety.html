Gender Equity and Women's Safety — Playbook

How safe Toronto actually is for women and gender-diverse people, in transit, planning, safety, and who holds power.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can actually do on gender equity and women's safety — each move with its costs, its beneficiaries, and its receipts.

v2.0 · 2026-08-11

---

The honest bottom line

Toronto declared gender-based and intimate-partner violence an epidemic in 2023, joining more than 95 other Ontario municipalities — the province still hasn't followed suit; Bill 173, which would have forced the same declaration provincially, was sent to committee and had not passed as of November 2024. The tracking data behind the declaration is real and current, not this project's own: the Ontario Association of Interval and Transition Houses counted 16 confirmed femicides in the province in the twelve months ending May 2026, about flat year-over-year, and its own report notes that inquest-after-inquest recommendations keep repeating without confirmation they're being implemented.

Publish the Gender Equity Strategy's actual implementation status. (a recommendation card) Toronto's Gender Equity Unit has run since 2019, and the Strategy's first-term 2023–2026 action plan window is closing this year — but no public record confirms it was ever formally adopted by Council or says what it delivered [NEW-2026-GE-4]. That could mean a real record simply wasn't found this review, or a multi-year strategy running out its own clock without a public accounting; this card doesn't guess which. A single, dated status report is a near-zero-cost fix for a transparency gap this research ran into directly.

Fund Toronto's own invention again, aimed at who needs it most. (a recommendation card) In 1989, Toronto's METRAC invented the Women's Safety Audit — a participatory tool for flagging unsafe public space (bad lighting, no sightlines, nowhere to signal for help) — first applied to the TTC in 1994 and since adopted internationally. It sits mostly unused at scale today. Canada's most recent (October 2024) CEDAW review specifically found that refugee, asylum-seeking, and migrant women and girls face compounded discrimination and disproportionate gender-based-violence risk [NEW-2026-GE-6]; reviving the audit at citywide scale, with outreach and translation built around that finding, is a concrete, already-proven idea, not a new invention.

Neither move claims to fix gender-based violence on its own — the deepest levers here, justice and health, are provincial and federal, and this file says so plainly. Both are within the City's own existing authority, using tools it has already either built or funded once before.

a recommendation card — Publish the Gender Equity Strategy's Actual Implementation Status

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: gender-equity-womens-safety · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (anchored to this review's discovery gap, not the inherited master briefing doc alone)

Problem

The backgrounder's own live discovery could not confirm whether Toronto's Gender Equity Strategy — whose own first-term action plan was reported as scoped to run 2023-2026 — was ever formally adopted by Council or has a published implementation status [NEW-2026-GE-4]. With that first-term window closing in 2026, the City either has an implementation record this review's search-based discovery simply failed to locate, or a strategy whose own reported timeline is lapsing without a public account of what it delivered. This card addresses the transparency gap itself, not a guess at which of those two is true.

Action

Direct the Gender Equity Unit to publish a single, public, dated status report accounting for the Gender Equity Strategy's 2023-2026 first-term action plan — what was adopted, what was delivered, what gender-responsive-budgeting mechanism (if any) is currently operating — ahead of or alongside whatever comes next for the Strategy, so the public record matches what this page’s own research was unable to confirm existed.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: publishing a status report on the City's own existing unit and strategy is entirely within existing municipal authority and administrative capacity — no new authority is required.
  • City demands of Province: none identified as required for this specific action; not applicable.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified as required for this specific action; not applicable.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low, likely well under one million CAD — a reporting-transparency action within an existing unit's own mandate, not a new program. No City-stated figure for a status-report exercise was located this review; this range is this document's own extrapolation from comparable municipal reporting functions, flagged as such.

Funding path

Within the existing Gender Equity Unit's own operating budget; no new funding mechanism is required.

Who benefits, and how

Toronto residents and civil-society organizations (WomanACT, METRAC, and others named in the inherited backgrounder) who currently cannot verify from the public record what the Strategy has actually delivered; City Council itself, via a factual basis for deciding what comes after the current action-plan window closes.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via the Gender Equity Unit's existing operating budget; opportunity cost is negligible given the action's administrative scale.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified — the backgrounder's own Cui Bono table came up empty; this card does not manufacture one. If anything, the status quo (an unclear public record) primarily serves institutional inertia rather than any identifiable financial beneficiary.

Financial ROI

No source quantifies this — a transparency action of this scale has no plausible direct fiscal cost or saving beyond existing staff time; a low-stakes gap, not a manufactured figure.

Economic ROI

No source quantifies this, and no plausible mechanism exists for a broader economic effect from a status-reporting action alone.

Social ROI

Directional only: a clear public account of what a multi-year equity strategy has delivered is a precondition for this page’s other findings (the femicide-tracking gap, the CEDAW follow-up) being actionable — this card claims the reporting is a prerequisite for assessing the Strategy, not that reporting itself changes outcomes. Confidence: low-medium — sound logic, but the actual value depends entirely on what the (currently unknown) status report reveals.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely neutral — a reporting/transparency action carries no construction, land-use, or emissions mechanism. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-GE-4 · NEW · CBC News, Gender Equity Strategy 2023-2026 action-plan window — motivating discovery gap

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the mechanism (a status-report requirement is concrete, low-cost, and low-risk); low confidence on any quantified ROI, since the action's value depends on information not yet public. This card cannot verify whether the Gender Equity Strategy is already delivering well and simply under-reported, or genuinely stalled — the point is to make that distinguishable from the outside, not to presume either answer.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: whatever the City's own (currently unlocated) implementation record actually says; fairness and legal review is a lower priority here than for cards making fiscal claims, given the low-risk, transparency-only nature of the action.

a recommendation card — Fund METRAC's Women's Safety Audit at Renewed, Named Scale

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: gender-equity-womens-safety · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: CONTEXTUAL (combines the inherited METRAC/Vienna case with this review's CEDAW-sourced targeting finding)

Problem

Toronto invented the Women's Safety Audit (METRAC, 1989, first applied to the TTC in 1994), a participatory safe-design method since adopted internationally, per the inherited backgrounder. Meanwhile, Canada's most recent (2024) CEDAW review specifically flagged that "refugee, asylum-seeking and migrant women and girls face intersecting forms of discrimination and disproportionate levels of gender-based violence" [NEW-2026-GE-6] — a population this page’s own inherited document does not single out in its safety-audit discussion specifically. This card addresses funding the City's own tool at renewed scale, explicitly incorporating that current international finding into who the audits prioritize.

Action

Fund a renewed, citywide Women's Safety Audit program, administered in partnership with METRAC (the tool's own originating organization), with an explicit outreach and translation component targeting neighbourhoods with high concentrations of refugee, asylum-seeking, and newcomer women, directly responsive to the CEDAW Committee's own 2024 finding rather than a generic "all women" design exercise.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: commissioning and funding a safety-audit program on City-controlled public space (transit stops, parks, sightlines, lighting) is within existing municipal authority; Toronto has already done this once (the 1994 TTC audit).
  • City demands of Province: none for the audit mechanism itself — the deepest GBV response levers (justice, health) remain provincial per the inherited document's own framing, but this is a municipal design/planning tool, not a justice or health intervention.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified as required for this specific action; not applicable.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low millions CAD, anchored to the general scale of comparable municipal public-realm safety-audit and lighting/sightline improvement programs — not a specific City-stated figure for this exact program. This review did not locate a current, City-stated budget figure for a renewed audit program at citywide scale; this range is this document's own extrapolation, flagged as such.

Funding path

Most naturally layered onto existing Parks, Forestry and Recreation and Transportation Services capital/lighting-improvement budget lines, with the audit/outreach component funded through the Gender Equity Unit's own operating budget or a CPIP-style community-partnership grant to METRAC directly; no specific current City budget line was confirmed as already covering this exact program.

Who benefits, and how

Women and gender-diverse Torontonians using public space and transit generally, via the audit's design-improvement mechanism (lighting, sightlines, request-stop programs, per the inherited document's Vienna-comparator framing); refugee, asylum-seeking, and newcomer women specifically, via the outreach/translation component responsive to CEDAW's own 2024 finding [NEW-2026-GE-6].

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via the relevant capital and operating budget lines; opportunity cost is the marginal capacity not directed to other public-realm safety or capital priorities.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified — the backgrounder's own Cui Bono table came up empty; this card does not manufacture one.

Financial ROI

No source quantifies this — the inherited "GBV is expensive when unaddressed" cost-avoidance argument applies directionally, not quantified for this specific program. A live-discovery search this review did not locate a study quantifying fiscal offset from a municipal safety-audit program specifically, as distinct from broader public-realm lighting/safety capital spending.

Economic ROI

No source quantifies this. A future pass should look for research on public-realm safety-design economic effects (e.g., transit ridership or local-business-activity impacts of improved perceived safety) before inventing a figure.

Social ROI

Directional only: the inherited document's own citation of the Women's Safety Audit as an internationally-adopted model, paired with Vienna's gender-mainstreaming precedent, supports a plausible mechanism — safer-feeling public space increases women's access to jobs, education, and public life — but this card does not claim an independently quantified effect size for a renewed Toronto program specifically. Confidence: low-medium — a real, historically-grounded tool with plausible logic, not an independently evaluated renewed program.

Environmental ROI

Modest, likely small positive: improved lighting and public-realm design can carry minor co-benefits (e.g., LED upgrades over legacy fixtures), incidental to the action's safety purpose, not a primary environmental case. No source quantifies this specific incidental effect; a live-discovery search this review found general municipal lighting-upgrade sustainability practice but nothing modelling this program's specific marginal footprint. Confidence: low.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-GE-6 · NEW · UN CEDAW Committee meeting summary on Canada, October 2024 — migrant/refugee/undocumented women GBV concern, motivating the targeting design
  • carried-forward · this page’s carried-forward master briefing (gender equity womens safety) · METRAC Women's Safety Audit origin, Vienna gender-mainstreaming comparator, cited as-is

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the mechanism (reviving a City-invented, internationally-adopted tool is a concrete and precedented ask); low confidence on any quantified ROI dimension, since no independent outcome evaluation of a renewed citywide audit program was located in this review. The card takes a position — that CEDAW's 2024 finding should shape the audit's targeting — that the neutral backgrounder itself does not take; this resolution is stated explicitly here per template discipline, not smuggled into backgrounder prose. This card's action must be read alongside the community-safety and police-accountability pages' own warning that "safety" agendas can be misused toward surveillance or over-policing — this card is scoped to design/lighting/audit methodology, not enforcement, and should not be read as endorsing an enforcement-based safety mechanism.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: a current, City-stated budget figure for a renewed audit program at citywide scale; a directly-fetched primary-source evaluation of Vienna's own program outcomes; fairness and legal review given the card touches both GBV-sensitive framing and newcomer/migrant-specific targeting.
