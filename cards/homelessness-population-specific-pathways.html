Population-Specific Pathways Into and Out of Homelessness — Playbook

Youth, seniors, veterans, families — how the road into and out of homelessness actually differs by who you are.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can actually do for the populations its shelter system wasn't built around — each move with its costs, its beneficiaries, and its receipts.

v2.0 · 2026-08-11

---

The honest bottom line

Toronto's shelter system was, in effect, built around a default client — a single adult, typically male, entering the system for reasons the system already knows how to handle. Everyone who doesn't fit that default gets served by the same system anyway, and the evidence shows it, in measurable ways, for population after population. Standard Housing First, effective on average, shows no significant effect for ethno-racial groups in Toronto without cultural safety adaptations — the reason this whole leaf exists. Older adults are the pleasant surprise: Housing First actually works better for people 50 and up than for younger adults, a bigger gain in days stably housed and higher long-term retention; the gap there isn't program effectiveness, it's a five-year hole in dedicated long-term-care capacity before Toronto's George Street Revitalization project opens in 2029. The share of people released from Ontario jails into homelessness nearly doubled in five years — from 8.8% to 17.3% of all releases, even as the total number of people being released fell; at Toronto South Detention Centre specifically, it hit 23.4%. Most of these people were never convicted of anything — 80% of Ontario's provincial jail population is in remand, awaiting trial — and the mechanism is fixable: OW and ODSP payments get cut on admission, so income-support continuity breaks on top of housing-continuity breaking, and the person walks out with neither. A fix already exists and is already running, just not everywhere: Ontario piloted "Community Reintegration Planning Tables" at five jails, out of roughly two dozen, and even where they exist, community-agency participation isn't mandatory. 2SLGBTQ+ representation in Toronto's homeless population nearly doubled since 2021 — from roughly one in nine respondents to 22% of everyone counted in the 2024 Street Needs Assessment, and 31% among refugee claimants specifically — against a City shelter directory of 49 emergency locations, only two of them specific to this population. And veterans are overrepresented in homeless counts nationally at just under 5%, though even that figure remains unverified in this project's own claims register; three Canadian communities have hit "functional zero" veteran homelessness, Toronto isn't one of them, and nobody has confirmed whether Toronto is even tracking toward it, because there's no dedicated, published Toronto-specific veteran count.

Take the corrections-discharge-table model Ontario already built and proved, and cover the rest of the province with it. (a recommendation card) Five jails, including Toronto South Detention Centre, run Community Reintegration Planning Tables that build a release plan before someone walks out the door. The rest of Ontario's roughly two dozen provincial jails don't. This isn't a new program design — it's coverage and mandate: extend the same model everywhere, and make community-agency participation mandatory rather than optional.

Count Toronto's veterans experiencing homelessness, specifically and publicly. (a recommendation card) Using the same annual Street Needs Assessment the City already runs, add one identification question it may not currently ask, and publish the resulting count using the same functional-zero tracking methodology three other Canadian communities have already used to reach zero veteran homelessness.

Neither invents a new system. Both take something that already exists — a proven pilot, an annual survey — and close a gap in how far it reaches. What this doesn't settle: which population's gap is most urgent, since the evidence doesn't support that kind of ordering; whether a Toronto-specific hospital-discharge policy exists (a peer-reviewed source says plainly Ontario has none, provincially); and how to reconcile two different ways of counting 2SLGBTQ+ homelessness that use different bases and shouldn't be read as one continuous trend line.

a recommendation card — Expand Ontario's Community Reintegration Planning Tables Beyond Five Jails

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: homelessness-population-specific-pathways · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (backgrounder NEW-POP-12, NEW-POP-13)

Problem

Between 2016/17 and 2021/22, the proportion of Ontario correctional releases into homelessness rose from 8.8% to 17.3%, even as total discharges fell; at Toronto South Detention Centre specifically, the rate was 23.4% [NEW-POP-12]. A July 2025 John Howard Society of Ontario report found over 7,400 people released from Ontario's provincial jails with no fixed address in 2023-24 alone, and states the true number is "likely much higher" since housing status is recorded at admission, not release [NEW-POP-13]. Ontario has already piloted a working design — Community Reintegration Planning Tables, standing panels of support workers collaborating on individual release plans before discharge — but as of the most recent reporting located, only five of roughly two dozen Ontario jails have established one, including Toronto South Detention Centre, and participation by outside community agencies is inconsistent and not mandatory [NEW-POP-12]. This card addresses only the coverage and mandate gap in an already-proven design, not corrections policy generally.

Action

The Province expands Community Reintegration Planning Tables to all Ontario provincial correctional facilities, and makes participation by relevant community housing/homelessness-service agencies mandatory rather than optional, using the same design already piloted at Maplehurst, Toronto South Detention Centre, Elgin Middlesex Detention Centre, Thunder Bay Jail, and Thunder Bay Correctional Centre.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: Toronto-area community housing/homelessness agencies (funded in part through municipal channels) participate as mandatory table members at Toronto South Detention Centre and the Vanier Centre for Women, once mandated; the City can report on local effect via existing TSSS case-management data linkage where privacy-compliant.
  • City demands of Province: expand the Reintegration Planning Table model to all provincial correctional facilities and make community-agency participation mandatory, not discretionary.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified as directly required — Ontario correctional facilities are entirely provincial jurisdiction; federal recidivism-reduction research (cited in the same finding) is referenced as corroborating evidence, not as a jurisdictional lever.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: not costed in any source located this review for a full provincial expansion. Comparator: the existing five-jail pilot itself is the named anchor — this card asks for the same design's coverage to expand from 5 of ~24 facilities to all of them, not a new program design, so costs should scale roughly with facility count, though no per-facility cost figure was found to make that scaling precise. Confidence in this cost range is accordingly low.

Funding path

Provincial correctional-services operating budget, extending the existing Reintegration Planning Table budget line already funding the five-jail pilot; community-agency participation costs would need a corresponding funding commitment if participation becomes mandatory rather than voluntary, named here as a real cost this card does not itself size.

Who benefits, and how

People released from Ontario correctional facilities with no fixed address — the population directly named in both the Globe and Mail's FOI-based analysis and JHSO's 2025 report — via a pre-arranged discharge plan rather than release with no housing plan at all [NEW-POP-12, NEW-POP-13]. This population disproportionately includes Indigenous, Black, and 2SLGBTQIA+ people, per JHSO's own finding [NEW-POP-13]. The corrections system benefits secondarily via the documented link between post-release homelessness and the roughly 30% two-year reoffending rate this page’s substrate cites [NEW-POP-12].

Who bears the cost, and how

Provincial taxpayers, via the correctional-services operating budget; community housing/homelessness agencies, via the opportunity cost of mandatory table participation if not separately funded — a cost this card names but does not resolve, since no source confirms whether expanded participation would come with new agency funding or be an unfunded mandate.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — the backgrounder's own Cui Bono section states this page’s cross-population comparative scope did not examine procurement, contracting, or per-diem billing structures that might identify a beneficiary of the status quo, and no such finding was located in this review's discovery pass. This card carries no "who benefits from the status quo" line beyond this note.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Not yet estimable as a net fiscal figure — no source in this review costs a full provincial expansion or prices the avoided cost of post-release homelessness against the cost of expanded table participation; the existing five-jail pilot is the closest real comparator, but no source located this review discloses its own budget. Confidence: low.

(b) Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — no third-party economic-impact study of discharge-planning-table expansion specifically was located this review; flagged as a genuine gap. Confidence: low.

(c) Social ROI

Directional: this page’s substrate directly links post-release homelessness to a roughly 30% two-year reoffending rate nationally, with homelessness itself identified as a risk factor for reoffending [NEW-POP-12] — expanding a discharge-planning model already shown to be implementable (not merely proposed) addresses one specific, named mechanism in that cycle. No source quantifies the expected reduction in homelessness or reoffending from table expansion specifically. Confidence: low-medium — the mechanism is well-evidenced; the magnitude of this specific intervention's effect at full-provincial scale is not isolated in any source found.

(d) Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a discharge-planning/staffing-process expansion with no construction, land-use, or physical-infrastructure component; none needed to support that finding. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-POP-12 · source quote (this review) · release-into-homelessness trend (8.8%→17.3%), facility-specific rates, five-jail pilot coverage
  • NEW-POP-13 · source quote (this review) · 7,400+ no-fixed-address releases 2023-24, disproportionate impact on Indigenous/Black/2SLGBTQIA+ people

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence in the problem statement (directly evidenced across two independently sourced analyses). Low confidence in cost — no source prices a full provincial expansion. This card does not know whether mandatory community-agency participation would require new agency funding; that is named as an open question, not resolved.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: a provincial costed estimate for full expansion; confirmation of whether community-agency participation would be separately funded; fairness and legal review.

a recommendation card — A Public, Named Toronto-Specific Veteran-Homelessness Count

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: homelessness-population-specific-pathways · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: earlier research plus new load-bearing findings (CL-80297, CL-80300, backgrounder NEW-POP-8)

Problem

Veterans are overrepresented among people experiencing homelessness nationally at just under 5% of respondents in the 2020-2022 national Point-in-Time count cycle — itself still marked “still being checked”, not verified [CL-80297]. Three Canadian communities (London, St. Thomas-Elgin, Fort McMurray) have been confirmed by Built for Zero Canada as reaching "functional zero" veteran homelessness [CL-80300] — Toronto is not among them, and no source reviewed in this review confirms whether Toronto is even tracking toward that designation. This is a genuine, named gap: Toronto has no confirmed, dedicated veteran-homelessness count distinct from the general SNA total, despite dedicated federal ($106.8 million, 2023/24-2027/28) and provincial (Soldiers' Aid Commission) programming already existing for this specific population [NEW-POP-8, backgrounder "Veterans"].

Action

The City adds a veteran-status identification question to the Street Needs Assessment (if not already present) and publishes a standalone, named veteran-homelessness count and trend line, using the same Built for Zero Canada functional-zero tracking methodology already applied by London, St. Thomas-Elgin, and Fort McMurray.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: add or confirm a veteran-identification question in the SNA instrument; publish the resulting count using existing SNA reporting infrastructure — within existing municipal survey-design authority.
  • City demands of Province: none required for the count itself; the Soldiers' Aid Commission's existing $2,000/year housing-assistance program is named as an existing provincial support this count would help target more precisely, not a program this card asks the Province to change.
  • City demands of Feds: access to the Veteran Homelessness Program's (Housing, Infrastructure and Communities Canada) own data-sharing or coordination mechanisms, if any exist, to cross-validate a Toronto-specific count against federal veteran-support caseload data — named as a possible, not required, ask.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low, a survey-instrument addition and reporting-line addition to an already-existing, already-funded annual count — comparator: the SNA already collects and publishes comparably granular demographic breakdowns (2SLGBTQ+ status, refugee-claimant status) at no separately disclosed incremental cost, per this page’s own NEW-POP-10 finding, so a veteran-status question is anchored to that same existing-instrument cost profile rather than a new survey.

Funding path

Existing SNA/TSSS survey-administration operating budget — no new funding mechanism required, only an addition to an instrument the City already funds and runs annually.

Who benefits, and how

Veterans experiencing homelessness in Toronto, via a dedicated count that could support Toronto's own path toward Built for Zero Canada's functional-zero veteran designation, the same designation three other Ontario/Canadian communities have already achieved [CL-80300]. Federal and provincial veteran-support programs (the Veteran Homelessness Program, the Soldiers' Aid Commission), via better-targeted delivery once the local scale of need is actually known rather than inferred from a national percentage.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, negligibly, via the existing SNA administration budget; no new payer class identified.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — see Card a recommendation card's identical note above. This card carries no "who benefits from the status quo" line beyond this note.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Not directly estimable — a measurement/reporting addition, not a service-delivery change, and it does not itself generate savings or costs beyond the negligible marginal cost of one additional survey question; the SNA's own existing practice of tracking other demographic breakdowns at no separately disclosed incremental cost is the comparator. Confidence: low — a this library's internal records action with no direct fiscal-return case to size.

(b) Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — no source prices the economic value of veteran-specific homelessness measurement specifically. Confidence: low.

(c) Social ROI

Directional: a confirmed count is the precondition for Toronto pursuing the same functional-zero veteran designation three other Canadian communities have already achieved [CL-80300], and for accurately targeting the existing federal and provincial veteran-specific programs named above. No study quantifies the downstream benefit of measurement alone, as distinct from the services it might better target. Confidence: low-medium — the value of closing a named measurement gap is well-argued by analogy to the three precedent communities, but not quantified for Toronto specifically.

(d) Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a survey-instrument and reporting addition with no construction, land-use, or physical-infrastructure component; none needed to support that finding. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • CL-80297 · “still being checked” · national veteran overrepresentation (~5%) in PiT count
  • CL-80300 · “still being checked” · Built for Zero Canada functional-zero veteran communities (London, St. Thomas-Elgin, Fort McMurray)
  • NEW-POP-8 · source quote (this review) · federal Veteran Homelessness Program ($106.8M, 2023/24-2027/28)
  • NEW-POP-9 · source quote (this review) · Ontario Soldiers' Aid Commission (up to $2,000/year)

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence in the problem statement (the absence of a Toronto-specific veteran count is directly stated as a gap in the backgrounder, and the underlying national/precedent figures are cited, though at “still being checked” status in the claims register). Low confidence in all ROI dimensions except the directional Social ROI case — this is a measurement proposal, not a service change, and its value is instrumental to future decisions this card does not pre-empt.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming whether the SNA instrument already collects veteran status and simply doesn't report it separately (this review did not confirm either way); fairness and legal review.
