Public Space Costs of Homelessness — Playbook

Homelessness doesn't stay in the shelter system — what it actually costs the TTC, libraries, and parks to respond.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can actually do to make the true, dispersed cost of homelessness response visible — each move with its costs, its beneficiaries, and its receipts.

v2.0 · 2026-08-11

---

The honest bottom line

The TTC isn't a homelessness agency. It runs trains and buses. And in 2026, it's spending $31.7 million on homelessness and complex-needs response anyway — because the shelter system doesn't have room for everyone who needs it, and the subway does have somewhere warm to sit. This is real, funded infrastructure: Special Constables, Streets to Homes outreach workers, a mental-health crisis team, Toronto Police officers assigned system-wide, funded through a specific agreement with the City. Which is exactly the problem — every dollar of that $31.7 million comes from the Toronto-Ontario New Deal Agreement, the same deal that also funds $200 million a year of the actual shelter system's operating budget. Both funding streams end at close to the same time, March 31, 2027; if neither gets renewed, two different City divisions lose their homelessness-response money in the same window, not staggered, not independent risks. In 2023, TTC outreach workers made 7,844 visits to people on the transit system and successfully referred 517 of them to shelter. In 2024, visits went up to 9,179 — more contact, more effort — but successful referrals fell to 122. Three out of four fewer people got placed, even as outreach activity increased. Nobody has updated that number since. In October 2024, the TTC Board drew a real accountability line: buses would only be used as emergency winter shelter below -15°C, not as a general warming solution, and the Board directed that whatever it cost the TTC, the City should pay it back, not the transit budget silently absorbing it forever. Nobody has confirmed whether that repayment actually happened, or how much.

Make the TTC publish its referral-outcome number every year, not just when someone happens to ask. (a recommendation card) A $31.7 million program deserves a metric that gets updated — the TTC has already collected and published this figure twice before; this asks that it keep doing so on a recurring basis, so a funding increase can be judged against whether the outcome it's meant to support is improving, stable, or continuing to decline.

Confirm whether the October 2024 cost-recovery decision actually happened. (a recommendation card) A narrow, specific, answerable question this library's own research couldn't resolve, and the City can — whether the TTC Board's own cost-recovery direction was implemented, and for how much.

Both cards address measurement and transparency gaps, not the underlying capacity problem the referral collapse reflects, which belongs to shelter-system-capacity-strain's own cards. This file also doesn't repeat Toronto Public Library's own crisis-support program numbers or park-level encampment cleanup costs — both live in this library's own dedicated pages, so restating them here risks two documents drifting out of sync with each other.

a recommendation card — Restore and Publish the TTC Shelter-Referral Outcome Metric

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: homelessness-public-space-costs · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: earlier research plus new load-bearing findings (CL-827 + backgrounder NEW-2026-PSC-1, NEW-2026-PSC-3)

Problem

The most recent published TTC-specific shelter-referral outcome figure shows a collapse: 517 successful referrals from 7,844 site visits in 2023, falling to 122 referrals from 9,179 site visits in 2024 — visits up roughly 17%, referrals down roughly 76% [CL-827]. No updated 2025 or 2026 figure was located either by this page’s inherited T-0065 discovery pass or by this review's own independent live-discovery attempt, even as the TTC's own Community Safety, Security and Well-being Program funding rose to $31.7 million for 2026 [NEW-2026-PSC-1] and the same outreach programs continue actively operating [NEW-2026-PSC-3]. This card addresses only the measurement/transparency gap — not the underlying capacity problem the collapse reflects, which belongs to shelter-system-capacity-strain's own cards.

Action

The TTC, as a condition of continued Community Safety, Security and Well-being Program funding, publishes the same site-visit/referral-outcome metric on a recurring (at minimum annual) basis going forward, so a funding increase can be assessed against whether the outcome it's meant to support (successful shelter referrals from transit outreach) is improving, stable, or continuing to decline.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: City Council, as the body approving TTC's operating budget including this program line, can condition continued funding on this specific reporting requirement — within existing municipal budget-oversight authority.
  • City demands of Province: none identified as required — the funding is provincial, but the reporting requirement itself is a City/TTC governance matter, not something requiring provincial action.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified as required for this specific action.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low — a reporting requirement using data the TTC has already collected and published at least twice before (2023, 2024 figures both cited in this page’s inherited master briefing), not a new data-collection program.

Funding path

Existing TTC/City budget-reporting processes; no new funding mechanism required for the reporting itself.

Who benefits, and how

City Council and the public, via visibility into whether a $31.7 million annual program [NEW-2026-PSC-1] is producing improving or worsening outcomes on its own most direct, already-defined success metric; people experiencing homelessness on transit, indirectly, if visible worsening outcomes prompt a policy response rather than going unmeasured.

Who bears the cost, and how

TTC staff time for continued reporting; no new payer class, since the underlying data collection already occurs.

Financial ROI

Not separately estimated; this is a transparency/reporting action, not a spending program with its own fiscal offset — its value is informational, feeding future funding decisions rather than generating savings itself.

Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — a reporting requirement has no construction, employment, or induced-spending effect of its own, and none is needed to support that finding. Confidence: high on the neutrality of this specific action's own economic footprint.

Social ROI

Directional: visibility into a declining referral-outcome trend, if the trend continues, could prompt an earlier policy response than the current pattern of the metric simply going unpublished — the same logic underlying a recommendation card's combined-dashboard proposal in that page's recommendation cards, applied here to a different but structurally similar measurement gap.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a data-reporting exercise with no construction, land-use, or physical-infrastructure component; none needed to support that finding. Confidence: high — a reporting action has no physical footprint.

Evidence

  • CL-827 · carried-forward (unresolved in claims register) · 2023-2024 TTC site-visit/referral collapse
  • NEW-2026-PSC-1 · source quote (this review) · $31.7M 2026 TTC Community Safety Program funding
  • NEW-2026-PSC-3 · source quote (this review) · continued 2025-2026 outreach-program activity

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-high confidence. The measurement gap itself is directly documented across two independent discovery attempts (this page’s own inherited T-0065 pass and this review's own search); whether the TTC already tracks this metric internally without publishing it, versus genuinely not tracking it currently, was not confirmed in this review.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirmation with TTC directly on internal tracking status; fairness and legal review.

a recommendation card — Confirm and Report the Transport Bus Initiative Cost-Recovery Direction

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: homelessness-public-space-costs · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (backgrounder NEW-2026-PSC-4)

Problem

In October 2024, the TTC Board formally limited the use of transit buses as an informal winter shelter/warming solution and directed the TTC to recover associated costs from the City of Toronto [NEW-2026-PSC-4] — a concrete, dated institutional decision directly on-point for this page’s "de facto shelter" cost-shifting theme. No source located in this review confirms whether that cost-recovery direction was ever actually implemented, or at what dollar amount. This card addresses only that follow-through gap.

Action

City Council requests a report from the TTC confirming whether the October 2024 Transport Bus Initiative cost-recovery direction was implemented, and if so, the dollar amount recovered from the City to date — closing a specific, narrow accountability gap this page’s own live-discovery pass found.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: City Council can request this report directly from the TTC within existing municipal governance/oversight authority — no new authority required.
  • City demands of Province: none identified as required for this specific action.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified as required for this specific action.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: negligible — a status-confirmation report using an existing TTC Board decision as its reference point, not a new program or data-collection exercise.

Funding path

Existing TTC/City governance-reporting processes; no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

City Council and the public, via confirmation of whether a formally-directed cost-recovery mechanism (naming, explicitly, that emergency-bus-as-shelter use is a cost that should be recovered from the City rather than absorbed silently by the TTC) was actually followed through on — directly relevant to this page’s own "true cost of homelessness" accounting theme, since an undisclosed, unrecovered cost is exactly the kind of dispersed, invisible cost this page’s inherited master briefing (this library's prior synthesis Part 4) identifies as a systemic transparency gap.

Who bears the cost, and how

TTC/City staff time for the confirmation report; no new payer class identified for the report itself.

Financial ROI

Not separately estimated; this is a transparency/accountability action, not a spending program.

Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — a status-confirmation report has no construction, employment, or induced-spending effect of its own, and none is needed to support that finding. Confidence: high on the neutrality of this specific action's own economic footprint.

Social ROI

Directional: closing this specific accountability gap directly serves this page’s own broader theme — making visible, rather than leaving dispersed and invisible, the true public cost of homelessness response across City divisions.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a governance/reporting exercise with no physical-infrastructure component; none needed to support that finding. Confidence: high — a reporting action has no physical footprint.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-PSC-4 · source quote (this review) · October 2024 Transport Bus Initiative decision, cost-recovery direction

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence. The underlying decision is directly, verbatim documented; whether the cost-recovery direction was ever actually acted upon is a genuine open question this card exists specifically to resolve, not something this card can pre-answer.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: TTC/City confirmation; fairness and legal review.
