Gaming, Esports, and Strategy Games — Playbook

Should Toronto court the esports industry the way it courts film and tech — and what happened to the promised arena.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can build on in gaming and esports — and why a stalled half-billion-dollar arena still tangled up in coverage of the file needs a straight answer before anything new gets promised.

The honest bottom line

Toronto really is a video-game powerhouse. The city's flashiest esports project is not — and treating them as the same story is where this file usually goes wrong. Toronto's game development industry is real and big: more than 300 studios, an estimated 7,000 people working directly in the sector, going back to Ubisoft opening its Toronto studio in 2010 with a $263 million, ten-year boost from the Ontario government. This isn't a promotional exaggeration — it's directly confirmed from the source, and it's a genuine Canadian success story worth building on. That same source citing the industry's size is Toronto Global — the region's own investment-attraction agency, whose job is to make Toronto look good to companies deciding where to set up shop. That doesn't make its numbers wrong, and this doesn't dispute the 300-studio, 7,000-job figures. But a $5.5 billion national GDP claim and a "55 percent of Canada's indie game scene" claim, coming from the agency whose entire purpose is selling the region, deserve to be read as that agency's own pitch, not as an independently audited fact, until someone checks it against a neutral source. Here's the correction that matters most. If you've seen coverage of Toronto's esports ambitions, you've probably seen mention of a planned $500 million arena at Exhibition Place — a 7,000-to-10,000-seat venue meant to be the home stadium for Toronto's Overwatch and Call of Duty league teams. City Council fully approved it back in 2021. It was supposed to break ground in 2022 and open by 2025. It didn't happen. As of the most recent information found in this research, the project's status is unknown — stalled, most likely, tangled up with the broader Overwatch League's own commercial decline. Nobody has published a clear "this is cancelled" or "this is still happening" statement. That matters because a lot of writing about Toronto's esports future still talks about this arena in the present tense, as if it's coming soon. It isn't, as far as anything in this research shows. Any city strategy built on the assumption that this venue exists or is close to opening is planning around something that isn't actually there. This doesn't mean Toronto's esports ambitions are dead — the underlying game-development industry the arena was supposed to sit on top of is doing fine on its own. It doesn't prove the arena will never get built, either; "status unknown" is genuinely different from "cancelled." And it doesn't tell us who, if anyone, is on the hook for whatever land-use commitments or planning promises the City made back in 2021 — a real open question nobody has answered publicly. Neither move below bets big. Both are aimed at building on what's actually working — the game-development industry, library programming — instead of what merely sounds impressive on paper.

a recommendation card — Library-Based Strategy-Gaming Clubs, Scaled From Existing TPL Infrastructure (No New Capital Project)

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: egaming-esports-strategy · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: CONTEXTUAL (combines the inherited master briefing's strategy-gaming case with this page’s own corrective finding on large-capital-project risk)

Problem

The master briefing makes a case, honestly hedged, that strategy games may build executive function and self-mastery, and identifies libraries as the natural, already-existing municipal venue for this (public computers, existing gaming programs, digital-divide-bridging access). This review's own discovery of the OverActive Media esports arena's stalled status — a $500 million capital project approved in 2021 whose current status is unknown as of the most recent sources located — is a concrete, documented cautionary example of large esports-infrastructure capital projects carrying real execution risk. This card deliberately proposes the lower-risk, already-precedented alternative: expanding strategy-gaming programming at existing library branches rather than any new capital project.

Action

Expand strategy-gaming clubs and programs (chess, Age of Empires II-style RTS titles, and similar) at Toronto Public Library branches, using existing library computer/gaming infrastructure and program-delivery models already in place, prioritizing branches in amenity-gap neighbourhoods per the cross-referenced libraries-civic-commons page’s own civic-commons framing. No new venue, no new capital construction — an extension of existing library programming capacity.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: TPL is a City division (via its arm's-length Board); expanding an existing program category at existing branches is within TPL's own existing operational authority, requiring only a budget allocation, not new City Council authority beyond normal budget approval.
  • City demands of Province: none identified as required for this specific action.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified for this specific action.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low, anchored to the general scale of existing library program-expansion costs cited in the libraries-civic-commons page’s own backgrounder (e.g., TPL's 2026-funded "Reducing Seniors' Social Isolation" program-type initiatives, in the low-to-mid hundreds of thousands of dollars range per named program) — this card does not invent a new cost figure but anchors to the same order of magnitude as comparable, already-funded TPL program expansions.

Funding path

Existing TPL operating budget, following the same annual program-enhancement mechanism that already funds comparable initiatives (per the libraries-civic-commons page’s own backgrounder); no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

Youth and adults in amenity-gap neighbourhoods, via low-cost access to strategy-gaming programming and the plausible (per the master briefing's own honestly-hedged evidence) cognitive and self-mastery benefits; people who cannot participate in physical sport (disabled, socially anxious, geographically isolated residents), via an inclusive, non-physical competitive/community activity, per the master briefing's own connection/inclusion case.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via the existing TPL operating budget line; opportunity cost is the marginal TPL program budget not directed to other library programming.

Financial ROI

No independent financial-ROI study for library-based strategy-gaming programming specifically was located. This card does not assert a specific dollar-offset figure — flagged as an open estimate, not a sourced one.

Economic ROI

Not modeled — this is a small-scale program extension, not an economic-development action, and this card does not reuse the sector-wide $5.5B national GDP figure or the Toronto Global 300+ studio figure as a stand-in for this narrower library-programming action, since those figures describe the private game-development industry, not library-based public programming. Confidence: high, in the sense that no meaningful economic-development return is claimed — this card deliberately does not overstate a modest civic program as an economic-development lever.

Social ROI

Directional, anchored to the master briefing's own honestly-hedged cognitive-benefit case ("evidence suggests strategy games can build planning, attention, and cognitive flexibility," not a guarantee, per the master briefing's own citation of mixed RTS/StarCraft II findings) and its connection/inclusion case for people who cannot do physical sport. Not a quantified wellbeing-dollar figure. Confidence: low-medium — inherits the master briefing's own "promising but mixed" characterization rather than upgrading it.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — this action uses existing library computer/gaming infrastructure and does not involve new construction. Confidence: high — no plausible environmental mechanism attaches to a program-expansion action at existing facilities.

Evidence

  • carried-forward · this page’s carried-forward master briefing (egaming strategy) · strategy-game cognitive evidence (honestly hedged), connection/inclusion case, library-as-venue framing, cited as-is
  • NEW-2026-1 · NEW (cross-referenced within this page’s own backgrounder) · OverActive Media arena stalled status, cited as the cautionary contrast motivating this card's low-risk framing

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the mechanism (library-based gaming programming is already precedented and low-risk); low-to-medium confidence on the underlying cognitive-benefit claim, which the master briefing itself characterizes as promising but mixed, not proven. This card explicitly resolves the "should the City invest in gaming infrastructure at all" question toward the lower-risk, already-existing-infrastructure option rather than a new capital project, given this page’s own documented cautionary finding about the OverActive arena.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: a City-sourced figure on current TPL gaming-program participation rates or amenity-gap ward data, neither of which was located in this review.

a recommendation card — Public Accounting of the OverActive Media Arena's Current Status Before Any Future Esports-Infrastructure Commitment

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: egaming-esports-strategy · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (anchored directly to this page’s own corrective discovery finding)

Problem

The OverActive Media esports arena — a $500 million, 7,000-to-10,000-seat venue at Exhibition Place, fully approved by City Council in December 2021 — has an unknown current status as of the most recent sources located in this review, following the Overwatch League's commercial decline. No definitive, dated 2026 primary source (a City Council update or a corporate statement) was located confirming whether the project is cancelled, paused, or still notionally active. This card addresses the risk that this uncertainty persists indefinitely, with the City's own prior approval and any associated land-use or planning commitments left in limbo rather than formally resolved.

Action

Request a public status update from City staff (Economic Development and Culture, and/or Exhibition Place's governing board) on the OverActive Media arena approval's current standing — whether the 2021 approval remains active, has lapsed, or has been formally withdrawn — before any future esports-infrastructure proposal is evaluated, so that a new proposal is assessed on its own merits rather than in the shadow of an unresolved prior commitment.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: requesting a status update from City staff and Exhibition Place's board is within existing Council/Committee inquiry authority; no new authority required.
  • City demands of Province: none identified.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified.

Cost

Negligible — a staff-report request using information City staff and Exhibition Place's board already hold or can readily obtain, not a new program or study requiring significant new resources.

Funding path

Absorbed within existing City/Exhibition Place administrative capacity; no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

City Council and the public, via clarity on whether a major (previously $500 million) approved capital commitment remains live, informing any future decision about esports-infrastructure investment; Exhibition Place's own planning process, via resolved certainty about what land/site commitments, if any, remain encumbered by the 2021 approval.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, negligibly, via existing staff capacity; no meaningful new cost identified.

Financial ROI

Not applicable in a quantified sense — this is a transparency/status-clarification action, not a spending program. Indirectly protective: an unresolved, decade-old capital approval could carry latent financial or opportunity-cost exposure (e.g., site-use restrictions) that this card's own action would surface rather than leave hidden, though no specific dollar figure is asserted.

Economic ROI

Not applicable — no direct economic mechanism attaches to a status-clarification request. Confidence: high — no plausible economic mechanism, positive or negative, attaches to this specific action.

Social ROI

Not applicable in the conventional sense used elsewhere in this card set — this action is procedural/administrative rather than a program with direct wellbeing effects.

Environmental ROI

Not applicable. Confidence: high — no plausible environmental mechanism.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-1 · NEW · OverActive Media esports arena stalled/unknown status, cited via this page’s own backgrounder, cross-referencing multiple 2026-current secondary sources plus 2021-2022 original announcement reporting

Confidence & uncertainties

High confidence on the underlying problem (the status genuinely is unclear based on this review's discovery, which found no definitive 2026 primary-source resolution either way); this card does not claim to know the arena's actual current status, only that clarity does not currently exist and should be sought before further esports-infrastructure commitments are evaluated.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: nothing structurally; this is a low-cost, low-controversy transparency request whose main uncertainty is political prioritization, not technical feasibility.
