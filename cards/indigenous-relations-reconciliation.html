Toronto's Reconciliation Action Plan — Playbook

Toronto adopted 28 reconciliation actions from the Truth and Reconciliation Commission — how many are actually done.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto's own transparency and accountability mechanisms can do here — never a position on Indigenous priorities themselves, which this project does not speak for.

v2.0 · 2026-08-11

---

The honest bottom line

Honest limitation, stated up front per this library's Indigenous-sources provenance standard: this piece is written by a non-Indigenous-curated research project. It is not an Indigenous perspective, and no Indigenous reader has reviewed it. Where it states what an Indigenous person or organization says, it is quoting them directly, by name, in their own words — never speaking for them. Curation is not consultation. Both cards below recommend City delivery, funding, and transparency mechanisms only — they do not, and cannot, claim to speak for Indigenous priorities.

In 2022, Toronto's Council voted unanimously to spend the next ten years making 28 specific promises about how the city treats Indigenous people, land, and history — the Reconciliation Action Plan, 2022 to 2032, with 141 more specific sub-actions underneath. As of the City's most recent public accounting, November 2025: 103 of those 141 are started and moving, 3 are finished, 18 haven't been touched yet, and the City has put real money behind it — more than $120 million so far into Indigenous housing providers alone, $21 million into a Residential Schools memorial called the Spirit Garden, and tens of millions more spread across shelters, an Indigenous entrepreneurship centre, and crisis-response services. None of that is invented or rounded up generously — it's the City's own tally, in the City's own progress report. The same report also says, in its own words, that the plan "has not been Indigenous-led and important concerns remain," naming a "lack of a meaningful community advisory structure for validation, feedback, updates, or the development of required accountability measures," "limited co-creation," and that "some actions have been undertaken without the engagement of the Indigenous community." Both of those things are true at once, and this file doesn't pretend otherwise.

Give the plan the accountability structure the City itself says is missing. (a recommendation card) The City already runs a body with real teeth in a related area — the Toronto Indigenous Community Advisory Board has actual final say over a piece of homelessness policy, not just an advisory voice — and its own report says the RAP as a whole doesn't have anything like that. TASSC, which by the City's own description represents every Indigenous-led social service agency in the city and serves two-thirds of Toronto's Indigenous population, got a one-time $350,000 top-up in the same report, explicitly to address what the City calls TASSC's own "longstanding underfunding" — one grant, once, for a gap the City admits has been there a long time. This card proposes funding and establishing a standing accountability body at a comparable scale, not inventing a new funding mechanism, just extending what's already been tried once into something standing.

Publish a straight line from the eight national priorities to the actual work. (a recommendation card) Back in 2015, Toronto's Council picked eight of the national Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 94 Calls to Action as the city's own priorities. Nearly ten years later, the City tracks 141 detailed sub-actions but doesn't publish, in the document this review reviewed, a direct map showing which sub-actions are actually advancing which of those eight original priorities. This card asks the City to publish that crosswalk using data it already collects.

Neither card decides whether the RAP's underlying 28 actions are the right 28, or whether $120 million in housing investment is the right amount — those are judgments for the people the plan is actually about, not for a research file. Neither resolves whether Toronto's 73% activity rate represents real progress or an activity measure that doesn't capture what actually matters: Eva Jewell, who co-authored five straight years of independent national tracking of the TRC's 94 Calls to Action for the Yellowhead Institute, found only five fully completed nationally in five years of watching, and named a specific pattern she doesn't count as progress — "the rise of consultants offering Indigenous 101 workshops, the symbolic raising of Indigenous flags, orange donuts." "Started and in progress" and "fully completed" aren't the same yardstick, and this file isn't claiming Toronto is quietly beating the national record; the two pictures sit next to each other without a clean way to compare them yet. What both cards do is close a specific, nameable gap: right now there's no standing structure for Indigenous partners to formally check the City's work on this plan, and no public document showing whether the City's original eight stated priorities are actually the ones getting delivered.

a recommendation card — Fund the RAP's Own Named Accountability-Structure Gap — Recommendation Card

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: indigenous-relations-reconciliation · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (backgrounder NEW-2026-IR-1)

Problem

The City's own November 2025 progress report names, in its own words, a "lack of a meaningful community advisory structure for validation, feedback, updates, or the development of required accountability measures" as one of four specific challenges facing RAP implementation [NEW-2026-IR-1]. This is a City-identified institutional gap, not a claim this card makes on Indigenous organizations' behalf — the City's report also separately states that TICAB, in the distinct homelessness-governance domain, already exercises a materially stronger model (final decision-making authority over specific policy areas, per this page’s own cross-reference to homelessness-indigenous-overrepresentation), showing a stronger accountability structure is already precedented within Toronto's own institutions.

Action

The City funds and formally establishes a RAP-specific community advisory/accountability body with defined authority (at minimum, a formal review-and-feedback role on RAP progress reporting, modelled structurally on the binding-authority precedent TICAB already holds in the homelessness-Coordinated-Access domain, though this card does not assert TICAB's specific model should be copied wholesale without Indigenous partners' own design input) — addressing the gap the City's own report names, not a gap this card asserts independently.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: funds and formally establishes the body within existing municipal authority (the same authority already used to establish TICAB and the Aboriginal Affairs Advisory Committee).
  • City demands of Province: none identified as required — this is entirely within municipal governance authority.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified as required.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low tens of thousands to low hundreds of thousands CAD annually for a formal advisory body's operating/honoraria costs — comparator: the City's own $350,000 one-time capacity-funding allocation to TASSC in the same November 2025 report [NEW-2026-IR-1, ⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people)] is the closest available named anchor for the scale of capacity-funding gaps the City itself has already identified and funded once.

Funding path

Existing Social Development / Indigenous Affairs Office operating budget, at a scale comparable to the City's own recent $350,000 TASSC allocation [NEW-2026-IR-1, ⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people)]; this card does not invent a new funding mechanism.

Who benefits, and how

Indigenous community partners currently engaging with the RAP without, per the City's own report, "a meaningful community advisory structure for validation, feedback, updates" [NEW-2026-IR-1] — via a formal channel for that feedback to have documented, tracked effect; City divisions, via clearer accountability benchmarks than internal self-reporting alone provides.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, via existing Social Development operating budget, at a scale comparable to the City's own recent one-time capacity grants.

Financial ROI

Not separately estimated; this is a governance/accountability-structure cost, not a program with a direct fiscal-return case of its own.

Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — a governance-structure change has no first-order employment or induced-spending effect distinct from existing City administrative operations, and no comparator was identified. Confidence: low.

Social ROI

Directional: addresses a specific, City-self-identified gap in RAP accountability; whether it would satisfy Indigenous partners' own stated concerns about co-creation is not asserted by this card, since no direct Indigenous-authored citation locating TASSC's or TICAB's own specific proposed remedy for this gap was found in this review — flagged as a real limitation of this card's own confidence, not smoothed over.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a governance/advisory-structure change has no construction, land-use, or physical-infrastructure component. Confidence: high on the neutrality of this specific action.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-IR-1 · source quote (this review) · City's own named accountability-structure gap and TASSC capacity-funding precedent
  • homelessness-indigenous-overrepresentation (cross-reference) · TICAB's documented final-decision-making authority model

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the gap's existence (directly, plainly stated by the City itself); low-medium confidence on this specific remedy's adequacy, since this card does not have a direct Indigenous-authored source confirming this is the remedy Indigenous partners themselves would propose — this is stated explicitly as this card's own limitation, consistent with this page’s voice rule that a card may propose City delivery/transparency mechanisms but must not claim to know or speak for Indigenous priorities.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: fairness and legal review; direct outreach to or citation of TASSC's/TICAB's own stated position on what accountability structure they would want, which this review did not locate and does not substitute for.

a recommendation card — Publish the Eight Named TRC Calls to Action Against Their Actual Text, Annually, Alongside the Sub-Action Tracker — Recommendation Card

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: indigenous-relations-reconciliation · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (backgrounder NEW-2026-IR-1, NEW-2026-IR-2)

Problem

The City's own November 2025 report tracks 141 sub-actions in granular detail but does not, in the document this review reviewed, map that tracking directly and explicitly back to the eight specific, named TRC Calls to Action (23, 43, 57, 68, 77, 82, 88, 94) Council itself identified as Toronto's municipal priorities in 2015 [NEW-2026-IR-1, NEW-2026-IR-2]. This creates a transparency gap distinct from the sub-action tracker itself: a reader cannot currently see, in one place, which of the eight specifically-named national Calls each RAP theme or sub-action is actually advancing.

Action

The City publishes, alongside its existing sub-action progress table, a direct crosswalk mapping each of the eight named TRC Calls to Action to the specific RAP sub-actions intended to advance it and their current status — making the City's own 2015 prioritization decision and its 2022-2032 delivery plan checkable against each other in one place.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: produces the crosswalk using data the City already collects and publishes in its existing sub-action tracker, within existing Indigenous Affairs Office reporting capacity.
  • City demands of Province: none identified as required.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified as required.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low — an additional reporting/mapping exercise using already-collected data, comparator: the same existing sub-action tracking table structure the City already produces and publishes biennially [NEW-2026-IR-1].

Funding path

Existing Indigenous Affairs Office / progress-reporting operating capacity; no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

Council, the public, and Indigenous community partners seeking to assess whether Toronto's 2015 choice of eight priority Calls to Action is actually being delivered on, via a single, checkable document instead of needing to cross-reference the RAP's own text against the sub-action tracker manually.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, negligibly, via existing Indigenous Affairs Office reporting-staff time.

Financial ROI

Not applicable — a reporting/transparency action with no direct fiscal cost or saving.

Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — no comparator sought, since this action's value is transparency-instrumental, not economic in itself. Confidence: low.

Social ROI

Directional: closes a specific, named transparency gap between the City's 2015 Call-to-Action prioritization decision and its current, more granular sub-action tracking — not independently quantified.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a reporting/mapping exercise has no physical footprint. Confidence: high on the neutrality of this specific action.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-IR-1 · source quote (this review) · existing sub-action tracking structure and granularity
  • NEW-2026-IR-2 · source quote (this review) · the eight named TRC Calls to Action, per the 2015 Council decision
  • CL-0227 · verified (claims register, co-produced) · TRC's 94 Calls to Action, June 2, 2015

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-high confidence. The underlying data gap (no direct eight-Calls-to-141-sub-actions crosswalk found in the report this review reviewed) is based on a direct review of the November 2025 report's own text and appendix structure as fetched; it is possible the City's own Appendix 1 (not independently re-verified line-by-line in this review) already contains this mapping in a form this review's summary-level review did not fully capture — flagged as a real possibility rather than asserted as a confirmed gap.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: fairness and legal review; a direct line-by-line check of Appendix 1 of the November 2025 report (141 sub-actions) to confirm whether this crosswalk already exists in a form this review did not surface.
