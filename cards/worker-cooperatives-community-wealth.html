RC-WC-01 — A Social-Procurement Community-Benefit Weighting, Sequenced Before a Standalone Worker Co-op Development Fund

Card id: RC-WC-01 · File: research/cards/worker-cooperatives-community-wealth.md#rc-wc-01 · Status: DRAFT Issue slug: worker-cooperatives-community-wealth · Backgrounder: worker-cooperatives-community-wealth Trust class: INHERITED + NEW · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

The single most rigorously evaluated community-wealth-building result available anywhere in this leaf's evidence base — a 2025 peer-reviewed, difference-in-differences study of Preston, directly fetched this pass — found a statistically significant local employment effect (4%, 95% CI 2.4–5.7%) driven by anchor-institution procurement redirection and Living Wage accreditation, while the same study explicitly characterizes Preston's own worker-co-op development as "modest" to date [backgrounder, "Preston," directly fetched 2026-07-14]. This is a direct sequencing lesson: the procurement-and-wages lever has stronger evidence behind it than worker-co-op formation specifically. This card addresses only the procurement-weighting design, not a citywide co-op development strategy.

Action

The City adds a modest (~10–15%, per the master briefing's own suggested weighting) community-wealth-retention and democratic-ownership evaluation criterion to competitive RFPs issued under the City's existing Social Procurement Policy (2016), for contracts below international-trade-agreement thresholds (CFTA/WTO) that would otherwise bar preferential local/ownership weighting [the inherited master briefing's §Toronto-specific factors, "the honest gap for an Assembly"].

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: amending its own Social Procurement Policy's RFP evaluation criteria, for the City's own procurement and contracts below trade-agreement thresholds, is within existing municipal authority.
  • City demands of Province: advocacy to widen the latitude available to broader-public-sector bodies (hospitals, universities) bound by the Broader Public Sector Accountability Act and Procurement Directive, which currently constrains them from simply directing contracts to local co-ops [the inherited master briefing's §Toronto-specific factors].
  • City demands of Feds: none identified as directly required for the City's own procurement weighting; federal trade-agreement thresholds (CFTA/WTO) are a constraint the City operates within, not a change being demanded here.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: near-zero in new budget — this is a redirection of existing procurement spend via an evaluation-criteria change, not new program spending, per the master briefing's own explicit framing ("≈$0 in new budget, just redirected spend") [the inherited master briefing's §Toronto-specific factors]. Preston's own comparator shows the scale such redirection can reach: anchor-institution spend retained within Preston rose from roughly £38 million (5% of anchor spend, 2013) to £112 million (2017), per the primary study fetched this pass [Rose et al. 2025, "Discussion," directly fetched 2026-07-14].

Funding path

No new funding mechanism required — implemented as a policy/evaluation-criteria change within the existing Social Procurement Policy administrative structure.

Who benefits, and how

Worker- and community-owned suppliers competing for City contracts, via improved odds in competitive RFP evaluation; workers at those enterprises, via the employment-stability and pay-equity evidence the backgrounder documents for co-ops generally [backgrounder, "Worker co-ops survive as well or better than conventional firms"].

Who bears the cost, and how

No direct new taxpayer cost; incumbent suppliers not organized as worker- or community-owned enterprises bear a competitive disadvantage in RFP scoring relative to the status quo — a real, named tradeoff the master briefing itself flags (displacement of incumbent suppliers, some employing local workers under decent conditions) [the inherited master briefing's §The strongest case AGAINST, "counterfactuals and displacement"].

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Range: not yet estimable as a City fiscal figure — this is a redirection of existing spend, not new revenue or cost, so the "return" is distributional (which suppliers receive existing spend) rather than a net fiscal gain or loss to the City itself. Comparator source: Preston's own confirmed procurement-retention increase (£38M to £112M, 2013–2017) as an order-of-magnitude illustration of what redirected anchor spend can look like at comparable city scale, not a Toronto-specific projection [Rose et al. 2025, directly fetched 2026-07-14]. Confidence: low — Preston's figure is real and directly fetched, but Toronto's own total procurement base and realistically redirectable share remain unquantified, a gap the backgrounder itself names [backgrounder, "Open questions / data gaps"].

(b) Economic ROI

Range: not yet estimable as a Toronto-specific figure. Comparator source: Preston's own directly-fetched 4% employment-rate effect (95% CI 2.4–5.7%), with substantially larger effects for disabled residents (+16.4 to +22.1 percentage points) and minority ethnic groups (+6 percentage points) [Rose et al. 2025, Table 1, directly fetched 2026-07-14] — cited as the strongest comparator evidence in this leaf's entire base, while flagged explicitly that Preston's own worker-co-op component was "modest," so this effect should be attributed primarily to procurement-and-wages, not co-op formation specifically. Confidence: medium for the comparator's own internal validity (a peer-reviewed, controlled study) — low for its transferability to a Toronto-specific dollar or employment-rate projection, since no equivalent Toronto baseline or projection exists.

(c) Social ROI

The Preston study's subgroup findings are the strongest cited social-equity evidence available in this leaf: employment gains concentrated among people with disabilities, minority ethnic groups, and people with lower educational attainment — precisely the populations a community-wealth-building strategy aims to reach [Rose et al. 2025, Table 1, directly fetched 2026-07-14]. Comparator source: Rose et al. 2025, as above. Confidence: medium — the comparator study itself is rigorous, but its findings describe Preston's full CWB programme, not this card's narrower procurement-weighting action in isolation.

(d) Environmental ROI

Range: not yet estimable, and likely modest and indirect — shorter supply chains from local procurement redirection have a plausible, directionally positive transportation-emissions effect, but no source located this pass quantifies this for Preston or any comparator. Comparator source: none identified with a quantified figure. Confidence: low.

Evidence

  • the inherited master briefing's §Toronto-specific factors · INHERITED · Social Procurement Policy (2016), ~10–15% weighting proposal, trade-agreement threshold constraint
  • the inherited master briefing's §The strongest case AGAINST · INHERITED · incumbent-supplier displacement caution
  • Rose TC et al., "Understanding the differential effects on employment of a community wealth building programme in England" (2025) · NEW, directly fetched 2026-07-14 · 4% employment effect, subgroup effects, £38M→£112M procurement retention, "modest" worker-co-op development

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-high confidence this is a low-cost, evidence-backed first move, directly supported by the strongest single study in this leaf's evidence base. Low confidence in any Toronto-specific dollar or employment projection, since neither Toronto's redirectable procurement base nor a comparable controlled study exists yet. Explicitly flagged: Preston's own study attributes its effect primarily to procurement-and-wages, not worker-co-op formation — this card is honest that it is drawing on the strongest available evidence, which happens to support the procurement lever more directly than the worker-ownership lever this leaf is named for, and sequences accordingly (procurement first, dedicated co-op development fund as RC-WC-02, second).

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: quantifying Toronto's own redirectable procurement base, provincial advocacy outcome on Broader Public Sector Accountability Act latitude, Lane R pass.

RC-WC-02 — Seed a Small Co-op Development and Business-Succession Conversion Pilot, Modeled on Preston's Own Modest-Scale Approach

Card id: RC-WC-02 · File: research/cards/worker-cooperatives-community-wealth.md#rc-wc-02 · Status: DRAFT Issue slug: worker-cooperatives-community-wealth · Backgrounder: worker-cooperatives-community-wealth Trust class: INHERITED + NEW · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

Worker co-ops are rare despite favourable performance evidence because of a documented financing-and-founding-ecosystem gap — banks are wary of lending to businesses with no outside equity owner, and raising growth capital normally means selling the control that defines a co-op [backgrounder, "Worker co-ops survive as well or better than conventional firms"; the inherited master briefing's §The strongest case AGAINST]. Toronto faces a "silver tsunami" of retiring business owners, a named, underused, lower-risk path to co-op creation (workers buying a proven business, not starting from scratch), but this pass could not establish how many Toronto businesses are actually in this succession window or in which sectors [the inherited master briefing's §Toronto-specific factors; backgrounder, "Open questions / data gaps"]. This card addresses only a small, deliberately modest pilot — not a large-scale development fund, matching Preston's own confirmed "modest" approach to worker-co-op development specifically, rather than assuming Toronto should attempt something larger than the best-evidenced comparator itself achieved [backgrounder, "Preston," directly fetched 2026-07-14].

Action

The City seeds a small co-op development organization (technical/legal/governance assistance for conversions and startups) and a modest first-loss capital-guarantee arrangement with a partner credit union, explicitly scoped and sized to Preston's own "modest" worker-co-op development experience rather than to Mondragón- or Emilia-Romagna-scale ambition, paired with a business-succession outreach program targeting retiring owners specifically.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: seed funding for a development organization and outreach program is within existing municipal grant-making authority.
  • City demands of Province: modernization of the Co-operative Corporations Act beyond the 2019 partial reform (non-member-business threshold, audit rules; re-dated from "2025-2026," independently adjudicated 2026-07-17, ruling B9 — Ontario's own FSRAO regulator lists no CCA change after 2017 at all) — specifically, Québec-style co-op investment tax incentives and broader structural modernization matching the federal Canada Cooperatives Act [backgrounder, "Ontario's Co-operative Corporations Act," VERIFY; the inherited master briefing's §Policy recommendations, item 7].
  • City demands of Feds: none identified as specifically required for a City-seeded pilot at this scale.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low millions CAD for seed funding plus a ring-fenced first-loss guarantee (the master briefing's own illustrative figure: a ~$2M City backstop unlocking roughly $10–12M in commercial credit) [the inherited master briefing's §Toronto-specific factors]. Flagged: this figure is inherited from the master briefing's own illustrative modeling, not independently re-verified or re-costed this pass, and no Toronto-specific business-succession-wave sizing exists to confirm the appropriate scale.

Funding path

Seed funding from a ring-fenced source (the master briefing suggests a share of vacant-home-tax-style revenue, with the explicit caveat that the VHT itself is one ~$105M pot with multiple competing claims, not a dedicated stream for this program) [the inherited master briefing's §Policy recommendations, item 6], partnered with a credit union (e.g., Meridian, Alterna, per the master briefing's own named examples) for the capital-guarantee structure.

Who benefits, and how

Workers at businesses facing owner retirement or closure, via a lower-risk conversion path to ownership than starting a co-op from scratch; new co-op founders generally, via reduced financing and technical-assistance barriers.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, via the seed-funding and guarantee-backstop appropriation; opportunity cost of that funding versus other uses of the same ring-fenced revenue source, a tradeoff the master briefing itself names rather than assumes away [the inherited master briefing's §Policy recommendations, item 6].

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Range: not yet estimable as a net fiscal figure for the City; the master briefing's own illustrative $2M-backstop-unlocking-$10-12M-in-credit figure describes leveraged private credit access, not a City revenue or savings figure. Comparator source: master briefing's own illustrative capital-guarantee modeling [the inherited master briefing's §Toronto-specific factors] — inherited, not independently re-verified this pass. Confidence: low.

(b) Economic ROI

Range: not yet estimable. Comparator source: none identified specific to a Toronto-scale co-op development fund; Preston's own worker-co-op development was "modest" and the primary study fetched this pass does not isolate a co-op-specific economic effect from the broader CWB programme's effect [Rose et al. 2025, directly fetched 2026-07-14]. Confidence: low.

(c) Social ROI

Directionally supported by the general co-op performance evidence (stability, pay equity, job retention in downturns) [backgrounder, "Worker co-ops survive as well or better than conventional firms"], but not quantified for a Toronto-specific pilot of this scale. Comparator source: Mondragón's job-stability/pay-equity record, cited with its own complications (Fagor, overseas non-member labour) preserved rather than smoothed [backgrounder, "Mondragón"]. Confidence: low-medium — the directional case is well-evidenced generally; the Toronto-specific magnitude is not.

(d) Environmental ROI

Range: not yet estimable, and likely minor/indirect — worker co-ops as a legal/ownership form have no inherent environmental footprint distinct from the sector they operate in. Comparator source: none identified. Confidence: low — stated plainly as likely near-neutral on direct-footprint grounds rather than left unaddressed.

Evidence

  • the inherited master briefing's §The strongest case AGAINST · INHERITED · financing/founding-ecosystem gap
  • the inherited master briefing's §Toronto-specific factors · INHERITED · silver-tsunami succession opportunity, illustrative capital-guarantee figure, credit union partners
  • the inherited master briefing's §Policy recommendations, items 2, 3, 6, 7 · INHERITED · development ecosystem, succession-conversion program, ring-fenced funding caveat, legislative advocacy
  • Rose TC et al. 2025 · NEW, directly fetched 2026-07-14 · Preston's own worker-co-op development characterized as "modest," used here to size ambition realistically
  • backgrounder, "Ontario's Co-operative Corporations Act" · NEW, VERIFY · partial 2025-2026 reform, non-member-business threshold, housing co-ops excluded

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence that a small, Preston-scaled pilot (rather than an Emilia-Romagna- or Mondragón-scale ambition) is the fiscally and evidentially realistic next step. Low confidence in the specific cost range, which is inherited from the master briefing's own illustrative modeling rather than independently costed this pass, and in the succession-wave sizing, which remains genuinely unquantified for Toronto specifically. This card deliberately does not promise Mondragón- or Emilia-Romagna-scale outcomes, since the backgrounder's own international-context section states plainly that both are, by the evidence's own account, not realistically replicable at Toronto's starting scale.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: Toronto-specific business-succession-wave sizing (not available in sources found this pass), confirmed credit-union partnership terms, provincial legislative advocacy outcome, Lane R pass.
