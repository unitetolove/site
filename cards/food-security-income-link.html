Food Security as an Income Problem — Playbook

Food bank lines keep growing — the case that not having enough money, not enough food, is the real cause.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can do about a food-bank crisis its own research says isn't really a food problem — fund the school-food gap that's genuinely the City's to fund, and track the number that actually measures the underlying need.

The honest bottom line

Toronto's own food banks and its own research partners have already published the diagnosis. Toronto food banks recorded 4,124,313 visits in the year covered by Who's Hungry 2025 — a 340% increase since before the pandemic, and the highest number ever recorded. More than one in ten Torontonians now rely on food banks, up from one in twenty just three years ago. The shift isn't just volume: for the first time since 2020, most visits now come from people returning again and again rather than new clients showing up for the first time, and 112,554 people turned to a food bank for the first time this year alone. The research behind Toronto's own December 2024 emergency declaration says this isn't a food problem. The University of Toronto's PROOF program, the authoritative Canadian source on this question, published data in May 2025 showing 25.5% of people in the ten provinces lived in a food-insecure household in 2024 — the third consecutive annual record — and stated plainly: "all the research points to the need to provide low-income households with more adequate and secure incomes." Food insecurity concentrates hardest among Black households (46.7%), Indigenous people living off-reserve (39.9%), and children (nearly a third of all kids nationally). Income policy sits almost entirely with the federal and provincial governments. What the City controls directly is school food, and it's moving on it: a four-phase rollout to universal morning meals by the 2026/2027 school year and a strategic path to universal lunch by 2030. The City's own June 2025 report to Executive Committee puts a real number on what's left: $21.721 million to bring the next 155 unfunded school communities online, split between $7.492 million in capital and $13.454 million in annual operating cost. The honest number sitting next to that commitment: Ontario's own per-student contribution to school food hasn't moved since 2015. It's currently $17 a year — nine cents a day — the second-lowest of any province or territory in the country, behind only Saskatchewan. The federal National School Food Program covers less than 4% of elementary meal costs nationally. Toronto's own programs currently get government funding for only 37% of what they actually spend on food, and the City's own report says plainly that shortfall "forces many programs to reduce service days, close early, or offer smaller portions" — right now, before the expansion is even finished. For the school food investment: it's real, it's costed, and it delivers a direct, quantified relief effect to families right now — an estimated $129 to $378 a month in grocery savings depending on household size. This is squarely inside municipal authority and doesn't require permission from anyone else. For staying honest about what school food can't fix: food banks have expanded their capacity substantially — Daily Bread's own food-purchasing budget grew from $1.5 million before the pandemic to $29 million this year — and food-insecurity prevalence kept rising anyway, in the same years, nationally. That's exactly the pattern PROOF's own research says you'd expect if the underlying problem is income, not food supply or food-charity capacity. Toronto's own emergency declaration says this too: it explicitly calls on the federal and provincial governments to raise and index income supports, because the City knows the deepest fix isn't its own to make. Both of these are true at once: fund the school food gap because it's real, it's yours to fund, and it helps families today — and don't let doing that substitute for the income fight that's the actual cure, because the City's own cited research says charity and food-access programs, however well-funded, haven't moved the underlying number. Neither move below invents a new number or a new program. Both ask the City to act on the diagnosis its own emergency declaration already accepted.

a recommendation card — Fund the Student Nutrition Program's Phase 3/4 Funding Gap Directly Rather Than Letting Provincial Stagnation Set the Pace

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: food-security-income-link · Backgrounder: our research file for that page §"The City's own food-specific lever" · Trust: New load-bearing findings

This page carries no formally registered claims — every citation below is carried-forward, NEW (live-fetched this review), or a named comparator.

Problem

Toronto's Student Nutrition Program reaches roughly 243,000 students in 75-81% of public schools, but "155 school communities in Toronto remain without funding for a student nutrition program" as of the City's own June 2025 report, and expanding to them (Phases 3 and 4) is costed at $21.721 million [City of Toronto, "A Vision and Strategy for a Universal School Food Program in Toronto," June 3, 2025]. Ontario's per-student contribution "has not increased since 2015," currently "$17 per student annually ($0.09 per day)" — the second-lowest of any province or territory in Canada — and the federal National School Food Program covers "less than 4% of elementary and 2.5% of secondary meal costs" nationally. On current funding, Toronto's own programs receive government funding for only "37% of their total food costs on average," a shortfall that "forces many programs to reduce service days, close early, or offer smaller portions."

Action

Council directs staff to fully fund the identified $21.721 million Phase 3/4 capital and operating gap from municipal sources on the currently planned timeline (Winter 2026 / 2026-2027 school year), rather than making the timeline contingent on provincial or federal funding increases that have not materialized since 2015 provincially. This does not replace continued advocacy for provincial/federal funding increases (a separate, ongoing ask per the City's own report) — it removes municipal-budget uncertainty as a reason the 155 currently unfunded school communities' rollout could slip.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: the entire funding action for Phases 3 and 4 — the City's own report already treats municipal capital-from-current funding as the primary financing vehicle for the capital component ($7.492 million), with the operating component ($13.454 million) subject to the 2026 budget process.
  • City demands of Province: increase the $17/student/year base grant, unchanged since 2015, and index it to food-cost inflation going forward — the City's own report's Recommendation 1 and 2 already make this specific ask.
  • City demands of Feds: increase National School Food Program funding to Ontario specifically, and prioritize "dedicated support for infrastructure" — the City's own report's Recommendation 3 already makes this specific ask.

Cost

Order of magnitude: $21.721 million one-time/near-term (Phases 3-4 combined: $7.492M capital, $0.775M one-time start-up, $13.454M annual operating), rising to a projected $20-60 million in ongoing annual cost once a universal morning meal program is fully implemented across all Toronto public schools — both figures directly cited from the City's own June 2025 report, not an external estimate.

Funding path

Capital: City Building Fund / Capital from Current, as already structured in the City's own 2025 Capital Budget amendment for this purpose. Operating: City's own 2026 Operating Budget process, per the report's own stated mechanism — this card asks that the operating gap be resolved through this existing municipal channel rather than left contingent on provincial/federal increases that have a nine-year track record (2015-2024) of not materializing at the provincial level specifically.

Who benefits, and how

Students in the 155 currently unfunded Toronto school communities, via direct meal access; families of participating students broadly, via the City's own estimated household savings of "$129 to $189 per child each month... with potential annual savings of $2,580 to $3,780 for families with two children" — a direct income-relief effect distinct from, but complementary to, the income-adequacy policy response the backgrounder's own broader diagnosis points toward.

Who bears the cost, and how

Toronto taxpayers city-wide, via the capital and operating budget allocations described above — a real, disclosed municipal cost the City is choosing to bear at a faster pace than provincial/federal funding alone would support, distinct from and larger than what senior governments are currently contributing per student.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified — the backgrounder's own Cui Bono section found 0 ESTABLISHED/REPORTED entities profiting from Toronto's food-insecurity emergency specifically, and flags a LEAD-grade (not yet registrable) grocery-sector-concentration thread as a distinct, unrelated angle not applicable to this specific card's Action.

Financial ROI

Not independently modeled for cost-avoidance/downstream-savings terms — the City's own report frames this as a direct household-savings program ($129-$378/month per family depending on number of children) rather than a City fiscal-return program. No source estimates offsetting savings to the City's own budget from expanded school food access specifically. Confidence: low for a City-fiscal-return claim (not attempted by the source), high for the household-savings figure itself (directly disclosed in the City's own report).

Economic ROI

Not directly modeled for Toronto specifically. A national economic-rationale study cited in the City's own report — Ruetz & Edwards (2024), "The Economic Rationale for Investing in School Meal Programs for Canada" — found the federal National School Food Program is "expected to... create up to 207,700 jobs over 10 years" nationally. This is a national, not Toronto-specific, jobs estimate and should not be read as a Toronto-scale forecast. Confidence: low — the comparator is real and named but national in scope; no Toronto-specific share of that jobs estimate is available.

Social ROI

Directional: the City's own report cites a Toronto District School Board evaluation finding "improvements in student attendance, behaviour or attitude, and reduced incidence of disciplinary problems as a result of the program" (a 2012 TDSB evaluation). Not independently quantified in dollar or standardized-effect-size terms. Confidence: medium — the underlying evaluation is named and City-cited, though its own methodology was not independently reviewed in this review.

Environmental ROI

Modest; the City's own report notes an aspiration to align school food procurement with its Coolfood Pledge commitments and explore "opportunities to assess and reduce GHG emissions in partnership with relevant City divisions" through centralized purchasing — a stated goal, not yet a quantified outcome. Confidence: low — real and named, but explicitly forward-looking/unquantified in the source.

Evidence

  • NEW, live-fetched 2026-07-14 · City of Toronto, "A Vision and Strategy for a Universal School Food Program in Toronto" (June 3, 2025) · program scale, funding phases and costs, provincial/federal funding-gap figures, household savings, TDSB evaluation, Ruetz & Edwards jobs estimate
  • our research file for that page · this page’s own backgrounder, synthesizing the above

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-high confidence on feasibility (the City has already structured a capital funding mechanism and costed the operating gap precisely) and low-medium confidence on whether municipal-only funding, absent the provincial/federal increases separately demanded, is fiscally sustainable long-term — the City's own report frames senior-government funding increases as necessary to the program's durability, not merely accelerative, a tension this card's Action does not fully resolve.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: 2026 Operating Budget process outcome for the operating-cost component; no confirmation in the evidence base that Council has already committed to fully municipal-funding the gap independent of provincial/federal response.

a recommendation card — Track the Food-Insecurity Rate, Not Just Food-Bank Visit Counts, as the City's Own Emergency-Response Metric

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: food-security-income-link · Backgrounder: our research file for that page §"What the evidence does and doesn't support"; §"Key tensions / tradeoffs" · Trust: New load-bearing findings

Problem

The most visible, most frequently cited metric in Toronto's food-insecurity response is food-bank visit volume (4,124,313 visits, per Who's Hungry 2025) — a metric that, per PROOF's own research, can rise even as underlying food-insecurity prevalence changes for reasons distinct from food-charity capacity (charity expansion, awareness, or eligibility shifts), and per the backgrounder's own "Key tensions" section, food-bank visit growth and national food-insecurity prevalence have moved together over the same recent period without a clear resolution of which is driving which at the local level. Visit-count metrics also structurally undercount the food-insecure population who do not use food banks at all — the inherited master briefing's "3-7 times more food-insecure people than food-bank users" figure, though not independently re-verified this review, points at the same undercount risk.

Action

Council directs the City's Poverty Reduction Strategy office to adopt and publicly report Toronto-specific household food-insecurity prevalence (via Statistics Canada's Canadian Income Survey / Household Food Security Survey Module, disaggregated to the Toronto CD/CSD level where StatsCan methodology permits) as the primary tracked metric for the food-insecurity emergency declaration's own progress reporting, alongside — not instead of — food-bank visit counts.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: commission or compile the metric from existing StatsCan public data releases (the same source PROOF's own national figures draw from) and report it alongside the existing Poverty Reduction Strategy indicators — an analytical/reporting task within existing City capacity.
  • City demands of Province: none identified — this is a City reporting-practice change, not a request requiring provincial action.
  • City demands of Feds: none directly, though the City may need to request Toronto-specific data-table releases from Statistics Canada if not already publicly disaggregated at the municipal level — ⚠️ still being checked, this review did not confirm whether Toronto-CD-level food-insecurity prevalence is currently available in StatsCan's public tables or would require a special request.

Cost

Order of magnitude: low — an analytical/reporting task using existing public StatsCan data tables (the same tables PROOF itself uses), not a new survey or data-collection program. No source estimates this specific reporting cost; treated here as incremental staff analysis, consistent with the low-cost precedent set by a comparable transparency-reporting recommendation in this project's sibling leaf climate-adaptation-stormwater-flooding (a recommendation card of that leaf).

Funding path

Existing Poverty Reduction Strategy office operating budget; no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

Council and the public, via an emergency-response metric less susceptible to the specific ambiguity this backgrounder's own evidence base flags — whether rising visit counts reflect worsening prevalence, expanding charity capacity, or both, is not resolvable from visit-count data alone. Policymakers deciding between food-access spending (e.g., a recommendation card above) and income-adequacy advocacy, via a metric that tracks the actual diagnosis (income inadequacy) rather than a proxy (charity utilization) that both the inherited master briefing and this review's own live discovery treat as an imperfect measure of the underlying problem.

Who bears the cost, and how

No new payer — analytical/reporting task on data the City's Poverty Reduction Strategy office can access through existing public StatsCan releases.

Financial ROI

Not applicable in the direct-yield sense — this is a reporting-practice change, not a spending or revenue action.

Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — this is a reporting mechanism, not a program with its own economic footprint. Confidence: low — no plausible economic-impact mechanism attached to a reporting-practice change of this kind.

Social ROI

Directional: better-diagnosed emergency-response metrics support more accurately targeted policy responses (income-adequacy advocacy versus food-access spending), per this backgrounder's own "Key tensions" finding that the two are currently tracked through an imperfect proxy. Not independently quantified.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a data-reporting change with no plausible emissions, land-use, water, or waste mechanism. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW, live-fetched 2026-07-14 · Daily Bread Food Bank, "4.1 Million Food Bank Visits Reveal Record Depth of Poverty in Toronto" (Oct. 27, 2025) · visit-count metric currently in use
  • NEW, live-fetched 2026-07-14 · PROOF, "New data on household food insecurity in 2024" (May 5, 2025) · the prevalence metric this card asks the City to adopt alongside visit counts
  • our research file for that page, "What the evidence does and doesn't support" and "Key tensions / tradeoffs" · the metric-ambiguity gap this card responds to directly

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-high confidence on feasibility (StatsCan data already exists and PROOF already uses it at the national/provincial level) and low confidence on whether Toronto-CD-level disaggregation is readily available without a special data request — flagged ⚠️ still being checked above and not resolved in this review.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming whether Toronto-specific (CD-level) food-insecurity prevalence is available in StatsCan's existing public tables or requires a custom data request.
