a recommendation card — Complete the Comprehensive Development Charges Review with a published stability commitment — Recommendation Card

Card id: a recommendation card · Status: DRAFT · Backgrounder: our research file for that page §"What development charges are and Ontario's repeated legislative amendments to them" Provenance: New load-bearing findings · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

Toronto's DC framework has changed at both the municipal and provincial level at least five distinct times since 2022 (Bill 23, 2022; the City's 2022 DC Background Study; Bill 185, 2024; the City's June 2025 rate update and EX24.2/MM32.5 Council changes; Bill 17, 2025) [backgrounder §"What development charges are and Ontario's repeated legislative amendments to them"]. Even AMO, the body most directly tracking DC-revenue impact on municipalities, revised its own Bill 23 cost estimate substantially after initial passage (from a preliminary $5.1 billion over nine years to approximately $1 billion annually) — evidence that policy volatility itself, independent of which direction any single change moves DC revenue, is a real planning cost for both the City and developers pricing multi-year projects. The City's own Comprehensive Development Charges Review is "in the early stages... expected to continue into 2027," with no published interim milestones.

Action

Council directs staff to publish a public timeline with named interim milestones for the Comprehensive Development Charges Review (currently open-ended through 2027), including a commitment to a minimum notice period before any further rate or bylaw structural change outside that Review's own conclusion.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: the entire action — the Review itself, its scheduling, and any stability/notice commitment around it are within Council's own existing DC-bylaw authority.
  • City demands of Province: none directly for this specific action, though the backgrounder notes the province has itself amended the DC Act three times in three years (Bill 23, 185, 17) — a City-side stability commitment cannot control the province's own legislative pace, and this card does not pretend otherwise.
  • City demands of Feds: none.

Cost

Order of magnitude: negligible, non-capital — a scheduling and communications commitment, not a new program. Named comparator: the same order of cost as the City's own routine DC Background Study and bylaw-update process it already performs on a multi-year cycle.

Funding path

Existing City Finance/Development Charges division operating budget; no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

Developers and builders planning multi-year projects, via reduced regulatory-uncertainty cost when pricing projects against a known DC trajectory rather than three unplanned legislative changes in three years. The City's own budget office, via more predictable DC-reserve revenue forecasting.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, via the negligible administrative cost of the commitment itself. No other named cost-bearer — this is a low-cost transparency/predictability action, not a rate change in either direction.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table (empty, per the honest explanation given there — this is characterized as a policy-design and intergovernmental-coordination problem, not a case with an identified financial beneficiary, though the backgrounder flags development-industry lobbying activity around the Bill 23/185/17 sequence as an unexplored lead for a future Accountability Observatory pass).

Financial ROI

Not separately quantified — no source in this page’s evidence base models a specific dollar value for reduced regulatory uncertainty. The qualitative case (reduced planning-cost volatility for both the City and developers) is real but not converted into a range here, stated plainly rather than invented.

Economic ROI

Range: Not yet estimable — no source models a specific economic effect of DC-policy predictability itself, as distinct from DC rate levels. Comparator source: None identified this review specific to policy-predictability effects; the backgrounder's own BC-vs-Ontario C.D. Howe comparison addresses capital-spend timing, not legislative predictability, and is not directly transferable to this narrower claim. Confidence: Low — genuine gap.

Social ROI

Directional only: to the extent regulatory predictability supports more consistent housing-construction planning, it indirectly supports Ontario's stated (though currently off-track, per the backgrounder) 1.5-million-homes goal. No source quantifies this specific card's own contribution to that goal, and this card does not claim one.

Environmental ROI

Range: Genuinely neutral — a scheduling/communications commitment does not itself change construction volume, land use, or emissions. Any environmental effect flows from whatever development activity DC policy eventually supports, not from this card's own action. Comparator source: None needed for a genuinely neutral action, per the template's allowance. Confidence: High for "the action itself is neutral."

Evidence

  • NEW, live-fetched 2026-07-14 · City of Toronto, "Development Charges" and "Bylaws, Rates & Study" pages · Comprehensive DC Review status, 2025 Council/provincial change timeline
  • NEW, live-fetched 2026-07-14 · Environmental Registry of Ontario, notice 019-8371 · Bill 185's specific reversal pattern, evidence of provincial-level volatility
  • carried-forward this library's inherited source document (Ontario's 1.5-million-homes target and development charges) · AMO's revised Bill 23 cost estimate as volatility evidence

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on feasibility (a scheduling/communications commitment is low-complexity and within clear existing authority). Low confidence on quantified ROI in any of the three modelled dimensions — this is honestly a predictability/governance-quality card, not a program with a modelled financial or economic return, and it is presented as such rather than forced into a false-precision range.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: nothing structural. Would benefit from direct confirmation with the City's Development Charges division of the Comprehensive Review's actual current internal milestones, if any exist informally but are not yet public.

a recommendation card — Track and publicly report Bill 17's actual local effect on Toronto's DC revenue — Recommendation Card

Card id: a recommendation card · Status: DRAFT · Backgrounder: our research file for that page §"What development charges are and Ontario's repeated legislative amendments to them" / "Open questions / data gaps" Provenance: New load-bearing findings · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

Bill 17 (2025) further amended the Development Charges Act, with provisions coming into force on two separate 2025 dates (June 5 and November 3) — but this page’s own evidence base, including its inherited master briefing, does not yet characterize Bill 17's specific provisions or their dollar effect on Toronto's DC revenue, unlike the well-documented effects of Bill 23 (2022) and Bill 185 (2024) [backgrounder §"Open questions / data gaps"]. This is a genuine, named information gap, not a settled fact being ignored.

Action

Council directs City Finance staff to publish a plain-language explainer of Bill 17's specific provisions and a dollar estimate of its net effect on Toronto's DC revenue, using the same methodology AMO used to revise its own Bill 23 estimate, within the City's next DC-related budget or Review reporting cycle.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: the entire action — this is an internal analysis and public-reporting function within existing City Finance capacity.
  • City demands of Province: none directly, though the City could reasonably request the Ministry of Municipal Affairs and Housing's own impact analysis (if one exists) as an input, per the same transparency standard the ERO decision-notice process itself models for provincial consultations.
  • City demands of Feds: none.

Cost

Order of magnitude: negligible, non-capital — an analysis and reporting task using existing City Finance staff capacity, on the same order as the City's routine DC bylaw and rate-schedule reporting.

Funding path

Existing City Finance operating budget; no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

City Council and residents, via a clear, City-sourced account of a legislative change that directly affects municipal infrastructure funding capacity but currently lacks any public local-impact estimate. Future policy analysis (including this project's own backgrounder) that currently lacks this data point.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, via the negligible staff-time cost. No other named cost-bearer.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table (empty).

Financial ROI

Not applicable in the traditional sense — this is an information/transparency action, not a program with a fiscal return. The action's value is in enabling better-informed future fiscal decisions, not in itself generating revenue or savings.

Economic ROI

Range: Not yet estimable — an information-transparency action has no direct economic effect of its own. Comparator source: None needed; genuinely thin category for this action type. Confidence: Low, and a poor fit for this action type rather than a real gap in the evidence.

Social ROI

Not applicable / genuinely neutral — a reporting action with no direct wellbeing or cohesion effect.

Environmental ROI

Range: Genuinely neutral — an analysis/reporting action does not itself change construction volume or land use. Comparator source: None needed, per the template's allowance for a genuinely neutral action. Confidence: High for "the action itself is neutral."

Evidence

  • NEW, live-fetched 2026-07-14 · City of Toronto, "Development Charges" page · Bill 17 in-force dates (only fact currently available about Bill 17 in this page’s evidence base)
  • carried-forward this library's inherited source document (Ontario's 1.5-million-homes target and development charges) · AMO's Bill 23 revision as the methodological comparator for how such an estimate could be produced

Confidence & uncertainties

High confidence on feasibility (low-complexity, within existing authority, analogous to work the City and AMO have both already done for prior DC-Act amendments). This card exists specifically because of a confirmed evidence gap (Bill 17's effects are uncharacterized in this page as of this review) — its own justification is the gap itself, stated plainly rather than inflated.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: nothing structural. This card and a recommendation card are complementary, not sequential — either could proceed independently.
