Most Educated Population / Learning City — Playbook

Could nudges and better defaults turn Toronto into a genuine lifelong-learning city — what actually moves the needle.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can actually do to become a genuine learning city — each move with its costs, its beneficiaries, and its receipts.

v2.0 · 2026-08-11

---

The honest bottom line

Toronto likes to describe itself as the kind of place that could be the world's most educated city, and the national numbers back a version of that story: 64% of Canadian adults aged 25–64 now hold a college or university credential, up from 55% a decade ago, well clear of the 41% average across wealthy OECD countries, with Canada's college system alone (25% of adults) more than tripling the OECD average and women (71%) pulling further ahead of men (57%) over the past decade. None of that is Toronto-specific, so it corroborates the general story without proving anything about this city in particular. What is Toronto-specific and directly checkable is UNESCO's Global Network of Learning Cities — 356 cities in 79 countries working on lifelong learning — and Toronto is not a member. Edmonton is, since 2022, using its own public library and a cross-institutional advisory committee, without a large new budget line. And a cautionary number for anyone tempted to think funding alone fixes this: Singapore's SkillsFuture, the most generous individual learning-credit program of any comparable country, non-expiring free money given to every citizen 25 and older, has been used by only about half of eligible Singaporeans — ever, not annually. Money was never the real barrier; time, exhaustion, and the rest of life are.

Apply to join UNESCO's Global Network of Learning Cities. (a recommendation card) Edmonton's 2022 accession shows this doesn't require a new program: it requires naming a focal point, pulling together what already exists (library, community programs, university partnerships), and submitting the case. This card doesn't invent new spending — it asks the City to formalize and internationally benchmark work already happening.

Build one place to actually find these programs. (a recommendation card) Toronto Public Library's own Adult Literacy Program — checked directly for this file — requires English fluency and an in-person, 1.5–2 hour assessment before anyone learns whether they qualify, with waitlisting or referral elsewhere a likely outcome. A single, plain-language, multilingual page laying out who qualifies for what — before anyone has to show up — is a small, cheap fix aimed at exactly that friction.

Neither card touches the deeper problem this page inherited from its own briefing: Toronto's real issue isn't too little education, it's credentials that go unused — the trained professional stuck in work that doesn't need their training. That's the separate newcomer-success-credential-recognition page’s job, and formal education funding, learning-account design, and credential licensing remain provincial and federal matters outside what either card below proposes.

a recommendation card — Pursue UNESCO Global Network of Learning Cities Membership, Following Edmonton's Precedent

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: most-educated-population-learning-city · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (backgrounder NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2)

Problem

Toronto is not a member of UNESCO's Global Network of Learning Cities (GNLC), an active 356-city, 79-country peer network for lifelong-learning policy exchange [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2]. Edmonton, a substantially smaller Canadian city, joined in 2022 and uses membership to convene a cross-institutional Senior Advisory Committee and a public-library-led "City of Learners" initiative [NEW-2026-2]. This card addresses only the absence of formal peer-network membership and the missed access to GNLC's knowledge-exchange, clustering, and award mechanisms — not a new learning-city program from scratch, since much of what membership would formalize (a library-led learning ecosystem, community programs) already exists in Toronto (carried-forward).

Action

The City directs the appropriate body (most plausibly a joint submission involving the City Manager's Office, Toronto Public Library, and Economic Development & Culture, mirroring Edmonton's own cross-institutional model) to prepare and submit a GNLC membership application through UNESCO's Institute for Lifelong Learning, naming a formal focal point and documenting Toronto's existing learning-ecosystem assets — TPL's scale and 2026 hours expansion, existing community and settlement-sector programming, and university/college partnerships — as the application's evidentiary base.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: prepare and submit the GNLC membership application; designate a focal point; participate in GNLC clusters and the biennial International Conference on Learning Cities once admitted — within existing municipal authority to represent the City internationally and convene existing City-affiliated institutions, mirroring Edmonton's own municipally-led process.
  • City demands of Province: none required for membership itself; a request that the Province recognize any GNLC-derived learning-city strategy in future provincial workforce-development funding decisions is a reasonable adjacent ask, not one this card asserts has already been made.
  • City demands of Feds: none — UNESCO membership operates through Canada's own UNESCO channels (the Canadian Commission for UNESCO), not a federal funding relationship.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low — staff time for application preparation, focal-point designation, and ongoing GNLC participation (conferences, cluster meetings). Comparator: Edmonton, roughly one-third of Toronto's population, has sustained GNLC membership through an existing Senior Advisory Committee structure with no dedicated new funding line documented on UNESCO's own member-profile page [NEW-2026-2]; no specific Edmonton budget figure was located this review, a gap stated rather than invented.

Funding path

Existing City Manager's Office / Toronto Public Library / Economic Development & Culture operating budgets, using existing international-relations and library-strategy staff capacity; no new funding source identified as necessary for the application and focal-point stages.

Who benefits, and how

Toronto's existing learning-ecosystem institutions (TPL, community programs, settlement-sector partners), via access to GNLC's peer-exchange knowledge base, clustering with comparable cities, and the UNESCO Learning City Award as a recognition mechanism that could support future advocacy for provincial/federal learning-account or credential-recognition funding (carried-forward). Residents indirectly, via any resulting policy improvements GNLC peer-learning surfaces.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, via modest staff time within existing departmental budgets; no other payer class identified.

Financial ROI

Not separately estimated — a low-cost advocacy/convening measure without a direct fiscal-offset case in the sources reviewed.

Economic ROI

No source located this review models a direct economic-growth or employment effect attributable to GNLC membership itself, as distinct from the underlying learning-ecosystem investments membership doesn't fund; Edmonton's own UNESCO profile describes qualitative initiatives (Senior Advisory Committee, "City of Learners") but no quantified economic-impact figure [NEW-2026-2]. Confidence: low — a genuine gap, not computed from anything real.

Social ROI

Directional only: peer-network membership is a plausible, low-cost complement to an existing learning ecosystem (carried-forward "a learning culture compounds everything" framing), though no source quantifies a social-cohesion or learning-participation effect specifically attributable to membership as distinct from programs a member city runs regardless of membership status; Edmonton's own profile names qualitative initiatives but no outcome study [NEW-2026-2]. Confidence: low-medium.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely neutral — an international network membership and application process has no plausible emissions, land-use, water, waste, or resilience effect beyond staff time and periodic conference travel, itself unquantified. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-1 · source quote (this review) · Edmonton as Canada's first (and only, per this review's search) GNLC member; network scale (356 cities/79 countries)
  • NEW-2026-2 · source quote (this review) · UNESCO UIL's own Edmonton member-profile page: membership mechanics, focal point, qualitative initiatives
  • carried-forward · this page’s master briefing · Toronto Public Library as "the engine" of a learning city; existing community/settlement-sector programming

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence that GNLC membership itself is achievable and low-cost, based on Edmonton's precedent; low confidence on what concrete policy benefit membership delivers beyond peer-exchange access, since no quantified outcome study exists for any GNLC member city, including Edmonton. Whether Toronto has previously considered or been declined for GNLC membership was not confirmed this review — a real open question that should be checked before this card advances, since an undisclosed prior rejection would materially change its framing.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming whether Toronto has any prior GNLC-related history; identifying a real Edmonton (or other comparator city) cost/staffing figure for sustained membership; fairness and legal review.

a recommendation card — A Toronto Public Library-Led "Learning Discovery" Single-Access Point for Existing Adult-Learning Programs

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: most-educated-population-learning-city · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: carried-forward (friction-reduction/behavioural-design thesis) + New load-bearing findings (backgrounder NEW-2026-5)

Problem

The inherited briefing's central behavioural-design thesis is that reducing friction — making learning "free, nearby, and one-tap to find and start" — is one of the most powerful, lowest-cost levers available (carried-forward). This review's direct fetch of Toronto Public Library's own Adult Literacy Program page found the opposite for at least this program: eligibility is restricted to English speakers, intake requires an in-person 1.5–2 hour assessment, and enrollment is "limited and not guaranteed," with waitlisting or referral elsewhere as likely outcomes [NEW-2026-5]. This card addresses only the discovery/access-friction gap for adult learners navigating Toronto's substantial but fragmented learning ecosystem — it does not propose new funding for additional literacy-program capacity, a separate and unaddressed resourcing question.

Action

Toronto Public Library builds or designates a single, plain-language, multilingual "find a learning program" discovery page/tool that surfaces TPL's own adult-learning offerings (Adult Literacy, computer training, eLearning, job/career help) alongside cross-referrals to community, settlement-sector, and where relevant provincial/federal programs (bridge training, LINC, SkillsFuture-style Canadian equivalents where they exist), designed against the specific friction points this review documented — language restriction, in-person-only intake, unclear wait-time expectations — by stating those constraints clearly up front rather than requiring a full in-person assessment to discover them.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: build/designate the discovery tool using TPL's existing digital-services capacity (the library already operates "eLearning," "A-Z List of Databases," and "Programs" digital infrastructure per this review's direct site review) — within existing municipal/library-board authority, no new authority required.
  • City demands of Province: that provincially-funded adult-learning and bridge-training programs (per the sibling newcomer-success-credential-recognition page’s findings on Ontario Bridge Training funding status) maintain current, machine-readable program-status information the City's tool could draw on, rather than residents discovering funding holds only after applying.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low — a digital discovery/wayfinding tool built on existing TPL web infrastructure. Comparator: TPL already operates comparable-scale digital service-discovery infrastructure (its website's program listings, eResources hub, locations map), observed live in this review's direct site fetch, so this is not a new category of system for the library to build.

Funding path

Existing Toronto Public Library digital-services operating budget; no new funding source identified beyond incremental development/content-maintenance capacity within TPL's existing web-services function.

Who benefits, and how

Adult learners city-wide, particularly those the inherited briefing names as most at risk of being missed by an undesigned learning agenda — low-income residents, newcomers, the digitally excluded, those exhausted by survival work (carried-forward) — via a lower-friction path to discovering which existing program actually fits their eligibility and language before undertaking an in-person assessment process this review found can take 1.5–2 hours [NEW-2026-5].

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, via TPL's existing digital-services budget; no other payer class identified, since this rides on infrastructure the library already operates rather than creating a new program category.

Financial ROI

Not separately estimated — a discovery/wayfinding tool, not a program with a direct fiscal-offset case in the sources reviewed.

Economic ROI

No source located this review models a local-growth, spending, or employment effect for a program-discovery tool specifically, as distinct from the underlying learning programs it would help residents find; a live search for municipal learning-discovery-tool economic-impact studies did not surface one scoped to this mechanism. Confidence: low — a genuine gap, not computed from anything real.

Social ROI

Directional only: addresses a documented, directly-observed friction point — in-person-only intake, unclear eligibility, unclear wait times [NEW-2026-5] — that sits in tension with the inherited briefing's own friction-reduction thesis (carried-forward). No source quantifies how much a discovery tool alone would change actual program uptake, consistent with the inherited briefing's own caution that access/friction fixes are necessary but not obviously sufficient (per the SkillsFuture participation-gap finding elsewhere in this page’s backgrounder).

Environmental ROI

Genuinely neutral — a digital discovery tool built on existing web infrastructure has no plausible emissions, land-use, water, waste, or resilience effect beyond ordinary web-hosting, itself unquantified. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-5 · source quote (this review) · TPL Adult Literacy Program eligibility, in-person intake, and waitlist mechanics
  • carried-forward · this page’s master briefing · friction-reduction as a core behavioural-design lever ("free, nearby, and one-tap to find and start"); Matthew-effect equity risk of undesigned learning agendas

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence that a discovery-tool gap exists for at least the Adult Literacy Program specifically, based on this review's own direct fetch; lower confidence on whether the same friction pattern holds across TPL's other adult-learning offerings, not individually audited this review. Whether TPL already has an equivalent discovery tool this review simply did not locate — the library's own site does have a general "Programs" listing page, observed live, which may partially address this gap already — is a real open question that should be resolved before this card advances, since it may be proposing an incremental improvement to something that substantially exists, not a greenfield build.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming what discovery infrastructure TPL's existing "Programs" and "A-Z List of Services" pages already provide, to avoid proposing a duplicate of an existing tool; a real comparator cost figure; fairness and legal review.
