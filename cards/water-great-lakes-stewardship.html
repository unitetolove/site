a recommendation card — Publish a Standing CSO Discharge-Volume Public Dashboard

Card id: a recommendation card · Status: DRAFT · Backgrounder: our research file for that page Provenance: New load-bearing findings (anchored to a documented data gap this review itself hit) · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

This review's own live-discovery attempt for Toronto's actual annual combined sewer overflow (CSO) discharge volume — as opposed to the structural requirement that 90% of wet-weather-flow volume be captured and treated, and the count of 80 CSO discharge points — could not locate a directly-quotable, current primary source stating the actual annual discharge volume [see backgrounder, "Open questions / data gaps"]. If a our live-discovery track drafter working in good faith, with live web search, cannot find this figure in a reasonable search pass, the general public almost certainly cannot either. This card addresses that specific transparency gap, not CSO volume itself.

Action

The City publishes a standing, regularly updated public dashboard (or, at minimum, an annual plain-language summary table) reporting actual measured or modelled CSO discharge volume by outfall or by system-wide total, alongside the existing 90%-capture regulatory target, so residents and researchers can check performance against the target directly rather than inferring it from capital-plan progress reports.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: Toronto Water already collects and reports CSO-related data internally and to the province under existing regulatory requirements; packaging existing monitoring data into a public-facing dashboard is a data-publication and reporting-format decision within existing municipal authority, not a new legislative power.
  • City demands of Province: none required for this specific action; not applicable — this is a City reporting-transparency decision, not a request for provincial authorization.
  • City demands of Feds: none required for this specific action; not applicable.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low hundreds of thousands to low millions CAD one-time build cost plus modest ongoing maintenance, anchored to comparator municipal open-data dashboard builds generally (e.g., existing City of Toronto Open Data Portal infrastructure, which this could extend rather than duplicate) — this document does not have a specific CSO-dashboard cost comparator and states this range as an extrapolation from general municipal-dashboard build costs, not a cited City figure. Flagged as an estimate, not a sourced number.

Funding path

Existing Toronto Water operating/capital budget, potentially as a rider on the existing $4.3B+ stormwater/Wet Weather Flow Master Plan capital program the sister leaf (water-wastewater-broadband-infrastructure) documents, or through the City's existing Open Data Portal program budget — no new funding mechanism is proposed.

Who benefits, and how

Residents and researchers seeking to verify lake-health claims independently, via direct access to the same data the City itself uses to track its 90%-capture target; waterfront and beach users, via earlier, more specific information about discharge events near recreational areas; this project's own future Verify-lane passes, via a stable, citable primary source that does not currently exist in an easily discoverable form.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via the Toronto Water or Open Data Portal operating line; opportunity cost is the marginal budget capacity not directed to physical CSO-reduction capital work itself (this card is a transparency measure, not a substitute for the capital program the sister leaf addresses).

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — the backgrounder's own Cui Bono section states explicitly that no ESTABLISHED- or REPORTED-grade beneficiary was located in this review's search, and this card does not manufacture one.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Range: not yet estimable — no comparator study of a CSO-transparency-dashboard's own fiscal return was located this review. Comparator source: none identified yet. Confidence: low — this is a transparency measure whose financial case rests on avoided-controversy/avoided-crisis-communication cost logic, which no source in this review quantifies.

(b) Economic ROI

Range: not yet estimable. Comparator source: none identified yet. Confidence: low — no plausible direct economic-multiplier mechanism was identified for a data-transparency measure specifically, distinct from the CSO-reduction capital program itself.

(c) Social ROI

Range: directional only — increased public trust and verifiability of lake-health claims, not quantified. Comparator source: none identified yet quantifying a trust or civic-engagement effect specifically from a municipal environmental-data dashboard; the general open-data/civic-trust literature exists but was not fetched and confirmed in this review, so is not cited as a comparator here. Confidence: low — directionally plausible, not evidenced with a specific study in this review.

(d) Environmental ROI

Range: genuinely near-neutral directly — a reporting dashboard does not itself reduce CSO discharge; its environmental value is indirect (better-informed public pressure and target accountability), not a direct emissions/land/water mechanism. Comparator source: not applicable — plain read of the action's own scope (a reporting tool, not a physical intervention). Confidence: high — no plausible direct environmental mechanism attaches to a dashboard build itself, distinct from the capital program it would report on.

Evidence

  • NEW-1 · NEW · City of Toronto 2025 Drinking Water System Annual/Summary Reports (context: what a genuinely transparent, well-structured regulatory report already looks like for drinking water — this card proposes an analogous public-facing product for CSO/wastewater)
  • Backgrounder's own "Open questions / data gaps" section — the documented absence of a directly-quotable current CSO volume figure, this review's own concrete evidence of the transparency gap

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence that the underlying data gap is real (this review's own search experience is direct evidence of it, not a modelled assumption); low confidence on cost, financial, economic, and social ROI, all flagged as not-yet-estimable rather than guessed. This card does not resolve whether Toronto Water already has this data assembled internally and simply doesn't publish it, versus whether the data itself has real collection gaps — that distinction matters for cost and is not resolved here.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming with Toronto Water directly whether the underlying CSO volume data already exists in a publishable form (this materially changes the cost estimate), and a City-sourced cost comparator.

a recommendation card — Adopt a Standing Council Position Opposing Bulk Water Export Ahead of the 2026 CUSMA Review

Card id: a recommendation card · Status: DRAFT · Backgrounder: our research file for that page Provenance: New load-bearing findings (anchored to the May 2025 sovereignty-tension reporting) · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

The backgrounder documents a specific, dated, and current (2025) diplomatic tension in which a sitting US president reportedly expressed intent to reopen or abandon Great Lakes water-sharing agreements, with the 2026 CUSMA review named as a plausible venue for bulk-water-export terms to resurface, against a backdrop of fragmented Canadian federal water-governance capacity [NEW-5]. Toronto, as the largest municipality on the Great Lakes and a major drinking-water drawer from Lake Ontario, has a direct material interest in the outcome but currently has no documented standing municipal position identified in this review's search.

Action

City Council adopts a standing resolution affirming Toronto's opposition to any bulk removal or export of Great Lakes water from the basin, referencing the existing legal architecture (Transboundary Waters Protection Act 2013; Great Lakes-St. Lawrence River Basin Sustainable Water Resources Agreement) as the standard the City expects Canada to hold firm on through the 2026 CUSMA review, and directs staff to communicate this position to the Province, the federal government, and the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence Cities Initiative.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: passing a Council resolution stating a municipal position is entirely within existing municipal authority — no external approval required.
  • City demands of Province: that Ontario maintain and, if needed, actively defend the Great Lakes-St. Lawrence River Basin Sustainable Water Resources Agreement's bulk-export prohibition in any provincial-federal coordination on the CUSMA review.
  • City demands of Feds: that Canada's federal negotiators "decline to agree" (per the legal architecture named in NEW-5) to any reopening of water-export terms in the 2026 CUSMA review, and that the federal government address the named Canada Water Agency capacity gap (fragmented authority across eight federal portfolios) [NEW-5] ahead of that review.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: negligible direct cost — a Council resolution and staff communication effort is a matter of political and staff time, not capital expenditure. This card does not claim a dollar cost because none is realistically incurred beyond ordinary Council/staff process costs.

Funding path

Not applicable — no new funding mechanism required; existing Council and City Clerk's Office operations cover the cost of passing and communicating a resolution.

Who benefits, and how

All Toronto water users, via the City adding its political weight (as the largest Great Lakes municipality) to the existing legal-prohibition architecture at a moment the backgrounder documents as under live political pressure; the City's own long-term water-security interest, via reduced risk that Great Lakes protections erode through inattention during a high-stakes trade renegotiation.

Who bears the cost, and how

No meaningful direct cost-bearer; to the extent there is an opportunity cost, it is Council and staff time that could be directed elsewhere, which this card treats as negligible relative to the action's scope.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — the backgrounder's Cui Bono section is explicitly empty with a stated reason (no ESTABLISHED/REPORTED-grade beneficiary of Great Lakes water-export pressure specific to Toronto was located in this review), and this card does not invent one. Note the asymmetry here is directional: this action opposes a change (bulk export) rather than ending an existing extraction arrangement, so a "who benefits from the status quo" framing does not naturally apply in the same way it would to, say, a shelter-operator contract — flagged as a genuine fit issue with the template's own framing for this specific card, not a gap in research effort.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Range: not applicable/negligible cost, so no meaningful financial ROI calculation applies in the conventional sense. Comparator source: not applicable. Confidence: high — the action's own near-zero direct cost makes a "return" framing largely moot; this is stated plainly rather than forcing a number.

(b) Economic ROI

Range: not yet estimable — the economic value of maintaining Great Lakes water security is almost certainly very large (drinking water for ~35 million people region-wide, per the inherited document's uncited figure, not independently re-verified this review) but no source in this review models a specific economic value specifically attributable to one municipal resolution's marginal contribution to that outcome. Comparator source: none identified yet — this card resists borrowing the inherited document's unconfirmed 35-million-person figure as a stand-in for a modelled economic-ROI figure, since population served is not the same claim as economic value protected. Confidence: low — genuine gap, stated as such rather than estimated from an unrelated figure.

(c) Social ROI

Range: directional only — reinforces public and political salience of Great Lakes protection at a moment the backgrounder documents as under real pressure; not quantified. Comparator source: NEW-5's own account of the 2025 diplomatic tension is the anchor for why this matters now, not a quantified social-outcome study. Confidence: low-medium — the timing rationale (ahead of the 2026 CUSMA review) is well-supported by NEW-5; the actual behavioural/political effect of one municipal resolution is not measurable from any source in this review.

(d) Environmental ROI

Range: indirect and non-quantifiable at the scale of one resolution — the environmental stakes of Great Lakes bulk-export protection generally are very large, but this specific card's marginal environmental contribution (one more voice reinforcing an existing legal prohibition) is not separately modelled by any source. Comparator source: not applicable at this card's scale. Confidence: low — stated honestly as not meaningfully separable from the larger legal-architecture's own environmental stakes.

Evidence

  • NEW-5 · NEW · National Magazine, "Water war" (2025-05-07) — the 2025 diplomatic tension, named legal-prohibition architecture, Canada Water Agency capacity gap, 2026 CUSMA review as the live venue
  • NEW-2 · NEW · Canada Water Agency COA page — confirms the binational/federal-provincial agreement architecture this card's resolution would reference

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-high confidence that the underlying tension this card responds to is real and current (NEW-5 is a specific, dated, quoted account, not a speculative framing). Low confidence on any quantified ROI dimension, all stated as not-yet-estimable rather than guessed, consistent with the action's own low-cost, symbolic-but-substantively-grounded nature. This card does not claim a municipal resolution would materially change the outcome of a federal trade negotiation — it claims only that adding Toronto's documented position to the existing legal-prohibition architecture, at a moment of documented pressure, is a low-cost, well-timed action consistent with the City's own direct material interest as the largest Great Lakes municipality.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming current City of Toronto membership status in the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence Cities Initiative or equivalent coalition (not independently confirmed in this review), and fairness and legal review given the card touches an active binational political controversy.
