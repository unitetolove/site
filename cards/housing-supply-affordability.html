Complete city-wide sixplex legalization — Recommendation Card

Card id: RC-0001 · File: research/cards/housing-supply-affordability.md#rc-0001 · Status: DRAFT Issue slug: housing-supply-affordability · Backgrounder: research/backgrounders/housing-supply-affordability.md §"Zoning reform in Toronto" Trust class: INHERITED (premise) / NEW-LOAD-BEARING (2026 status note) · Date: 2026-07-13

Problem

Toronto's Expanding Housing Options in Neighbourhoods (EHON) programme legalized fourplexes as-of-right city-wide in 2023, but sixplexes as-of-right exist only in the Toronto and East York district plus Ward 23 (Scarborough North), after Council's June 25, 2025 vote (18–6) diluted a staff proposal for city-wide sixplex legalization into a ward-by-ward opt-in model. City-wide sixplex legalization is one of 35 completion milestones under Toronto's $471.1 million federal Housing Accelerator Fund (HAF) agreement, and non-compliance carries a 25%-of-annual-payment penalty under that agreement [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning); this library's inherited source document (HAF and rental-market conditions, 2026)]. As of this pass (2026-07-13), no primary source confirms any additional ward has opted in since that vote — stated as ⚠️ VERIFY in the backgrounder, not resolved here.

Action

Council completes the pattern it already set with 2023's city-wide fourplex bylaw by extending as-of-right sixplex permission to all 23 wards, replacing the current opt-in model for Etobicoke, North York, and the Scarborough wards outside Ward 23.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: pass the zoning bylaw amendment. Council already exercised exactly this authority for fourplexes city-wide in 2023 and for the partial sixplex geography in 2025 — no new delegated power is required, only a further Council vote.
  • City demands of Province: none — unlike inclusionary zoning (see RC-0002), sixplex-level as-of-right permission is not provincially capped; the current limitation is a municipal Council decision, not a provincial override.
  • City demands of Feds: none required for the zoning action itself. The existing HAF agreement's own compliance deadline and clawback structure is the funding stake already in place, not a new ask.

Cost

Order of magnitude: low, non-capital. A zoning bylaw amendment is a staff-time and consultation cost, not a capital expenditure — the same order of cost as the 2023 city-wide fourplex amendment and the 2025 partial sixplex amendment, both already completed by the City without new capital funding [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning)]. Named comparator for the associated revenue side: Toronto has already waived development charges on projects up to six units, reportedly saving roughly $200,000–$270,000 per project and totalling approximately $200 million foregone across 8,000 purpose-built rental units city-wide under the existing DC-waiver policy — a cost the City has already absorbed at current uptake, which city-wide sixplex legalization would extend rather than newly create [quote-context: Toronto's Mayor, Ontario Construction News, "Toronto allows sixplexes in downtown and Scarborough neighbourhoods," accessed 2026-07-13].

Funding path

No new external funding mechanism is required for the zoning change itself. The financial stake is defensive: retaining the full $471.1 million HAF agreement (avoiding the 25%-of-annual-payment non-compliance penalty) functions as the funding path already committed and at risk [INHERITED: this library's inherited source document (HAF and rental-market conditions, 2026)].

Who benefits, and how

Homeowners and small developers in Etobicoke, North York, and the Scarborough wards outside Ward 23 who currently lack as-of-right sixplex permission, via the same as-of-right (no rezoning hearing, no OLT-appeal exposure) mechanism already available in Toronto & East York and Ward 23. Prospective tenants of missing-middle units city-wide, via a larger buildable geography — though the magnitude of any rent effect is not separately quantified for sixplexes specifically in the cited evidence (see Confidence, below).

Who bears the cost, and how

Existing single-family homeowners in the newly-affected wards face the density/character change that organized opposition cited in the actual June 2025 debate (concerns about parking, privacy, and infrastructure capacity, per councillors opposed to the original city-wide motion) [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning)]. City taxpayers bear the opportunity cost of further development-charge revenue foregone if uptake scales with the wider geography, on top of the roughly $200 million already waived city-wide.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — housing-supply-affordability.md's Cui Bono section reports 0 ESTABLISHED/REPORTED entities, since no source names an entity profiting specifically from the current sixplex-geography restriction itself (as distinct from the separately-documented, unnamed-developer Greenbelt land-value windfall the backgrounder flags as a gap, not a citable row).

Financial ROI

The clearest quantifiable ROI is defensive: retention of the full $471.1 million HAF agreement value by meeting this named milestone, rather than losing 25% of an annual payment to non-compliance. No source cited in this leaf's evidence base separately quantifies a positive revenue or cost-avoidance figure specific to sixplex expansion beyond that HAF-compliance value.

Economic ROI

Range: Order-of-magnitude only — applying Statistics Canada's general construction GDP multiplier (approximately 1.8x total output per $1 of construction spend) to the incremental sixplex-driven construction activity in the newly-affected wards implies a real, non-trivial local/regional induced-output effect, but no source in this leaf's evidence base isolates a sixplex-specific construction-spending total to apply that multiplier to, so no dollar range is stated. Comparator source: Statistics Canada, "National and Provincial Multipliers" input-output series (https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/en/catalogue/15F0046X; current detail-level tables at https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=3610059401), the standard national-accounts construction-sector output multiplier, applied here by analogy to residential infill construction generally rather than derived from a sixplex-specific study. Methodology caveat: this is a general construction multiplier (StatCan's dedicated report series was discontinued; multipliers now live only in linked data tables), not a missing-middle- or Toronto-specific economic-impact model. Confidence: Low — the multiplier itself is a credible national-accounts figure, but no sixplex-specific spending total exists in this leaf's sources to apply it to, consistent with the Cost/Social ROI sections' own flagged gap on sixplex-specific magnitude.

Social ROI

The full EHON reform suite (fourplexes, sixplexes, major-streets apartments, garden/laneway suites combined) is projected by the City's own planning research at approximately 54,600 units by 2031 and 163,785 units by 2051 [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning), citing City of Toronto's Missing Middle and Midrise Housing Implementation report, June 2025]. The evidence base does not decompose this projection by sixplex-specific geography, so this card does not claim a sixplex-specific unit count for full city-wide legalization — an honest gap, not a manufactured number.

Environmental ROI

Range: Directionally positive on two independent bases — lower embodied emissions per bedroom than single-family/detached forms, and reduced per-household operating emissions and driving from smaller, more efficient, better-located units — though neither figure is Toronto-sixplex-specific and both come from comparator jurisdictions/building types, not a modelled figure for this exact bylaw change. Comparator source: (1) Rankin, Arceo, Isin & Saxe, "Embodied GHG of missing middle," Journal of Industrial Ecology, 2024 (https://doi.org/10.1111/jiec.13461; https://csbe.civmin.utoronto.ca/research/embodied-ghg-ofmissing-middle/) — Ontario-grounded, finding missing-middle (low-rise multi-unit) forms average lower embodied GHG per bedroom than single-family homes (range 5,540–39,600 kgCO2eq/bedroom across all forms, with design mattering more than form). (2) Sightline Institute, "A Duplex, a Triplex and a Fourplex Can Cut a Block's Carbon Impact 20%" (Michael Andersen, 2019, https://www.sightline.org/2019/06/07/a-duplex-a-triplex-and-a-fourplex-can-cut-a-blocks-carbon-impact-20/), a Portland, Oregon-based analysis (not Toronto, not peer-reviewed — a think-tank piece extrapolating from a 2010 Oregon DEQ life-cycle study) finding replacing three detached homes with a duplex/triplex/fourplex cut per-household housing-carbon by ~20% and correlated with roughly 1,000 fewer vehicle-miles/year/household. Methodology caveat stated plainly: source (1) is peer-reviewed and Ontario-specific but covers "missing middle" broadly, not sixplexes narrowly or Toronto specifically; source (2) is US, advocacy-affiliated, and about smaller (2-4 unit) forms, extrapolated here to sixplexes as a directionally-similar but not identical building type. Confidence: Medium for direction (multiple independent sources agree low-rise infill reduces per-household carbon versus detached-home baselines); low for magnitude specific to Toronto sixplexes, since neither comparator is a sixplex-specific, Toronto-specific study.

Evidence

  • INHERITED this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning) · EHON timeline, 65% exclusionary-zoning baseline, June 2025 vote detail, EHON unit projections
  • INHERITED this library's inherited source document (HAF and rental-market conditions, 2026) (foreign-ledger IDs cited as-is) · HAF milestone structure, $471.1M agreement, 25%-of-annual-payment penalty
  • NEW, live-fetched 2026-07-13 · Ontario Construction News, Mayor's quote on DC waivers ($200M/8,000 units)

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on feasibility (the City has already exercised this exact authority twice — 2023 fourplex, 2025 partial sixplex) and on the funding stake (the HAF penalty structure is documented). Lower confidence on magnitude of benefit: no cited source quantifies expected new units, rent effect, or affordability impact specific to expanding sixplex permission beyond the currently-covered wards. The political resistance that produced the 2025 compromise (organized homeowners' associations, named councillor opposition) is a real, cited obstacle this card does not minimize.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: a real second comparator for the cost/benefit magnitude (currently anchored only to the City's own prior actions), and confirmation of whether any ward has opted in since June 2025 (⚠️ VERIFY, unresolved this pass).

Restore graduated inclusionary zoning depth — Recommendation Card

Card id: RC-0002 · File: research/cards/housing-supply-affordability.md#rc-0002 · Status: DRAFT Issue slug: housing-supply-affordability · Backgrounder: research/backgrounders/housing-supply-affordability.md §"Zoning reform in Toronto" Trust class: INHERITED · Date: 2026-07-13

Problem

Toronto's inclusionary zoning (IZ) policy took effect in August 2025 within 800m of Protected Major Transit Station Areas, after years of provincial delay. Its final form is a flat 5% of units/gross floor area, held affordable for 25 years, applied uniformly to rental and ownership — a provincial cap under the Bill 23-era override that replaced Council's own 2021 bylaw, which had set graduated rates (5–7%/7–10% rental/ownership initially, rising to 8–16%/11–22% by 2030) with 99-year affordability [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning), citing Social Planning Toronto, Oct 2025]. This is a live instance of the same executive-override/technical-body tension the backgrounder documents for the Greenbelt: municipal planning intent overridden by provincial regulation under the province's Planning Act authority [CL-0001].

Action

Council formally requests — by resolution — that the Province amend the Bill 23-era IZ regulation to restore municipal discretion over IZ set-aside rates and affordability terms (i.e., permit Toronto's own graduated 2021 bylaw design), and to expand IZ's geographic reach beyond the current 800m Protected-MTSA radius.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: pass a Council resolution formalizing the ask; nothing further is achievable unilaterally, since the cap itself is set by provincial regulation, not municipal choice.
  • City demands of Province: repeal or amend the regulatory cap that overrides Council's own 2021 graduated-rate, 99-year-affordability bylaw, and permit IZ application beyond 800m of Protected MTSAs.
  • City demands of Feds: none directly; federal housing-supply policy (HAF, National Housing Strategy) is an indirect audience but does not control this specific provincial regulatory lever.

Cost

Order of magnitude: the direct fiscal cost of a deeper IZ requirement falls on developers/landowners, not the City's own budget — IZ is a regulatory requirement on private development, economically incident on land price rather than a public expenditure [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (housing supply and rents)]. Named comparator: Toronto's own pre-override 2021 bylaw is the comparator for what "restored" IZ would look like, since it is the same city's own prior, Council-passed design — not an invented external figure.

Funding path

No City funding mechanism is required for the ask itself; the "cost" of a restored, deeper IZ is borne by the private development sector through the regulatory requirement, as under the original 2021 design. The funding path for the advocacy action is a Council resolution, at negligible direct cost.

Who benefits, and how

Renters and buyers who would access below-market IZ units, via a policy set-aside applied at deeper rates and to more of the city than the current 5%/800m-radius policy reaches. Toronto's own Council, whose 2021 planning judgment (graduated rates calibrated to market conditions) would be restored rather than overridden.

Who bears the cost, and how

Developers subject to a deeper set-aside requirement, and — per the incidence logic in this leaf's inherited evidence — landowners, who typically absorb IZ costs through reduced land price rather than developers passing it through entirely [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning)]. The current 800m-of-Protected-MTSA geographic limit already means IZ's benefit (and cost) is concentrated near transit, not city-wide — a distributional fact this card does not obscure.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — housing-supply-affordability.md's Cui Bono section reports 0 ESTABLISHED/REPORTED entities. The backgrounder does flag, as a documented-but-unnamed outcome (not a citable Cui Bono row), that landowners generally absorb IZ costs through reduced land price under the incidence logic cited above — but no source names a specific entity benefiting from the current, weaker 5%/25-year cap specifically, as distinct from a deeper regime.

Financial ROI

Not quantified in the cited evidence base — no source here estimates the incremental affordable-unit yield or public cost-avoidance from restoring graduated rates specifically. Flagged plainly rather than invented.

Economic ROI

Range: Not yet estimable — the empirical literature on inclusionary zoning's market effects is genuinely mixed, and none of it is Toronto-specific or scoped to this exact "restore graduated 2021 rates" scenario, so no range is stated. Comparator source: A live search this pass found conflicting empirical findings rather than one comparator: one empirical U.S. study found IZ policies on average did not affect municipality-wide permits or rents but were associated with an average 2.1% increase in home prices (ScienceDirect, "Do inclusionary zoning policies affect local housing markets?," https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0264275125000368); a separate Urban Institute review found cities with robust economies saw a 10–20% increase in affordable-housing units under IZ, while weaker-economy cities saw negligible effects (Urban Institute, "Inclusionary Zoning," https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/publication/99647/inclusionary_zoning._what_does_the_research_tell_us_about_the_effectiveness_of_local_action_2.pdf). Methodology caveat: both are U.S. jurisdictions under different market/regulatory conditions than Toronto's Bill 23-era provincial cap, and the two findings point in different directions (price effect vs. unit-production effect) — naming a single number here would misrepresent a genuinely contested literature. Confidence: Low — real comparators exist, but they disagree with each other and neither is Toronto-specific, so this card cannot honestly narrow to a range.

Social ROI

Directional only: a deeper, broader IZ policy would, per the reasoning in this leaf's inherited zoning briefing, generate more affordable units from market development without direct public subsidy — the same mechanism already in effect at the reduced 5% rate, just at greater depth and reach. No specific unit-count estimate for the restored-rate scenario is cited in the evidence base.

Environmental ROI

Range: Genuinely environmentally neutral, distinct from the physical-construction cards above — this card's action is a Council resolution requesting a provincial regulatory change to IZ set-aside depth and geographic reach; it does not itself authorize or trigger any construction. Any environmental effect would come from whatever development occurs under a restored IZ regime, which is the same underlying construction this leaf's other cards (RC-0001, RC-0003) already address, not a separate effect of this specific advocacy action. Comparator source: None needed for the resolution itself; stated directly per the template's allowance for a genuinely neutral action. Confidence: High for the "the resolution itself is neutral" claim; any downstream construction effect is already covered by this leaf's other cards rather than double-counted here.

Evidence

  • INHERITED this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning) · IZ history, 2021 bylaw's original graduated rates, provincial 5%/25-yr override, Social Planning Toronto critique
  • CL-0001 (this repo's ledger, VERIFIED) · provincial Planning Act sets municipal zoning authority, establishing why this is a demand of the Province rather than a unilateral City action

Confidence & uncertainties

Low-medium confidence. This is an advocacy ask entirely contingent on provincial political will under a Bill 23 framework the current provincial government itself enacted — the card is honest that municipal power here is limited to requesting, not compelling, the change. No financial-ROI figure exists in the evidence base for the specific magnitude of restoring graduated rates; this is stated as a gap, not papered over.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: no quantified ROI estimate exists yet for the restored-rate scenario; would benefit from a dedicated Lane M/V pass estimating expected additional affordable-unit yield before a stronger claim is made.

Fast, publicly-reported permitting for as-of-right missing-middle housing — Recommendation Card

Card id: RC-0003 · File: research/cards/housing-supply-affordability.md#rc-0003 · Status: DRAFT Issue slug: housing-supply-affordability · Backgrounder: research/backgrounders/housing-supply-affordability.md §"Zoning reform in Toronto" / §"Does building supply lower rents" Trust class: INHERITED · Date: 2026-07-13

Problem

Zoning permission alone does not build housing. This leaf's inherited land-use briefing itself names permitting delay as a central risk to the EHON reform's success ("a fourplex that takes 3 years to permit achieves nothing"), and its companion supply-and-rents briefing reports as-of-right multiplex permits were "reportedly issued in ~10–21 days, down from 6–12 months pre-2024" — a figure that source itself flags as needing confirmation [INHERITED, VERIFY: this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning); this leaf's inherited master briefing (housing supply and rents)]. Neither source establishes that this faster timeline is consistently applied, publicly reported, or measured in completed units rather than applications.

Action

Council directs Planning staff to set and publicly report, on a quarterly basis, a fixed processing-time target for all as-of-right EHON-eligible permit applications (fourplex, sixplex, garden/laneway suite), queued separately from larger discretionary applications, and to report actual median processing times and completed-unit counts — not applications filed — against that target.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: the entire action. Permit-queue prioritization, staff resourcing, and public reporting are existing municipal administrative functions requiring no new provincial or federal authority.
  • City demands of Province: none.
  • City demands of Feds: none directly, though faster, verifiable approvals is itself one of the reform themes the existing federal HAF agreement rewards, per this leaf's inherited HAF documentation — this card treats that as context, not as a new ask.

Cost

Order of magnitude: low, non-capital — a queue-prioritization and recurring-reporting process change, on the order of a modest reallocation of existing Planning division staff time. Named comparator: Toronto's own prior process change, which this leaf's inherited evidence reports (at VERIFY status) already cut as-of-right multiplex permit times from 6–12 months to roughly 10–21 days — i.e., the comparator is the City's own claimed precedent, not an external city, and this card does not manufacture a dollar figure beyond that.

Funding path

Existing Planning & Housing divisional operating budget; no new external funding mechanism is required, since this is a process and public-reporting change rather than a capital program.

Who benefits, and how

Homeowners and small developers pursuing as-of-right fourplex, sixplex, or garden/laneway-suite construction city-wide, via a faster, predictable, and publicly-auditable permit queue. Prospective tenants of missing-middle units, indirectly, through faster conversion of zoning permission into completed, occupiable homes.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, via the marginal administrative cost of prioritization and reporting. Applicants for larger, discretionary (non-EHON) planning applications potentially see relatively slower processing if staff attention is reallocated toward the prioritized EHON queue — a symmetric cost this card names rather than omits.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — housing-supply-affordability.md's Cui Bono section reports 0 ESTABLISHED/REPORTED entities relevant to permit-processing speed specifically.

Financial ROI

Not separately quantified for the reporting/prioritization change itself. The clearest qualitative case is supporting continued compliance with the existing HAF agreement's own approvals-speed theme, protecting the same $471.1 million funding value referenced in RC-0001, rather than a new, independently quantified return.

Economic ROI

Range: Not yet estimable — a permit-queue prioritization and reporting change has no direct local-growth or induced-spending effect of its own; its economic effect runs entirely through however many additional as-of-right units get built faster because of it, which this leaf's own sources do not quantify (the underlying permit-timeline figures themselves are at VERIFY, per Problem above). Comparator source: None identified — a targeted search this pass for a "faster municipal permitting economic impact" study specific to missing-middle/infill housing did not return a study modelling a permit-speed change's own downstream economic effect; this is stated as a genuine gap rather than stretched to fit an off-topic source. Confidence: Low — genuine gap; even the foundational timeline claim this card's case depends on is unconfirmed, so an economic range built on top of it would compound an already-flagged uncertainty.

Social ROI

This leaf's inherited land-use briefing identifies "the conversion rate from zoning permission to building permit to completed home" as the single most important variable determining whether the EHON reform's projected 54,600-by-2031 / 163,785-by-2051 units are actually realized [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning)]. This card's social ROI case rests on improving that conversion rate; no source cited here quantifies by how much a fixed processing target would move it, and this card does not invent that number.

Environmental ROI

Range: Genuinely environmentally neutral in itself — a queue-prioritization and public-reporting process change does not build anything or change land use; whatever environmental profile the eventual as-of-right units carry is the same missing-middle construction profile already addressed under RC-0001's Environmental ROI above, not a separate effect created by faster processing. Comparator source: None needed for the process change itself; where relevant, see RC-0001's Environmental ROI above (Rankin et al. 2024, Journal of Industrial Ecology; Sightline Institute 2019) for the construction-stage footprint of the units this card would help deliver faster. Confidence: High for "the process change itself is neutral"; any downstream construction effect is already scored under RC-0001 rather than duplicated here.

Evidence

  • INHERITED, VERIFY this leaf's inherited master briefing (land use zoning) / this leaf's inherited master briefing (housing supply and rents) · permit-timeline figures (6–12 months → 10–21 days), both explicitly unconfirmed in the source itself
  • INHERITED this library's inherited source document (HAF and rental-market conditions, 2026) · HAF's own approvals-speed reform theme and funding stake

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-high confidence on feasibility and jurisdiction (entirely within existing municipal administrative authority, no external approval needed). Low confidence on quantified ROI: the load-bearing permit-timeline figures this card's problem statement rests on are themselves at VERIFY, not independently confirmed, in the inherited source — this card proceeds on the reasonable premise that faster, measured permitting is directionally beneficial regardless of the exact prior baseline, but does not claim a confirmed dollar or unit figure.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: independent confirmation of the 6–12-month-to-10–21-day permit timeline claim (currently VERIFY in the inherited source), and Lane R pass before any public-facing use.
