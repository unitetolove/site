Road Safety and Vision Zero — Recommendation Cards

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/cards/road-safety-vision-zero.md

Issue slug: road-safety-vision-zero · Backgrounder: research/backgrounders/road-safety-vision-zero.md Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Status: DRAFT, both cards below

Two cards, scoped to the backgrounder's two sharpest, most actionable findings: (1) targeting Toronto's own infrastructure investment at the specific 21%-of-network arterial roads generating 83% of serious injuries/fatalities, entirely within municipal authority; (2) a formal Council demand that the Province fund a physical-infrastructure replacement for the automated speed enforcement capacity Bill 56 removed, given the documented safety evidence behind the removed tool. Card-id scheme RC-RSVZ-0N used here (leaf-scoped), parallel to the RC-MOB-0N/RC-RENT-0N convention already used elsewhere in research/cards/ — no global RC-#### registry exists yet to check against for collisions with concurrent leaf agents.

Target Arterial-Road Safety Infrastructure Where the Data Actually Points — Recommendation Card

Card id: RC-RSVZ-01 · File: research/cards/road-safety-vision-zero.md#rc-rsvz-01 Status: DRAFT Issue slug: road-safety-vision-zero · Backgrounder: road-safety-vision-zero Trust class: INHERITED (ledger claims) · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

Toronto's own collision data shows that 83% of the city's serious injuries and fatalities from 2021 to 2025 occurred on arterial roads, which make up only 21% of the city's total road network [backgrounder CL-0748]. This is a specific, actionable finding about where the highest-leverage infrastructure investment lies, distinct from the general "Vision Zero is working" narrative supported by the city's aggregate 50%-since-2016 fatality reduction [CL-0745] — an aggregate result that does not, on its own, establish whether investment has been proportionately targeted at the roads generating the most harm.

Action

Direct Toronto's Vision Zero Road Safety Plan to publish, alongside its existing annual fatality/injury dashboard, an annual accounting of infrastructure countermeasures (curb extensions, protected crossings, speed-limit reductions, physical traffic-calming) delivered specifically on the highest-collision arterial corridors identified by the City's own data — making the 83%/21% concentration finding [CL-0748] a standing prioritization criterion for capital allocation, not just a descriptive statistic.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: arterial-road infrastructure redesign, speed-limit setting on municipal roads, and Vision Zero Plan prioritization are within existing City of Toronto Transportation Services authority — no provincial or federal approval required for the core action.
  • City demands of Province: none required for the core action; however, some arterial roads carry provincial highway designations or interact with provincially-controlled signal/traffic infrastructure in limited cases — flagged as a boundary condition, not the card's primary ask.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified for this specific action.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: not separately estimated in the current evidence base. No source in this pass provides a per-kilometre or per-corridor cost for arterial-specific traffic-calming retrofits in Toronto specifically; the sibling mobility-congestion-transit backgrounder's Congestion Management Plan (2026-2028, $299.4 million across five pillars including transit-reliability and traffic-management measures with overlapping arterial-corridor scope) is the nearest named comparator for municipal traffic-infrastructure program cost at scale, though it is not a road-safety-specific budget and no sub-allocation to arterial safety retrofits was identified. This card does not manufacture a cost figure no source supports.

Funding path

City capital budget, most plausibly within or alongside the existing Congestion Management Plan appropriation given its overlapping "expanding traffic management" pillar (per the mobility-congestion-transit backgrounder), or a dedicated Vision Zero capital line if a separate one exists — not independently confirmed in this pass.

Who benefits, and how

Pedestrians and cyclists using Toronto's arterial roads, who together accounted for roughly half of the city's 39 road deaths in 2025 [backgrounder NEW-1] and a disproportionate share of collisions concentrated on the very roads this card targets; seniors aged 65+, who made up 29% of pedestrian collisions from 2021-2025 despite being 17% of the population [CL-0749].

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via the capital budget; drivers on affected arterial corridors, via any speed-limit reductions or lane reconfigurations that increase travel time, an opportunity cost this card does not quantify.

Financial ROI

Not separately quantified. Transport Canada's $36 billion national estimate of the social cost of motor vehicle collisions for 2020 [CL-0728] establishes that collision costs are large in aggregate, but no source in this pass apportions a Toronto-specific or arterial-specific avoided-cost figure to this particular action.

Economic ROI

Range: Not yet estimable as a Toronto-specific or arterial-specific figure. Comparator source: None identified in this pass. A future pass should look for a Toronto-specific or Ontario-specific study translating collision reduction into avoided healthcare/productivity costs at the corridor level, rather than relying on the $36B national aggregate [CL-0728], which is not decomposable to this action's scale. Confidence: low — genuine gap, not computed from anything real.

Social ROI

Directional case only, but unusually well-evidenced for this template's "how to fill honestly" standard: Ottawa's Road Safety Action Plan cites research finding pedestrians struck at 30 km/h have roughly a 10% chance of dying, rising sharply at higher speeds, and modelling finding a 15% increase in vehicle speed is associated with a 65% increase in fatal-collision risk [backgrounder CL-0752, CL-0753] — a direct, mechanistic case that speed-reduction infrastructure on the highest-risk roads should reduce fatality severity, not just collision frequency. Confidence is medium rather than low specifically because this mechanism (not just a correlational study) is independently documented.

Environmental ROI

Range: Modest, directionally positive. Traffic calming and arterial speed reduction can produce small mode-shift effects (toward walking/cycling on now-safer corridors) and modestly reduced emissions from smoother, lower-speed traffic flow, but this is a safety-primary intervention, not an environmental one. Comparator source: None sought specific to Toronto arterials; the action's primary evidenced case is safety, not environmental, and this card does not stretch a modest co-benefit into a headline claim. Confidence: low — no Toronto-specific study located; stated as a plausible modest co-benefit only.

Evidence

  • CL-0748 · VERIFY · Toronto arterial-road collision concentration (83% of SI/fatalities on 21% of network)
  • CL-0745 · VERIFY · Toronto 2025 fatalities, 50% reduction since 2016
  • CL-0749 · VERIFY · senior/demographic pedestrian-collision overrepresentation
  • CL-0752, CL-0753 · VERIFY · speed/pedestrian-survivability and speed/fatal-risk mechanisms (Ottawa Road Safety Action Plan citations)
  • CL-0728 · VERIFY · $36B national social cost of collisions estimate (context only, not apportioned)

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the problem statement (the 83%/21% concentration is a specific, City-sourced finding); low confidence on cost and Economic ROI, both flagged as genuine gaps rather than estimated. This card takes a position (arterial roads should be the explicit prioritization criterion) because the underlying mechanism (speed/survivability) is well-evidenced even though Toronto-specific cost data is not.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: a Toronto-specific arterial-safety-retrofit cost figure; confirming whether Vision Zero capital allocation is already arterial-prioritized in practice (this card's ask may already be partly met — not established either way in this pass); Lane R pass.

Formally Demand the Province Fund a Real Traffic-Calming Replacement for the Automated Speed Enforcement It Banned — Recommendation Card

Card id: RC-RSVZ-02 · File: research/cards/road-safety-vision-zero.md#rc-rsvz-02 Status: DRAFT Issue slug: road-safety-vision-zero · Backgrounder: road-safety-vision-zero Trust class: NEW-LOAD-BEARING (built on quote-context findings, not yet ledger-minted) · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

Ontario's Bill 56 ended municipal authority to operate automated speed enforcement, effective November 14, 2025, on the Province's stated rationale that ASE had become a revenue tool rather than a genuine safety measure [backgrounder NEW-2]. The Province did name a costed, dated replacement — $210 million for physical traffic-calming via the Road Safety Initiatives Fund, announced November 13, 2025, with $42 million released immediately (Toronto's specific share is reported as $10 million but was not confirmed against a primary source as of independently re-verified 2026-07-16) and the remaining $168 million open to municipal application from Feb. 19, 2026 [backgrounder NEW-7] — but the tool it replaced had a specific, named, recent evidence base for effectiveness (a SickKids/Toronto Metropolitan University study finding a 45% reduction in Toronto school-zone speeding and an 87% reduction in severe speeding, 20+ km/h over the limit, attributable to ASE — figure corrected from 88%, independently re-verified 2026-07-16 by direct fetch of the study and TMU's press release) [backgrounder NEW-3], and no source located in this pass establishes whether the $210 million commitment is sufficient in scale or scope to replace that measured effect, nor how Toronto's own reported $10 million (or any further tranche) has actually been allocated or spent. A Toronto staff report considered by council in mid-2026 found speeding increased at 101 of 104 comparable locations since the ban [independently re-verified 2026-07-16] — direct, local evidence the replacement funding's on-street deployment has not yet closed. The live, unresolved gap is not "no funding was named" (corrected in this pass) but whether the named funding is adequate, dated with a full disbursement timeline, and actually deployed where the removed tool's evidence was strongest.

Action

Toronto City Council formally request that the Province of Ontario publish a corridor-level accounting of how Toronto's reported $10 million immediate allocation (and any further tranche of the $210 million provincial traffic-calming commitment) [backgrounder NEW-7] has been or will be spent, with school-zone and community-safety-zone infrastructure prioritized first, given that is where the removed tool's own strongest evidence of effectiveness was concentrated [backgrounder NEW-3] — rather than treating a headline provincial commitment as self-executing without municipal-level transparency on deployment.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: draft and pass the resolution; identify Toronto's own priority school/community-safety-zone corridors as a ready-made target list if provincial funding materializes, building on the City's existing Vision Zero dashboard and arterial-concentration data [CL-0748].
  • City demands of Province: the funding commitment itself — a Ministry of Transportation budget and policy decision, since Bill 56 was itself a provincial law overriding municipal authority [backgrounder NEW-2].
  • City demands of Feds: none identified for this specific action.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: not a City cost — this is a demand that the Province specify and fund its own already-stated commitment. No dollar figure is invented here because the Province has not yet named one in any source located in this pass.

Funding path

Not applicable to the City; would be a provincial general-revenue or dedicated-program commitment if granted, structurally similar to the Ontario Gas Tax Program or the Ontario-Toronto New Deal Agreement's existing transit-specific transfers described in the sibling mobility-congestion-transit backgrounder, though no such road-safety-specific transfer mechanism was identified as already existing.

Who benefits, and how

School-zone-area residents, children, and other vulnerable road users in the highest-risk zones the removed cameras covered, via restored (non-camera) speed-reduction infrastructure, if funded; Toronto taxpayers, via provincial rather than municipal funding of the replacement, if the ask succeeds.

Who bears the cost, and how

Provincial taxpayers, via general revenue, if the ask succeeds — explicitly a demand on the Province's own budget, not a municipal cost. If the ask fails and no replacement materializes, the City and its residents effectively bear the cost via unaddressed risk in the affected zones, an opportunity-cost framing this card states rather than quantifies.

Financial ROI

Not estimated. No source in this pass models the fiscal cost of the current gap (no ASE, no confirmed replacement) in terms of increased collision-related costs; Transport Canada's $36 billion national social-cost estimate [CL-0728] is too high-level to apportion to this specific policy gap.

Economic ROI

Range: Not yet estimable. Comparator source: None identified — no study located quantifying the broader economic effect of Ontario's province-wide ASE ban, positive or negative. Confidence: low — genuine gap.

Social ROI

Directional case only, but resting on a genuinely strong mechanistic base: the same speed/survivability evidence cited in RC-RSVZ-01 [CL-0752, CL-0753] applies here directly, since ASE's own demonstrated effect was reducing vehicle speeds in exactly the zones this evidence identifies as highest-stakes (school zones, where pedestrian survivability is most speed-sensitive). The 87% reduction in severe speeding specifically [NEW-3, figure corrected from 88%, independently re-verified 2026-07-16 by direct fetch of the underlying study] is a strong, independently-verified directional signal.

Environmental ROI

Range: Not applicable in the ordinary sense — this action's case is safety, not environmental. Comparator source: None sought. Confidence: high that no material environmental pathway exists either way for this specific ask.

Evidence

  • NEW-2 · quote-context (WebSearch synthesis, accessed 2026-07-14) · Bill 56 mechanics
  • NEW-7 · primary news coverage, directly fetched (CP24/ontario.ca, independently re-verified 2026-07-16) · the province's $210 million traffic-calming replacement commitment, $42 million immediate release; "$10 million to Toronto" sub-figure not confirmed against a primary source
  • NEW-3 · independently re-verified 2026-07-16, direct fetch of TMU press release and the underlying Injury Prevention study · ASE effectiveness evidence (45%/87% speeding reduction — corrected from 88%)
  • NEW-5 · quote-context (WebSearch synthesis, accessed 2026-07-14; 15.6% Guelph figure confirmed by direct fetch independently re-verified 2026-07-16 for the program's first-five-months period only — see backgrounder NEEDS-JUDGMENT note on a conflicting later 68% figure) · municipal and professional-body reaction to the ban, including OACP's "proven life-saving technology" characterization
  • NEW-6 · independently re-verified 2026-07-16 via Canadian Press/CHCH republication (original Trillium URL JS-gated) · Toronto's measured post-ban speeding increase, 101/104 locations
  • CL-0752, CL-0753 · VERIFY · speed/pedestrian-survivability and speed/fatal-risk mechanisms

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the problem statement (Bill 56's mechanics, the $210 million replacement commitment, and the ASE effectiveness evidence are all independently sourced, though the effectiveness figure traces through a vendor press release rather than the underlying study directly — see the backgrounder's own "thin or contested" flag on NEW-3). This card was revised 2026-07-14 after an adversarial verification pass found the province had, in fact, already named a costed, dated replacement commitment ($210 million, Nov. 13, 2025) that this card's earlier draft had stated did not exist — the card's ask is correspondingly narrowed from "name a commitment" to "account for how the named commitment is actually being deployed at the municipal/corridor level," since no source located in this pass confirms disbursement beyond the initial $10 million to Toronto. This card takes a position (corridor-level accounting should be demanded) because the safety evidence behind the removed tool is genuinely strong, even though this pass could not fully verify the vendor-sourced effectiveness figures against their own primary study.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming how Toronto's $10 million immediate allocation (and any further provincial tranche) has actually been spent; independently verifying the SickKids/TMU study's figures directly rather than via the vendor's press release; Lane R pass.
