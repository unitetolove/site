Shelter Central Intake Failure — Playbook

One phone line is the only door into Toronto's shelter system — years of data on how often callers actually get turned away.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

v2.0 · 2026-08-11

---

The honest bottom line

This doesn't tell you whether the 2025 improvement will survive if refugee-claimant demand rises again. It doesn't tell you how many people were turned away from Central Intake specifically because of the 2022-2023 policy, even when beds existed — the Ombudsman's own report says "at least some," without a count, and this document doesn't invent one. And it doesn't name which of the 14 comparator jurisdictions Toronto's own scan actually looked at.

a recommendation card — Publish the Named Jurisdictional Scan Behind the Access to Shelter Framework

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: shelter-central-intake-failure · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (backgrounder NEW-CIF-15)

Problem

The City's November 2025 status report states the Access to Shelter Framework was informed by "a jurisdictional scan of 14 regions (11 Canadian municipalities and three U.S.)" that "identified varied approaches to shelter access, including residency requirements, financial screening, and centralized access systems" [NEW-CIF-15]. The report as fetched in this review does not name which of the 14 jurisdictions use which specific model, meaning neither this page’s backgrounder nor any external reviewer can currently evaluate whether Toronto's own Central Intake design reflects best available practice or identify a specific comparator city's model to examine directly [backgrounder, "International context"]. This card addresses only the transparency gap in an already-completed piece of City research, not Central Intake's design itself.

Action

The City publishes the underlying jurisdictional-scan document (or a detailed summary naming each of the 14 regions and which specific access model each uses) as a standalone public document, rather than only the single summary sentence currently available in the November 2025 committee report.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: publish research the City has already conducted and already summarized in a public committee report — no new research required, and entirely within existing Housing Secretariat/TSSS communications and reporting authority.
  • City demands of Province: empty bucket — this action requires no provincial action.
  • City demands of Feds: empty bucket — this action requires no federal action.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low, a publication/disclosure action for research the City states it has already completed — comparator: the City's existing practice of publishing detailed budget notes, committee reports, and audit responses at comparable length and detail to what full publication of a 14-region scan would require.

Funding path

Existing Housing Secretariat/TSSS communications and reporting operating budget — a disclosure action on already-completed research, not a new research commission.

Who benefits, and how

Researchers, journalists, City Council, and the public, via the ability to independently evaluate whether Toronto's Central Intake and shelter-access design reflects identified best practice from comparator jurisdictions, rather than having to trust a single unelaborated summary sentence. Advocacy organizations engaged in the Ombudsman/CC28.2 process, via a fuller evidentiary record for evaluating the Access to Shelter Framework's own design choices.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, negligibly, via existing communications/reporting staff time; no new payer class identified.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — the backgrounder's own Cui Bono section states plainly that Central Intake is a City-operated telephone service, not a third-party-contracted operation, and no entity was identified in this review as profiting from the current disclosure gap specifically. This card carries no "who benefits from the status quo" line beyond this note.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Not directly estimable — a transparency/disclosure action on already-completed research, not a service-delivery or spending change, so it has no fiscal-return case of its own; the cost anchor (existing reporting practice) is not a fiscal-return figure. Confidence: low.

(b) Economic ROI

Not yet estimable. No source prices the economic value of publishing a specific municipal jurisdictional-scan document; broad open-government-data economic-value literature exists (cited elsewhere in this project's sibling pages) but isn't sized to this narrow a disclosure action, so it isn't re-cited here as if it were. Confidence: low.

(c) Social ROI

Directional: full publication would let independent reviewers assess whether Toronto's Central Intake design reflects identified best practice — directly relevant given this page’s own documented five-year pattern of unmatched callers and the Ombudsman's systemic-discrimination finding, both of which raise the stakes of getting intake design right [backgrounder, "Key tensions/tradeoffs"]. No source quantifies the downstream benefit of this specific disclosure. Confidence: low-medium — the value of transparency in a high-stakes, already-scrutinized system is well-argued, not quantified.

(d) Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a document-publication action with no construction, land-use, or physical-infrastructure component. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-CIF-15 · source quote (this review) · 14-region jurisdictional scan referenced but not detailed in the public report
  • NEW-CIF-9 · source quote (this review) · Ombudsman systemic-discrimination finding, establishing the stakes of intake-design scrutiny

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-high confidence in the problem statement (directly evidenced by this review's own primary-source fetch showing the scan is referenced but not detailed). Low confidence in all ROI dimensions except the directional Social ROI case — this is a disclosure action whose value is instrumental to future scrutiny, not itself measurable.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming whether a fuller jurisdictional-scan document already exists internally and simply wasn't published in the report this review fetched; fairness and legal review.

a recommendation card — A Central-Intake-Specific Attribution Study Separating Process Fix From Demand Decline

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: shelter-central-intake-failure · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (backgrounder NEW-CIF-1, NEW-CIF-2)

Problem

Central Intake's unmatched-caller count fell from 225/day to 155/day between October 2024 and October 2025, a real, multiply-sourced 31% improvement [NEW-CIF-1, NEW-CIF-2]. But this page’s own backgrounder states plainly that no source reviewed in this review directly attributes this decline to a Central-Intake-specific process change versus the separate, well-documented decline in refugee-claimant shelter demand over the same period (from 6,600 nightly in August 2024 to roughly 3,500 by August 2025, per the sibling shelter-system-capacity-strain backgrounder) [backgrounder, "Key tensions/tradeoffs"]. This is the same open attribution question the sibling page’s own a recommendation card card raises for system-wide Central Intake gains — this card addresses the Central-Intake-specific angle of that same unresolved question, focused on whether Central Intake's own matching mechanism (as opposed to system-wide capacity or demand) actually improved.

Action

TSSS commissions an attribution analysis specifically isolating Central Intake's own operational/process changes (call-handling procedures, the Access to Shelter Framework's 24 CC28.2 directives, the family placement list's own management change) from demand-side effects (falling refugee-claimant caseload, falling overall shelter demand) in explaining the 2024-2025 unmatched-caller decline — distinct from, and narrower than, the system-wide attribution study the sibling shelter-system-capacity-strain page’s a recommendation card card proposes, focused specifically on whether Central Intake's own matching mechanism improved.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: commission the analysis using data TSSS already collects (Central Intake call logs, CC28.2 directive completion tracking, refugee-claimant caseload data) — within existing budget-office/TSSS analytical capacity, no new authority required.
  • City demands of Province: none directly for this card's action.
  • City demands of Feds: none directly for this card's action.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low, an analytical/reporting exercise using existing TSSS data systems — comparator: the same scale of exercise as the sibling shelter-system-capacity-strain page’s a recommendation card card, which this card is deliberately narrower than (Central-Intake-specific rather than system-wide).

Funding path

Existing TSSS budget-office/analytical operating capacity — no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

People seeking shelter access via Central Intake, if the analysis identifies which of the 24 CC28.2 directives (15 already completed as of November 2025 [NEW-CIF-14]) actually drove matching improvement, allowing the City to prioritize continued investment in what works rather than assuming the whole framework is equally responsible. City Council and TSSS, via an evidence base distinguishing a durable process fix from a demand-side effect that could reverse if refugee-claimant or overall demand rises again.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, negligibly, via existing TSSS analytical staff time; no new payer class identified.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — see Card a recommendation card's identical note above. This card carries no "who benefits from the status quo" line beyond this note.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Not separately estimated; the point of this card is to produce the information needed to evaluate which CC28.2 investments are working, not to assert a fiscal-return figure in advance. Confidence: low — genuinely instrumental to a later decision, not itself a cost/savings action.

(b) Economic ROI

Not directly modeled — an internal analytical exercise with no first-order induced-spending or employment effect of its own; the same category of action as the sibling page’s a recommendation card card, which reached the same "not yet estimable" conclusion for the same reason. Confidence: low.

(c) Social ROI

Directional: distinguishing a durable Central-Intake-specific fix from a demand-side artifact protects the population most directly served by the unmatched-caller metric — people phoning Central Intake seeking a bed — from an undiagnosed reversal if refugee-claimant or general demand rises again, echoing this page’s own documented five-year pattern of turn-aways before the 2025 improvement (117/day 2020-2021 to 174-225/day by 2023-2024) [backgrounder, "Five years of unmatched-caller data"]. Confidence: low-medium — the underlying risk is well-evidenced by this page’s own historical data; the specific attribution is not yet known, which is exactly what this card proposes to find out.

(d) Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — an internal analytical/reporting exercise with no construction, land-use, or physical-infrastructure component. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-CIF-1 · source quote (this review) · 225/day → 155/day unmatched-caller decline, Oct 2024-Oct 2025
  • NEW-CIF-2 · source quote (this review) · 31% reduction, corroborated in 2026 Budget Notes
  • NEW-CIF-14 · source quote (this review) · 15 of 24 CC28.2 directives completed as of November 2025
  • CL-0101 · verified (claims register) · 2025 AG winter-audit: 174 daily unmatched callers

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-low confidence on the underlying diagnosis (this card explicitly proposes producing the missing evidence rather than assuming an answer); high confidence on the underlying facts it's built from (the 2025 improvement and the CC28.2 directive-completion tracking are both directly quoted from City documents). This card is deliberately scoped narrower than, and complementary to, the sibling shelter-system-capacity-strain page’s a recommendation card card — the two should not be treated as duplicates.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: fairness and legal review; confirmation that TSSS does not already produce this Central-Intake-specific attribution analysis internally (this review did not locate one); coordination with the sibling page’s a recommendation card to avoid commissioning two overlapping studies.
