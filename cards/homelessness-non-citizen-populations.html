Non-Citizen Populations: Population Data and Service Eligibility — Playbook

How many people in Toronto shelters aren't citizens, by immigration status, and which services each group can actually access.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can actually do on the non-citizen populations in its shelter system — each move with its costs, its beneficiaries, and its receipts.

v2.0 · 2026-08-11

---

The honest bottom line

Two very different kinds of non-citizens show up in Toronto's shelter system, and the City's own data can only see one of them clearly. Refugee claimants must disclose status to access claimant-specific supports, so Toronto's numbers here are solid: nationally this group made up 6.6% of Canadian shelter users in 2024, up from 5.1% the year before and just 0.9% in 2021; Toronto's own share was 53% in October 2024, roughly eight times the national rate. That's not a stable number, though — by August-September 2025, Toronto's nightly refugee-claimant shelter population had fallen from a peak around 6,500-6,600 to roughly 3,400-3,500, tracking a 34% drop in national asylum claims, not a Toronto-specific policy shift. A real federal program, IHAP, funds a response to this population, because refugee claimants are by definition inside an active federal process. Toronto has also had a sanctuary-city policy since 2013 — Access T.O. — that tells staff not to ask about immigration status at all, which means the City's own data structurally cannot say how many undocumented people are in its shelters, because nobody is tracking that category. The federal government's own national estimate for the undocumented population runs between 500,000 and nearly a million — a wide range, because nobody can count this population precisely anywhere in the country. And there is no federal program at all for this group — not an underfunded one, none. A federal law that received Royal Assent in March 2026, Bill C-12, creates a documented mechanism that could push people from the counted, funded population into the uncounted, unfunded one: as of April 2026, 30,000 people nationally had already received a letter warning they might be barred from a full refugee hearing.

Publish a Toronto-specific version of the federal shelter study's own citizenship breakdown. (a recommendation card) The National Shelter Study reports citizens, resettled refugees/asylum seekers, permanent residents, and visa holders as separate categories nationally — but excludes domestic-violence shelters, immigrant/refugee-specific shelters, and transitional housing "due to insufficient data coverage." Toronto's 53% headline figure isn't disaggregated into those same categories, so it can't be compared to the national trend on equal terms. TSSS already collects most of the underlying data for IHAP administration; this asks for it published in the same categorical format the federal study uses.

Track Bill C-12's Toronto-specific impact before it shows up as an unexplained increase in an uncountable population. (a recommendation card) Someone who loses claimant status under Bill C-12 doesn't stop needing a shelter bed — they just move from the population the City counts and the federal government funds into the population the City can't count and nobody funds. Nobody has yet established how many of the 30,000 national letter recipients are in Toronto, or when any resulting shelter demand would show up. Using the data relationship TSSS already has with IRCC through IHAP, this card asks the City to track that transition as it happens rather than discover it later as an unexplained aggregate increase.

Neither move asks Toronto to abandon its sanctuary policy or start tracking immigration status directly. They ask it to get better at measuring what it can measure, and to watch, in advance, for the specific mechanism that could quietly grow the population it can't. What this doesn't settle: how big the without-status population actually is in Toronto's shelters — that number doesn't exist honestly, and manufacturing one would be worse than not having it. There is real news pulling the other way, too — federal permit cuts for international students and temporary workers (down from over 305,000 to around 150,000 for 2026) and a new May 2026 rule letting people who lose status apply to stay as visitors rather than falling directly into undocumented status — both of which cut against, not toward, this population becoming a larger pressure on Toronto's shelters going forward.

a recommendation card — A Toronto-Specific Supplement to the National Shelter Study's Citizenship Breakdown

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: homelessness-non-citizen-populations · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (backgrounder NEW-2026-NC-2, NEW-2026-NC-3)

Problem

The federal National Shelter Study provides a national citizenship/status breakdown of shelter users (85.7% citizens, 6.6% resettled refugees/asylum seekers, 3.4% permanent residents, 1.6% visa holders in 2024) [NEW-2026-NC-2], but excludes domestic violence shelters, immigrant/refugee-specific shelters, and transitional housing "due to insufficient data coverage" — a methodological gap that may materially affect comparability with Toronto's own dedicated refugee-shelter system. Toronto's 53% October 2024 refugee-claimant share is independently sourced but not directly disaggregated into the same permanent-resident/visa-holder/undocumented categories the national study uses [NEW-2026-NC-3]. This card addresses the resulting comparability gap.

Action

TSSS publishes a Toronto-specific breakdown of its shelter-using population by the same immigration-status categories the National Shelter Study uses (citizens, permanent residents, resettled refugees/asylum seekers, visa holders), covering both the base shelter system and the dedicated refugee-shelter system, so Toronto-vs-national comparisons are apples-to-apples rather than relying on a single headline refugee-claimant percentage.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: TSSS already collects refugee-claimant status data for IHAP-funded programming; this asks for its publication in the same categorical format the federal study uses, alongside the base-system breakdown Access T.O.'s no-status-inquiry design already limits — within existing municipal reporting authority.
  • City demands of Province: none required.
  • City demands of Feds: coordination with Housing, Infrastructure and Communities Canada to ensure Toronto's dedicated refugee-shelter system data is captured in future National Shelter Study updates, given the current stated exclusion of "immigrant and refugee shelters."

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low — a reporting-format alignment using data TSSS substantially already collects for IHAP administration; comparator: the federal study's own established categorical framework, which this card proposes adopting rather than inventing a new one.

Funding path

Existing TSSS data/reporting capacity; potential federal data-sharing coordination through the existing National Shelter Study relationship, no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

Researchers, journalists, and policymakers, via a Toronto-specific figure directly comparable to the national trend rather than a single percentage requiring external context to interpret; Council, via better information for assessing whether Toronto's disproportion is narrowing or widening as national and local trends both shift.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, negligibly, via existing TSSS data/reporting staff time.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder.

(a) Financial ROI

Not applicable — a data-reporting action, not a spending or savings program; none needed to support that finding. Confidence: low — not the right unit for this action.

(b) Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — no comparator identified. Confidence: low.

(c) Social ROI

Directional: more accurate, comparable data supports better-targeted service planning across immigration-status categories, though no claim in the claims register quantifies this specific mechanism's effect. Confidence: low.

(d) Environmental ROI

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-NC-2 · source quote (this review) · National Shelter Study 2024 citizenship breakdown and methodology exclusions
  • NEW-2026-NC-3 · source quote (this review) · Toronto's 53% October 2024 refugee-claimant share

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the underlying comparability gap (directly documented in the federal study's own stated methodology); low confidence on how much of TSSS's existing data collection could be repurposed without new systems, since this review did not review TSSS's own internal data schema.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming TSSS's existing data-collection scope; fairness and legal review.

a recommendation card — A Toronto-Specific Bill C-12 Impact Monitor

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: homelessness-non-citizen-populations · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: carried-forward (backgrounder NEW-2026-NC-10)

Problem

Bill C-12 (Royal Assent March 26, 2026) creates new eligibility bars affecting up to 30,000 asylum seekers nationally as of April 2026, with a direct, mechanical pathway from the IHAP-funded refugee-claimant category into the entirely-unfunded without-status category this page’s backgrounder documents [NEW-2026-NC-10]. How many of the 30,000 letter recipients are in Toronto, how many will be found ineligible, and what shelter-demand timeline would follow are all explicitly unknown per this page’s own carried-forward sources. This card addresses the monitoring gap, not the underlying federal policy.

Action

TSSS establishes a standing quarterly tracking item — using data it can obtain through its existing IHAP administrative relationship with IRCC — on the number of Toronto-area refugee claimants who have received Bill C-12 procedural fairness letters, been found ineligible for a full RPD hearing, or transitioned into shelter use without refugee-claimant status as a result, so that any shelter-demand shift is caught early rather than discovered only after it appears as an unexplained increase in the without-status population this project's own data limitations (Access T.O.'s no-status-inquiry design) would otherwise make invisible.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: establish the internal tracking item, within existing TSSS administrative capacity.
  • City demands of Province: none directly required.
  • City demands of Feds: IRCC data-sharing specific to Toronto-area Bill C-12 procedural-fairness-letter recipients, framed as an extension of the existing IHAP data relationship rather than a wholly new ask.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low — a monitoring/tracking item using an existing federal-municipal data relationship (IHAP); comparator: the scale of existing IHAP administrative data exchange TSSS already participates in.

Funding path

Existing TSSS administrative capacity; no new funding mechanism required for the monitoring function itself.

Who benefits, and how

Refugee claimants transitioning into the without-status category, via earlier service-planning awareness rather than being absorbed invisibly into a population the City's own sanctuary policy cannot count by design; TSSS and Council, via early warning of a shelter-demand shift specific to a named, dated federal policy change rather than an unexplained aggregate increase.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, negligibly, via existing TSSS/IHAP administrative staff time.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Not directly estimable — a monitoring action; any downstream budget-planning benefit (earlier warning of a funding-eligibility shift) is plausible but not modeled here. Confidence: low.

(b) Economic ROI

Not applicable. Confidence: low.

(c) Social ROI

Directional: earlier awareness of a documented, named risk pathway (IHAP-funded to unfunded) plausibly supports better service continuity for an affected population already established elsewhere in this project as facing elevated housing precarity risk. Confidence: low-medium.

(d) Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-NC-10 · source quote (this review) · Bill C-12 eligibility bars, 30,000 procedural fairness letters nationally

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the underlying risk mechanism (directly sourced from IRCC notices via this page’s carried-forward sources); low confidence on whether IRCC would actually share Toronto-specific Bill C-12 data through the existing IHAP relationship, since this review did not confirm the scope of that data-sharing arrangement.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming the scope of the existing IHAP data-sharing relationship; fairness and legal review.
