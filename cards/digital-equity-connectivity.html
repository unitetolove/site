Digital Equity and Connectivity — Playbook

Cost, not coverage, is why some Torontonians still can't get online — and how little the City alone can fix it.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can do to close the gap between what the City says it's doing on digital access and what it can actually show it's doing.

The honest bottom line

Toronto already has an answer to "does the City have a Wi-Fi program" — yes, and it's bigger than it was last year. What it doesn't have, despite an earlier briefing saying otherwise, is a broadband subsidy for people on Ontario Works or ODSP. That's not a small correction. It's the difference between "the City is building something" and "the City is pointing people toward programs it doesn't run." ConnectTO offers free public Wi-Fi at more than 250 City-owned locations — community centres, TCHC buildings, civic centres, public squares, even a handful of shelters — plus all 100 library branches, up from 100 sites total back in 2021. By the City's own count, 89 percent of those sites sit in neighbourhoods the City itself has flagged as needing the most support, with about 7,600 connections a day in 2024, seven in ten of them repeat visits to the same spot. But checking the City's own most recent program update — July 2025 — there's no City-funded subsidy in the works. What the City is actually doing is pointing OW and ODSP recipients toward two programs it doesn't run and doesn't pay for: the federal Connecting Families program ($20 a month, 200 GB, since 2022) and Rogers' own Connected for Success program. The City's own words for its 2025 activity: "updated the Human Services website with new links" and "developing a strategy" to mention these programs when people contact the City for other reasons. That's outreach, not a subsidy — worth being plain about, because a resident reading the earlier framing would reasonably expect a City cheque or a City-negotiated rate that doesn't exist. How big is the problem, actually? The City's own numbers don't agree with each other: one figure, from the City's own commissioned research, says about 58,000 Torontonians — roughly 2 percent of the city — lack affordable home internet; a different, older framing says something closer to 40 percent of the lowest-income households don't have it. Those aren't the same question, and nothing found in this review reconciles them, so both are stated here, honestly unresolved. The federal side has seen real movement — the CRTC opened fibre access to smaller providers in 2025 and locked in final wholesale pricing in April 2026 — but none of it is aimed at the specific population this page cares about, and the underlying two-network problem (phone company or cable company, nothing else) hasn't gone away: independent providers' market share was cut in half between 2020 and 2024, from 8.4 percent to 4.2 percent, a pattern Canada's own Competition Bureau has flagged directly. Neither card below touches the actual affordability problem, because that's a national telecom-market and federal-regulation question the City can influence only by asking, not by acting. Neither creates a City subsidy — that would be a bigger, different proposal than either of these. What both do is close a smaller, more honest gap: the gap between having a Wi-Fi program and knowing whether the people who need it most are actually being reached by it.

a recommendation card — A Measured, Funded Awareness Campaign for Existing OW/ODSP-Eligible Internet Subsidies

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: digital-equity-connectivity · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-4)

Problem

This page’s backgrounder establishes, via direct fetch of the City's own July 2025 ConnectTO Program Update, that no City-administered OW/ODSP broadband subsidy has launched or is in development — correcting this page’s inherited briefing's "exploring a subsidy" framing. The City's actual current posture is referral: the Human Services Integration office promotes enrollment in the pre-existing federal Connecting Families Initiative and the private-carrier Rogers Connected for Success program, both of which already cover ODSP/OW/GIS recipients [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-4]. The City's own stated 2025 action is limited to "updated the Human Services website with new links" and "developing a strategy for proactive services recommendations" [NEW-2026-1] — neither is a measured, funded, or targeted awareness campaign. A UK comparator shows a structurally similar referral-only mechanism (Ofcom-regulated social tariffs) suffers roughly 70% non-awareness among eligible households despite availability, with only ~8.6% uptake. This card addresses the risk that Toronto's own referral-only posture will replicate that same low-uptake failure mode without ever measuring whether it did.

Action

The City funds and runs a dedicated, measured awareness campaign — distinct from the general "sharing information" activity already described in the July 2025 report — targeting OW/ODSP recipients' enrollment in Connecting Families and Connected for Success, delivered through existing Human Services Integration channels, with a before/after enrollment-rate measurement built in from the start. This does not require a new subsidy — only real measurement of whether the current referral-only strategy reaches the population it's meant to reach.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: design and fund a measured awareness campaign using the existing Human Services Integration office and OW/ODSP caseworker touchpoints the City already operates — entirely within existing municipal service-delivery authority.
  • City demands of Province: that the Ministry providing OW/ODSP administration cooperate in identifying an appropriate, privacy-compliant channel to reach eligible recipients directly (e.g., an insert with existing benefit correspondence) rather than relying solely on recipients proactively visiting the Human Services website — a cooperation ask, not a funding demand.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified; Connecting Families itself is a federal program the City is not asking to be changed by this card — only that the City's own outreach into it be measured.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low (a communications/outreach campaign layered onto an existing service-delivery channel, not a new program with new caseworkers or new infrastructure), anchored to the general order of magnitude of the City's own existing "public awareness campaign" already committed for Q3 2025 for ConnectTO Wi-Fi generally [NEW-2026-1] — this card asks for the same campaign discipline (funded, planned, delivered) applied specifically to the subsidy-enrollment question, which the July 2025 report's own language ("sharing information," "developing a strategy") suggests has not yet received the same level of committed resourcing as the Wi-Fi awareness campaign.

Funding path

Existing Social Development/Human Services Integration operating budget, using the same administrative and communications channel already funded for the Q3 2025 ConnectTO Wi-Fi awareness campaign [NEW-2026-1]; no new funding source identified as strictly necessary, though a real budget-line figure for the existing Wi-Fi campaign was not located in this review and should be sourced before this card advances past DRAFT.

Who benefits, and how

OW/ODSP recipients who are eligible for but not currently enrolled in Connecting Families or Connected for Success, via increased actual enrollment (not just information availability) in subsidies they already qualify for; City Council and the public, via a real, measured answer to whether referral-only outreach works in Toronto's specific context, rather than an assumption either way.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, via the Social Development operating budget; no cost is imposed on OW/ODSP recipients themselves or on the carriers/federal program, since this card does not touch subsidy terms, only outreach and measurement.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified specific to referral-only outreach itself. The backgrounder's Cui Bono finding (Competition Bureau's two-network duopoly framing) concerns the broader telecom market structure, not the specific referral-versus-subsidy design question this card addresses.

Financial ROI

Low avoided cost, likely in the low tens of thousands CAD for campaign design/delivery, offset partially by reduced downstream city-service costs if more residents gain reliable internet access to City digital services (unquantified in this review). The City's own existing, already-funded Q3 2025 ConnectTO Wi-Fi awareness campaign [NEW-2026-1] is the closest named comparator for cost scale, though its own specific budget figure was not located. Confidence: low.

Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — a live search for a municipal subsidy-enrollment-campaign economic-impact study did not surface one; the broader gig-economy/labour-market multiplier literature reviewed elsewhere in this project is not on point for a subsidy-awareness intervention specifically, so is not named here as a stretched comparator. Confidence: low — genuine gap, not modelled.

Social ROI

Directional: increasing actual (not just theoretical) subsidy enrollment among OW/ODSP recipients plausibly narrows the specific "58,000 people, ~2% of Torontonians, lack affordable in-home internet" gap the City's own July 2025 report names [NEW-2026-1] — though this card does not assert what share of that 58,000 are OW/ODSP recipients specifically, which was not confirmed in this review. Consistent with the UK comparator's own finding that awareness campaigns for structurally similar programs face a real, documented uptake ceiling even where available.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a communications/outreach campaign layered onto an existing service channel has no plausible emissions, land-use, water, waste, or resilience effect. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-1 · source quote (backgrounder) · City's own referral-only 2025 posture; existing Q3 2025 Wi-Fi awareness campaign as cost comparator; 58,000-person divide figure
  • NEW-2026-4 · source quote (backgrounder) · Connected for Success ODSP/OW/GIS eligibility, independently confirmed
  • backgrounder, International context §2(b) · UK Ofcom social-tariff awareness gap (~70% unaware, ~8.6% uptake), cited as the risk this card responds to

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence this is within existing municipal service-delivery authority (the City already operates the HSI referral channel). Low confidence on cost, since no specific existing-campaign budget figure was located. The core premise — that measurement would reveal a meaningful awareness gap in Toronto specifically, as it did in the UK — is a reasonable inference from a structurally similar comparator, not a Toronto-specific finding; this card's own recommendation to measure is partly justified by the absence of existing Toronto-specific enrollment data, which is itself the gap being addressed.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: a real cost figure for the existing Wi-Fi awareness campaign as comparator; confirming what share of Toronto's disconnected 58,000 are OW/ODSP recipients specifically; fairness and legal review.

a recommendation card — A Published, Trackable Device Gap Metric Alongside ConnectTO's Existing Site-Count Reporting

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: digital-equity-connectivity · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (NEW-2026-5, NEW-2026-9) + carried-forward (four-legs framing)

Problem

This page’s inherited briefing names devices as one of four linked legs of the digital divide (access, affordability, devices, skills), alongside a documented finding that low-income households disproportionately depend on mobile-only access rather than a full computer [independently corroborated in this review by a StatsCan-derived finding that 24% of lowest-income-quartile households with children rely on mobile devices for internet access, versus 8% of the highest quartile, NEW-2026-5]. Toronto's own ConnectTO reporting is comprehensive on Wi-Fi site counts, daily connections, and session lengths, but this review did not locate a comparable, published, City-tracked device-gap headcount — a specific number of Torontonians who have Wi-Fi access but no adequate device to use it. By contrast, San Jose's SJ Access program publishes and tracks a specific device-gap figure (25,500 households "digitally disadvantaged," meaning no device or smartphone-only) [NEW-2026-9]. This card addresses only the measurement gap — it does not itself propose a device-distribution program, which belongs to the libraries-civic-commons leaf.

Action

The City adds a published device-access metric to its existing, already-funded ConnectTO research program — the same TMU/Dais-partnered research relationship that already produces the City's site-count and usage figures [NEW-2026-1] — specifically tracking how many Torontonians have Wi-Fi/connectivity access but lack an adequate device (mirroring San Jose's "digitally disadvantaged" category), reported alongside the existing site-count and connection metrics in future ConnectTO program updates.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: add a device-gap question/metric to the existing, already-commissioned TMU/Dais research relationship the City uses for its ConnectTO reporting [NEW-2026-1] — an extension of an existing, funded research contract, not a new authority or new program.
  • City demands of Province: none identified; device access is not a provincially-occupied policy area the way labour standards or health care are.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low (an additional research question added to an already-commissioned, recurring TMU/Dais research engagement the City already funds for its 2025-2026 ConnectTO research cycle, explicitly planned per the July 2025 report's own commitment to "commission further research in 2025-2026 to investigate the digital divide" [NEW-2026-1]) — this card asks only that a device-specific metric be included in research that is happening regardless.

Funding path

Existing Technology Services/ConnectTO research budget, riding on the already-planned 2025-2026 TMU/Dais research commission [NEW-2026-1]; no new funding source identified as necessary.

Who benefits, and how

City Council and the public, via a measurable, trackable device-gap figure that can inform future device-lending program scale (a program libraries-civic-commons already documents TPL as running, but without, per that page’s own backgrounder, a citywide device-gap denominator to measure against); residents in the "digitally disadvantaged" category, indirectly, via better-targeted future device programming once the gap is actually measured rather than assumed.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, via the Technology Services research budget; negligible marginal cost since the underlying research contract is already funded and planned.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified. A missing device-gap metric does not clearly benefit any identifiable private entity — this is a measurement gap, not an extraction pattern.

Financial ROI

Not separately estimated; this is a research-scope addition to an already-funded contract, not a program with a direct fiscal-offset case.

Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — a measurement addition has no direct modelled economic effect of its own. Confidence: low — genuine non-applicability, not a modelled gap.

Social ROI

Directional: better-targeted future device-lending and distribution programming (run by libraries and community partners) plausibly follows from having an actual measured gap to target, rather than allocating device resources without a denominator — San Jose's own program demonstrates this is a trackable, reportable metric in practice [NEW-2026-9], though this card does not claim Toronto's own device-lending outcomes would improve by any specific amount, since that depends on programming decisions this card does not itself make.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — an added research question within an already-planned research contract has no physical footprint. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • carried-forward · four-legs (access/affordability/devices/skills) framing; devices as an under-funded leg
  • NEW-2026-1 · source quote (backgrounder) · City's own already-planned 2025-2026 TMU/Dais research commission
  • NEW-2026-5 · source quote (backgrounder, ⚠️ still being checked secondary source) · 24%-vs-8% mobile-dependency-by-income finding
  • NEW-2026-9 · WebSearch-derived (backgrounder) · San Jose SJ Access's own published device-gap metric (25,500 "digitally disadvantaged" households)

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-low confidence overall. The comparator (San Jose) is real and its metric-publishing practice is documented, but this review did not confirm that Toronto's existing TMU/Dais research contract could readily add this metric without additional scope/cost the City has not yet agreed to — a genuine open question this card does not resolve. The NEW-2026-5 finding underlying the "devices" problem statement is itself flagged ⚠️ still being checked pending primary-source StatsCan confirmation.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming with Technology Services whether the existing TMU/Dais research contract scope can add a device-gap question without a separate procurement; primary-source verification of NEW-2026-5; fairness and legal review.
