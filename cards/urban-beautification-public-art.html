a recommendation card — Correct StART and public-facing City messaging that attributes a crime-reduction effect to murals — Recommendation Card

Card id: a recommendation card · Status: DRAFT · Backgrounder: urban-beautification-public-art Provenance: New load-bearing findings (problem framing) / NEW (cost anchors) · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

This page’s own backgrounder independently fetched the actual University of Cincinnati study behind the "murals reduce crime via eyes-on-the-street" claim that appears in this page’s carried-forward master briefing, and found the study itself states plainly: "The study found no statistically significant relationship between murals and either violent or nonviolent crime rates" (University of Cincinnati, "Murals boost Cincinnati's vitality, community development," July 9, 2025; fetched live 2026-07-14, cited in the backgrounder's "Current state" and "Key tensions" sections). This is not a claim that murals have no value — the same study found a real, statistically significant foot-traffic effect, controlling for demographic and density factors. It is narrowly a finding that the specific crime-reduction mechanism, if asserted publicly by the City in connection with its own StART program, would be citing research that does not support it. This card addresses only that narrow correction risk, not StART's funding or scope.

Action

Direct StART/City Planning communications staff to review any current public-facing StART materials for a crime-reduction claim attributed to murals and, if found, correct it to state the foot-traffic finding only (with the crime-neutral finding disclosed alongside it, not omitted) — a plain accuracy correction, not a policy change to the program itself.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: reviewing and correcting its own communications materials is entirely within existing municipal administrative capacity; StART already maintains public program webpages that could be corrected at negligible cost.
  • City demands of Province: none identified for this specific action.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified for this specific action.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: negligible — a communications/copy-editing correction to existing webpages, not a new program or study. No comparator cost figure is needed or asserted; the template's own guidance permits omitting a manufactured range where the action is genuinely low-cost and administrative.

Funding path

Existing City Planning/StART communications staff time; no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

Residents and journalists relying on the City's own public materials, via accurate information rather than an oversold safety claim; the City's own institutional credibility, via closing a documented gap between what a cited study actually found and what public messaging has (per the master briefing checked in this page) previously claimed on its authority.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via negligible existing staff time; no disproportionate payer identified.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table. The backgrounder's own Cui Bono section found this library's internal records contains no public-art- or mural-specific entry, and no this library's internal records/ directory yet exists in this repository — this card's own action responds to a documented factual-accuracy gap, not a named beneficiary of the current messaging.

Financial ROI

Not modeled — a communications-accuracy action, not a spending program. Comparator source: none required for a negligible-cost administrative correction. Confidence: low — stated as a plausible negligible-cost action rather than a modeled figure.

Economic ROI

Range: Not yet estimable. Comparator source: none identified. Confidence: low — a narrow accuracy correction of this scope has no obvious first-order economic effect.

Social ROI

Directional only: the actual UC study's own confirmed, statistically significant finding — foot traffic, not crime — is real evidence for a genuine (if narrower) social case for murals; correcting the overstated crime claim protects public trust in the City's own evidence base rather than adding a new social benefit of its own. Comparator source: University of Cincinnati, "Murals boost Cincinnati's vitality, community development," July 9, 2025 (foot-traffic finding, statistically significant controlling for demographic/density factors; crime finding, no statistically significant relationship either direction). Confidence: low-medium — the underlying foot-traffic finding is real and sourced; the correction's own effect on public trust is not separately measured.

Environmental ROI

Range: Genuinely environmentally neutral — a communications correction has no construction, land-use, or maintenance effect of its own. Comparator source: none required. Confidence: low — stated plainly per the template's guidance that "genuinely environmentally neutral" is a valid, honest fill.

Evidence

  • NEW (2026-07-14) · University of Cincinnati, "Murals boost Cincinnati's vitality, community development," July 9, 2025 · foot-traffic finding (positive, significant) and crime finding (no significant relationship, either direction) — the single most important finding in this page’s backgrounder
  • NEW (2026-07-14) · Backgrounder's own "Key tensions / tradeoffs" section · documents the master briefing's "eyes on the street... reduces crime" framing as unsupported by the study it cites

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-high confidence that a real, documented factual gap exists between the master briefing's framing and the actual study's findings — the backgrounder independently fetched and quoted the primary source. Low confidence on whether any current live StART public-facing material actually repeats the crime-reduction claim verbatim; this card's own action is preventive/corrective rather than responding to a confirmed current instance of the error in a specific City webpage, which was not independently checked this review.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: fairness and legal review; an independent check of current live StART public-facing pages for the specific crime-reduction language, which this page’s backgrounder did not itself perform (its own fetch targeted the StART program-description page and the UC study, not every StART sub-page).

a recommendation card — Commission a Toronto-specific evaluation of StART's actual foot-traffic, safety, and property-value effects, modeled on the Cincinnati study design — Recommendation Card

Card id: a recommendation card · Status: DRAFT · Backgrounder: urban-beautification-public-art Provenance: New load-bearing findings (problem framing) / NEW (cost anchors) · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

The backgrounder's own "International context" section states plainly that "whether any local academic partnership already evaluates StART's actual effects on foot traffic, safety, or property values was not established one way or the other this review — named as an open question, not an existing gap or an existing program." Toronto currently relies on StART's own stated goals (the City's language that StART programs "make our streets more beautiful and safe... and reduce overall infrastructure maintenance costs") as the only public description of the program's effects — the backgrounder is explicit that these are "presented by the City as the program's aims, not independently verified as achieved outcomes by this review's sources." This card addresses that specific evidence gap.

Action

Commission a university research partnership (following the Cincinnati precedent: a local university, partly funded by a competitive grant, studying an existing mural program using foot-traffic, crime, and demographic data the City already collects or can obtain) to evaluate StART's actual measured effects on foot traffic, crime, and neighbourhood property-value/demographic change in Toronto specifically, rather than relying on the program's stated goals as evidence of achievement.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: initiating and partly funding a research partnership with a Toronto-area university is within existing municipal capacity (comparable to how City Planning already commissions studies); providing StART program location/funding data to the research partner.
  • City demands of Province: none identified as required for this specific action, though a provincial arts-research grant stream could plausibly co-fund it — not independently confirmed this review.
  • City demands of Feds: the Cincinnati precedent study was itself "extended by a National Endowment for the Arts grant" (University of Cincinnati release, July 2025) — Canada's equivalent federal body, the Canada Council for the Arts, is named here as a plausible parallel funding source by analogy to the U.S. precedent, not as a confirmed Canadian program; flagged as unconfirmed rather than asserted.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low, likely low-to-mid hundreds of thousands of dollars CAD for a multi-year academic research partnership, anchored loosely to the general scale of comparable City- or university-commissioned urban-policy evaluation studies rather than a specific cited unit cost for the Cincinnati study itself (the UC release does not disclose its own study's budget) — this range is a rough order-of-magnitude estimate, not a sourced figure, and is flagged as such.

Funding path

A blended City Planning/StART study budget plus a potential post-secondary or arts-research grant match (by analogy to the Cincinnati study's National Endowment for the Arts extension); no specific Canadian funding program confirmed this review.

Who benefits, and how

Toronto residents and City Council, via an actual evidence base for StART's real effects rather than the program's own stated aims; StART itself, via either a confirmed positive-effect finding it could cite with confidence, or an honest finding that some of its stated goals (e.g., "reduce overall infrastructure maintenance costs") are not currently measured or supported — either outcome is a genuine improvement over the current state of not knowing.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via the City Planning/StART study budget; a research-grant funder (if secured) bears a share, per the Cincinnati-precedent funding structure named above.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — the backgrounder's own Cui Bono section found no this library's internal records/ entries exist for this topic and no live-discovery search surfaced a specific beneficiary entity; this card responds to a documented evidence gap, not a claim that a specific party benefits from StART remaining unevaluated.

Financial ROI

Not modeled directly. Directionally, the backgrounder notes StART's own stated goal of reducing "overall infrastructure maintenance costs" is an unverified claim — if a future Toronto-specific study confirmed a real avoided-maintenance-cost effect (as opposed to merely a stated aim), that would be a genuine avoided-cost case; this card does not assert that effect exists, only that the proposed study is the necessary first step to know. Comparator source: none — explicitly sequenced as "evaluate first, cost the effect second," matching the Cincinnati study's own retrospective-evaluation design. Confidence: low.

Economic ROI

Range: Not yet estimable. Comparator source: none identified for Toronto specifically; the Cincinnati study itself was not designed to produce a dollar-figure economic-impact estimate (it reports statistical associations, not a multiplier), so it is not usable as an Economic ROI comparator even by analogy. Confidence: low — genuinely contingent on a study that does not yet exist.

Social ROI

Directional: the Cincinnati study's own confirmed foot-traffic finding is real, positive, and controls for demographic/density factors — a plausible basis to expect a similar effect could be found in Toronto, though the same study just as clearly found no crime-reduction effect, a hedge this card preserves rather than assumes away. Whether Toronto's own StART murals would show the same foot-traffic pattern is not established by a study of Cincinnati; this is stated as a hypothesis the proposed evaluation would test, not a transferred finding. Comparator source: University of Cincinnati, "Murals boost Cincinnati's vitality, community development," July 9, 2025. Confidence: low-medium — directional plausibility from a real, comparable-city precedent; not a Toronto-specific finding.

Environmental ROI

Range: Not yet estimable — a this library's internal records action has no direct construction or land-use footprint of its own; any downstream environmental effect would depend entirely on what the study finds and what the City does in response, neither of which exists yet. Comparator source: none identified. Confidence: low.

Evidence

  • NEW (2026-07-14) · Backgrounder's own "International context" and "Open questions" sections · confirms no Toronto-specific StART evaluation study was identified this review
  • NEW (2026-07-14) · City of Toronto, "StreetARToronto" webpage (last modified August 7, 2025) · StART's own stated goals (safety, reduced maintenance costs), explicitly flagged by the backgrounder as aims, not verified outcomes
  • NEW (2026-07-14) · University of Cincinnati, "Murals boost Cincinnati's vitality, community development," July 9, 2025 · the precedent study design this card proposes replicating

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence that this is a genuine, documented evidence gap (the backgrounder's own live-discovery pass, targeted specifically at this question, found nothing); low confidence on cost and all four ROI dimensions, since the proposed action is explicitly a fact-finding study rather than a costed remediation program. This card takes the position that evaluating StART's real effects is itself the correct next action, not that any specific effect (positive or negative) is already confirmed for Toronto.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: fairness and legal review; confirmation of whether any Toronto university already has a relevant urban-planning research program that could be approached, which this page’s backgrounder explicitly did not resolve.

a recommendation card — Require and publish a public-art-specific breakout of Percent for Public Art Reserve Fund contributions and spending, disaggregated from broader Section 37 community-benefit figures — Recommendation Card

Card id: a recommendation card · Status: DRAFT · Backgrounder: urban-beautification-public-art Provenance: New load-bearing findings (problem framing) / NEW (cost anchors) · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

The backgrounder's own "Open questions / data gaps" section names this directly as "genuinely uncovered": "the current annual dollar value of Percent for Public Art contributions specifically (as distinct from the broader Section 37 community-benefit figure cited from the sibling leaf, which is not public-art-specific)." The backgrounder is also explicit that the Percent for Public Art Program's funding scale "tracks where and how much private development occurs, not a jurisdiction-wide equalized allocation, a structural feature named here as a fact about the mechanism's design" — a structural point that cannot currently be checked against real numbers because no public-art-specific figure, by ward or citywide, is disaggregated from the broader $123.9 million Section 37 figure cited in the sibling parks-recreation-access leaf. This card addresses only the transparency gap, not whether the underlying development-linked funding structure itself should change.

Action

Direct City Planning to publish an annual public-art-specific breakout of Percent for Public Art Reserve Fund contributions and disbursements (on-site vs. off-site, and off-site by ward collected/spent), separate from the broader Section 37 community-benefit reporting it currently appears folded into.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: this is a pure reporting/transparency action using data (Reserve Fund contribution and disbursement records) the City already administers through City Planning and the Toronto Public Art Commission; entirely within existing municipal administrative capacity.
  • City demands of Province: none identified for this specific action.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified for this specific action.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: negligible to low — a reporting/disaggregation task using data the City already collects, comparable in scale to Card 1's communications-correction action; no comparator cost figure is asserted, consistent with the template's guidance that not every card needs a manufactured range where the action is genuinely low-cost and administrative.

Funding path

Existing City Planning/TPAC staff time and existing financial-reporting infrastructure; no new funding mechanism identified as required.

Who benefits, and how

City Council and the public, via visibility into whether public-art funding — like the parkland cash-in-lieu mechanism documented in the sibling parks-recreation-access leaf — is pooled and reallocated across wards or spent only where collected; residents in lower-development wards specifically, via evidence either confirming or disconfirming whether the program's development-linked design produces geographic inequity in practice, rather than the question remaining permanently unanswerable for lack of disaggregated data.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via existing City Planning operating budget; no disproportionate payer identified.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder's Cui Bono table — the backgrounder's own Cui Bono section explicitly considered and declined to assert a beneficiary here, stating that "naming a specific beneficiary entity... profiting from it would require a specific, sourced finding this review did not locate." This card is a direct response to that documented gap, not a claim that withholding disaggregated figures currently benefits an identified party.

Financial ROI

Not modeled — a transparency/reporting measure, not a spending program. Comparator source: none required for a negligible-cost administrative action. Confidence: low.

Economic ROI

Range: Not yet estimable. Comparator source: none identified. Confidence: low — a reporting-disaggregation action of this narrow scope has no obvious first-order economic effect.

Social ROI

Directional: the same equity question already named explicitly for StART's own "Just Us" 2026 program (its call names "projects distributed equitably across the city" as an express criterion) cannot currently be checked for the much larger, development-linked Percent for Public Art mechanism for lack of disaggregated data — this card's social case is that closing this gap is a precondition for any future equity assessment, not itself a claim that inequity exists. Comparator source: backgrounder's own documentation of StART's "Just Us" 2026 explicit geographic-equity criterion, as a named point of contrast. Confidence: low.

Environmental ROI

Range: Genuinely environmentally neutral — a financial-reporting disaggregation action has no construction, land-use, or maintenance effect of its own. Comparator source: none required. Confidence: low — stated plainly per the template's guidance that "genuinely environmentally neutral" is a valid, honest fill.

Evidence

  • NEW (2026-07-14) · Backgrounder's own "Open questions / data gaps" section · confirms no public-art-specific annual dollar figure was located this review, distinct from the broader $123.9 million Section 37 figure (cited from the sibling parks-recreation-access leaf)
  • NEW (2026-07-14) · City of Toronto, "Percent for Public Art Program" webpage · three-option contribution mechanism (on-site/off-site/combination), Public Art Reserve Fund structure
  • NEW (2026-07-14) · Backgrounder's own "Key tensions / tradeoffs" section · documents the development-linked funding-scale structural feature and StART's contrasting explicit equity criterion

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium-high confidence that this is a genuine, documented reporting gap (the backgrounder's own research explicitly could not locate a disaggregated public-art-specific figure). Low confidence on whether disaggregation would actually reveal a geographic-equity problem, since that is precisely the question this card's own action would newly make answerable — this card takes the position that the reporting gap itself should close, not that a specific inequity is already proven.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: fairness and legal review; confirmation from City Planning or a City budget document of whether disaggregated Percent for Public Art figures already exist internally but are simply not currently published, which this page’s backgrounder did not resolve.
