Community Safety and Well-Being Plans — Playbook

Ontario requires every city to have a safety plan — how well Toronto's own plan, SafeTO, has actually delivered.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can do about a provincially-mandated plan with no provincial funding and no independent evaluator — get Queen's Park to fund the mandate it created, and give SafeTO the sustained scorecard Scotland used.

The honest bottom line

Ontario law has required every municipality to adopt a Community Safety and Well-Being Plan since 2021, reviewed every four years — but the province funds none of the implementation itself, per the City of Toronto's own report. Toronto's plan, SafeTO, reports strong Phase 1 delivery (2022-2024): a mental-health crisis service that has handled more than 25,000 calls and resolved 78% without police involvement, a Community Crisis Response Program answering 88% of critical incidents within 48 hours, and over 90% of the plan's 26 actions complete or in progress. The public-health approach this plan is built on has strong international backing: Scotland's Violence Reduction Unit, running the same model since 2005, cut weapon-carrying by 69% and hospital admissions from sharp-object assaults by 63% over an 11-year window. The same City report claiming 90%+ action completion also discloses that gun violence rose 34% in 2024 — the plan's final reported year — hitting youth and six specific neighbourhoods hardest. Nothing in the available sourcing resolves whether that is a lagging effect, a citywide trend outside the plan's reach, or a real shortfall — and no independent evaluation exists to check. Unlike Toronto Paramedic Services, which got direct Auditor General scrutiny, SafeTO's own numbers so far come only from the division that runs it. That is not unique to Toronto: the UK's own decades-long experience with a similar model found partnerships routinely skipped evaluation or leaned on raw crime counts with no shared definition of "working." And funding is genuinely precarious — $6.8 million in federal money expires by March 2026, and the City's own language says future funding "will be requested... subject to the City's financial resource capacity," not that it's secured. Two places to start. First: get the Province to fund the mandate it created. Ontario requires every municipality to build and maintain a CSWB plan but provides no dedicated money to do it — narrower, real provincial grants exist for specific pieces (crisis-team funding, hate-crime units), but nothing sized to the full obligation. The City is already part of a coalition asking for this through the Association of Municipalities of Ontario; formalizing a dedicated implementation transfer, rather than leaving municipalities to patch it together from city budgets and expiring federal grants, is the direct fix. Second: give SafeTO the sustained, health-linked scorecard Scotland used. Scotland's model worked, and the proof is an 11-year run of the same tracked numbers — weapon-carrying rates, hospital admissions. SafeTO doesn't yet have an equivalent, sustained metric set, or an evaluator outside the division running the program. Fix both ahead of the 2027 report-back already on the books: adopt a small, fixed set of health-system-linked outcomes tracked over the plan's full ten years, and bring in an independent evaluator. Neither move resolves the central open question: whether SafeTO's 2024 gun-violence increase reflects the plan underperforming, a lag before later-phase investments show results, or a citywide trend the plan was never positioned to fully offset. No accountability-claims register finding connects a specific named entity to profiting from CSWB planning gaps; this is a directly city-delivered, community-grant-funded model with no identified privatized angle, and the backgrounder's own Cui Bono search came up honestly empty.

a recommendation card — Formalize Provincial Cost-Sharing for CSWB Plan Implementation, Not Just Adjacent Grant Programs

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: community-safety-wellbeing-plans · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: NEW

Problem

The Community Safety and Policing Act, 2019 legally requires every Ontario municipality to prepare, adopt, and periodically review a CSWB plan, but the City of Toronto's own report states plainly that "Ontario municipalities do not receive any direct investments from the province to implement" that requirement — while a specific $6.8 million federal funding stream supporting SafeTO's Phase 1/2 transition expires by March 2026 with no confirmed replacement. This card addresses only the core-implementation funding gap — not the separate, already-funded provincial grant programs (Safer and Vital Communities Grant, Mobile Crisis Response Team Enhancement Grant), which this card's own backgrounder documents as real but narrower in scope than full plan-implementation funding.

Action

The City formally requests the Province establish a dedicated, ongoing CSWB plan-implementation transfer (distinct from the existing narrower, program-specific grants), sized to the legislative obligation the Province itself created, joining the advocacy the City's own report notes is already underway by the Association of Municipalities of Ontario and the Ontario Municipal Social Services Association.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: continue and formalize existing advocacy (the City's own report already documents joining AMO/OMSSA's call); ensure SafeTO's own budget requests to Council transparently distinguish core-implementation costs from grant-funded program costs, so the funding gap this card targets is legible in the City's own budget documents.
  • City demands of Province: establish a dedicated CSWB plan-implementation transfer program — this is squarely a provincial funding-and-legislative-design decision, since the Province created the mandate under the CSPA without an accompanying implementation-funding mechanism.
  • City demands of Feds: continued funding for the Crime Prevention Grant and Building Safer Communities Fund specifically — the City's own report's Recommendation 4 already makes this request directly, which this card treats as an existing, City-endorsed ask rather than a new one.

Cost

Not separately costed as a province-wide figure. Toronto's own cumulative SafeTO investment since 2021 is reported at $60.6 million gross, with annual figures separately cited in the $43-49 million range (not fully reconciled to the cumulative figure) — a single Ontario municipality's scale gives a rough sense of the per-city order of magnitude, but no source estimates a province-wide implementation-funding total across all CSWB-obligated municipalities. Toronto's own reported spending is the only concrete anchor available; no comparator province or jurisdiction's CSWB-equivalent funding formula was identified. Confidence: low — the ask is directionally clear but no source provides a costed provincial-transfer-program design to anchor a specific dollar figure.

Funding path

Provincial general revenue, via a new dedicated transfer program — this is the core of the ask itself, not a pre-existing mechanism being requested to continue. The federal Crime Prevention Grant/Building Safer Communities Fund renewal (Recommendation 4 of the City's own report) is a separate, narrower funding path already formally requested by the City.

Who benefits, and how

All Ontario municipalities carrying the CSWB planning mandate, via reduced reliance on general municipal tax revenue and federal grants with defined expiry dates to fund a provincially-legislated obligation; Toronto residents specifically benefit from reduced funding-cliff risk to programs like the Toronto Community Crisis Service and Community Crisis Response Program, both flagged in the backgrounder as currently funded through a mix of City resources and expiring federal grants.

Who bears the cost, and how

Ontario provincial taxpayers, via general revenue funding a new transfer program; in the absence of this card's ask, the cost continues to fall on municipal property taxpayers city-by-city and on federal taxpayers via time-limited grant renewals that are not guaranteed.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified — the backgrounder's own Cui Bono table came up empty for this page, since CSWB planning is delivered through direct municipal social-development divisions and community-partner grants rather than a privatized or single-vendor-contracted model.

Financial ROI

Not separately modeled as a fiscal-return figure for the province; the ask is a cost-shifting/formalization request (moving an existing City-borne cost partly onto provincial books) rather than a program with its own projected savings. A live-discovery search for a costed CSWB-implementation-funding-formula proposal (from AMO, OMSSA, or a comparable advocacy body) did not surface one with a specific dollar ask. Confidence: low — genuine gap; the City's own report confirms the advocacy exists but this page’s sources do not include AMO/OMSSA's own costed proposal, if one exists.

Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — this is a funding-mechanism/governance request, not a spending program with a direct local-economic-impact study attached. Confidence: low.

Social ROI

Directionally supported by the backgrounder's own documentation of concrete Phase 1 outcomes (25,000+ mental health crisis calls responded to, 78% resolved without police involvement, 1,844 critical incidents responded to at an 88% 48-hour response rate) that are themselves dependent on continued funding stability — the mechanism (funding certainty → program continuity → sustained outcomes) is directly supported by the backgrounder's own account of programs currently facing a funding cliff. Confidence: medium — the underlying programs' outcomes are well-documented in the City's own self-report; the causal link from "more secure provincial funding" to "better outcomes" specifically is a reasonable inference from funding-continuity logic rather than a separately modeled or cited finding.

Environmental ROI

Not applicable — a funding-mechanism request has no meaningful direct environmental footprint of its own.

Evidence

  • City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation" (2025-04-29) · NEW · no-direct-provincial-investment finding, federal grant expiry dates, Phase 1 outcome figures, AMO/OMSSA advocacy reference
  • Ontario Ministry of the Solicitor General, provincial grant program materials · NEW · named existing (narrower) provincial grant streams

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence that a real, documented funding gap exists and that the City's own report already supports the direction of this ask (it names the same advocacy coalition this card would join). Lower confidence on the specific dollar figure a "dedicated CSWB implementation transfer" should carry, since no source provides a costed proposal at that scale. The distinction between "no direct implementation funding" and "real but narrower program-specific grants exist" is preserved deliberately in this card rather than collapsed into a simpler but less accurate "totally unfunded mandate" framing.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: a costed provincial-transfer-program proposal (from AMO/OMSSA or a comparable source) to anchor a specific dollar ask, fairness and legal review.

a recommendation card — Adopt a Sustained, Health-System-Linked Outcome Metric Set for SafeTO, Modeled on Scotland's Violence Reduction Unit

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: community-safety-wellbeing-plans · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: NEW

Problem

SafeTO's own Phase 1 progress report claims over 90% of its 26 actions complete or in progress in the same document that discloses a 34% rise in gun violence in 2024, and no independent (Auditor General-level or academic) evaluation of SafeTO specifically was located in this page’s sources — unlike Toronto Paramedic Services, which received independent Auditor General scrutiny. The UK's own multi-decade Community Safety Partnership experience shows this is a common failure mode internationally, not a Toronto-specific oversight: an independent evaluation found partnerships had "no common agreement as to how to measure 'effectiveness'" and over-relied on recorded crime data alone. This card addresses only the measurement/evaluation-design gap — not a claim that SafeTO's programs are ineffective, which this page’s sources do not establish either way.

Action

Adopt a small, fixed set of health-system-linked outcome metrics (analogous to Scotland's VRU tracking weapon-carrying rates and hospital admissions for sharp-object assaults) tracked consistently over SafeTO's full 10-year horizon, reported alongside — not instead of — the existing action-completion and program-output metrics, and commission an independent (non-Social-Development-authored) evaluation at the Phase 2 report-back point already scheduled for 2027.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: define and adopt the metric set, direct Toronto Public Health and the SafeTO Collaborative Analytics and Learning Environment (already an existing, functioning data-sharing infrastructure per the backgrounder) to track it, and commission an independent evaluator for the 2027 report-back already scheduled — all within existing municipal authority and existing infrastructure.
  • City demands of Province: access to relevant provincial health-system data (hospital admissions data specifically, mirroring the Scottish model's own hospital-admissions metric) where not already available through existing data-sharing agreements — the backgrounder notes the SafeTO Collaborative Analytics and Learning Environment has already built partnerships with the University of Toronto and Statistics Canada, suggesting this kind of data partnership is a demonstrated, not novel, City capability, but hospital-system data specifically may require a distinct provincial data-sharing agreement not yet confirmed in this page’s sources.
  • City demands of Feds: none identified as directly relevant to this specific action.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: modest relative to SafeTO's existing $43-49 million annual investment — an independent evaluation and an expanded metrics-tracking function are typically a small fraction of a program's total budget, though no specific comparator figure for evaluation cost as a percentage of program budget was identified. The qualitative comparator (Scotland's own sustained, multi-decade tracking practice) supports the design, not the cost estimate — no direct cost figure for this specific action was found. Confidence: low — the design rationale is well-supported, but no source anchors a specific evaluation-cost figure.

Funding path

Within SafeTO's existing operating budget (Social Development's own analytics function already exists per the backgrounder) plus a modest incremental allocation for external evaluator contracting — no new dedicated funding mechanism is proposed beyond what SafeTO's own 2027 report-back commitment already implies should happen.

Who benefits, and how

All Toronto residents, via a SafeTO program whose actual effectiveness becomes independently checkable rather than resting solely on the implementing division's own self-report; residents in the six geographic areas the backgrounder identifies as bearing 46% of 2024 firearm incidents specifically benefit if sustained, disaggregated metrics reveal whether place-based interventions are working in those specific areas, which the current action-completion metrics do not directly measure.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, via SafeTO's existing operating budget plus a modest incremental evaluation cost; no new tax or fee proposed.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified — same empty Cui Bono finding as a recommendation card.

Financial ROI

Not quantified; better-targeted, evidence-checked interventions plausibly avoid wasted spending on ineffective program elements, but no source models this as a specific dollar figure. Confidence: low — genuine gap, not computed.

Economic ROI

Not yet estimable — an evaluation/measurement action does not have a direct economic-impact study attached in this page’s sources. Confidence: low.

Social ROI

The strongest case in this page’s evidence: Scotland's VRU model, tracked consistently over 11 years, produced independently confirmed, substantial outcome improvements (69% fall in weapon-carrying, 63% fall in hospital admissions, 50% reduction in sharp-object-assault deaths). This card's own action does not claim SafeTO will replicate those exact figures — only that adopting a comparably sustained, health-linked metric discipline is the transferable design element that could eventually allow a comparably confident outcome claim for Toronto, which is not currently possible given the evaluation gap this card addresses. Confidence: medium — the international precedent's outcome evidence is strong and multi-source-corroborated; the inferential leap to "adopting similar metrics will produce similar results in Toronto" is directional, not guaranteed, and this card does not overstate it as a prediction.

Environmental ROI

Not applicable — a measurement/evaluation-design action has no meaningful direct environmental footprint.

Evidence

  • City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation" · NEW · 90%+ action completion, 2024 gun-violence increase, SafeTO Collaborative Analytics and Learning Environment existing capability
  • search-summary re: Scottish Violence Reduction Unit outcomes · NEW · 11-year outcome dataset ⚠️ still being checked
  • search-summary re: UK Community Safety Partnership evaluation gaps · NEW · evaluation-discipline failure mode ⚠️ still being checked

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence that the measurement-design gap this card identifies is real (no independent SafeTO evaluation was located, and the UK comparator literature shows this is a common failure mode for this model generally). Lower confidence on the specific cost of implementing this card's Action, and explicitly no claim that SafeTO's current programs are underperforming — the 2024 gun-violence increase is stated in the backgrounder as an open interpretive question, not resolved by this card in either direction.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirmation of what hospital-admissions or comparable health-system data-sharing arrangement would actually be available to the City, a specific evaluation-cost estimate, fairness and legal review.
