Homelessness Accountability & Complaints — Playbook

Where a shelter client can actually complain about mistreatment, and what those complaints have turned up so far.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can actually do on homelessness accountability and complaints — each move with its costs, its beneficiaries, and its receipts.

v2.0 · 2026-08-11

---

The honest bottom line

If you're a shelter resident in Toronto and something goes wrong — a letter you never got, a meal you can't eat, a decision nobody will explain — there's exactly one independent office set up to actually listen: Ombudsman Toronto, which handled 4,326 cases last year. The small stuff mostly gets fixed: Anthony, staying intermittently in a shelter with no reliable phone, never got a benefits letter TESS mailed to his shelter instead of emailing — the Ombudsman got involved, and TESS built a whole new email option for every client in his position. Petro, a recent immigrant in a City-contracted shelter, couldn't eat catered food during a kitchen closure because of a medical dietary need nobody told him he could flag — one phone call from the Ombudsman's office fixed it. The big stuff is a mixed record. In December 2024, the Ombudsman found the City had quietly stopped letting refugee claimants into shelter beds for most of a year, systemic discrimination — specifically anti-Black racism, since nearly everyone turned away was Black. Council didn't discuss the report when it landed, the City Manager put his disagreement in writing, and it took three months and outside pressure before most of the 14 recommendations were adopted, with no formal way to check whether it's actually happening even now. Compare that to a 2025 case the same office investigated: 11 tenants in a rooming house went six months without heat, water, or power after a small fire, because City licensing staff didn't enforce their own bylaws — the City accepted every one of the 27 recommendations, publicly, on the record. Same office, same eighteen-month window, two very different responses; nobody has explained why. In 2021, the City told the Toronto Star there were 157 violent incidents in its shelters in 2019; a Freedom of Information request for the same year, same system, turned up somewhere between 1,076 and 2,408 — seven to fifteen times higher, depending on exactly what gets counted. And from 2010 to 2012, Toronto published exactly what it paid every single shelter operator, per bed, per night — dozens of named operators, real dollar figures — and hasn't published that since, even as every other number in the shelter budget has gotten more precise. One reported 2025 figure, $146 a night, surfaced once in a document this project's own search tools couldn't find again — suggesting the number probably still exists inside the City, just not where anyone would naturally look for it.

Bring back the per-operator per-diem schedule the City used to publish. (a recommendation card) Toronto already did this, for years, at a smaller system size. There's no new mechanism to invent, just an old one to restore.

Actually go look for operator-specific wrongdoing findings. (a recommendation card) Court records, Landlord and Tenant Board filings, licensing records. This project flagged the gap; nobody's closed it yet.

Give each division its own version of the Ombudsman's own clear annual scorecard. (a recommendation card) TSSS, TESS, and MLS already collect this data. They just don't publish it the way the Ombudsman publishes its own.

What this office does not settle. Nobody has found a substantiated wrongdoing finding against any specific shelter operator — only against the City itself. That's an open question, not a clean bill of health: this project looked, and didn't find one, which isn't the same as confirming none exists. Why complaints to the Ombudsman actually dropped in 2025 after years of steady increases is also unexplained. And the $146/night figure hasn't been independently confirmed — it's a lead, not a fact yet. None of the three moves below require new legislation or new money worth mentioning; they ask Toronto to be as transparent about its own shelter system as its own watchdog already is about itself.

---

a recommendation card — Republish a Current, Itemized Per-Operator Shelter Per-Diem Schedule

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: homelessness-accountability-complaints · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: carried-forward (backgrounder NEW-2026-AC-5)

Problem

Toronto published a detailed, shelter-by-shelter per diem rate schedule from 2010 through at least 2012; no equivalent has been published since, despite the system's budget growing roughly tenfold over the same window and every other line item in current TSSS budget documents being reported with comparable or greater precision [NEW-2026-AC-5]. A reported 2025 figure ($146/person/night) appears to exist in at least one non-headline council document, suggesting the underlying data is not necessarily gone, only no longer surfaced where the public would naturally find it. This card addresses only the publication gap, not any underlying cost-escalation argument.

Action

The City resumes publishing a current, itemized per-operator (or, at minimum, system-average) shelter per diem rate, in the same format used from 2010-2012, as a standing annual disclosure alongside the TSSS Budget Notes.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: entirely within existing municipal authority and existing TSSS budget-reporting practice — no new legislative or provincial/federal action required, since the City already published this exact data format as recently as 2012.
  • City demands of Province: none required.
  • City demands of Feds: none required.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low — a reporting-format change using data the City already collects for contract administration purposes; comparator: the City's own 2010-2012 precedent, which was produced as a standard staff report at that scale of shelter system, before the roughly tenfold budget growth since documented in this page [NEW-2026-AC-5].

Funding path

Existing TSSS budget-office reporting capacity; no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

Journalists, researchers, and the public, via restored ability to compare operator-level cost and value; Council itself, via better information for POS contract oversight and renewal decisions; indirectly, shelter clients, via improved public accountability pressure on operator performance.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, negligibly, via existing TSSS reporting staff time — no new payer class. POS shelter operators may experience increased public/competitive visibility into their individual per-diem rates, a real but non-fiscal cost to specific entities this card does not quantify.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder with a graded a registered accountability claim/a registered entity pair — the backgrounder's own Cui Bono section flags this as a plausible but unestablished lead (specific POS operators benefiting from reduced rate visibility), not yet promotable to a named row. See backgrounder "Open questions / data gaps."

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Not directly estimable — a disclosure action, not a spending or savings program in itself; any downstream cost-recovery effect (e.g., from improved contract-negotiation leverage) is speculative and not modeled here. No comparator needed; a reporting-transparency action of this kind is not expected to carry a first-order fiscal case. Confidence: low — genuinely not the right unit for this action.

(b) Economic ROI

Not yet estimable; no comparator identified. Confidence: low.

(c) Social ROI

Directional: restores a specific, previously-existing accountability mechanism the City itself once operated at a smaller system scale, using the City's own 2010-2012 precedent as comparator; no claim in the claims register quantifies the social-outcome effect of restored per-diem transparency specifically. Confidence: low-medium.

(d) Environmental ROI

Genuinely neutral — a reporting exercise with no physical-infrastructure component; no comparator needed. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-AC-5 · source quote (this review) · 2010-2012 per-diem publication precedent; current-document absence

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the underlying gap (independently confirmed by this page’s carried-forward sources's own dedicated search of 2024-2026 budget documents); low confidence on whether the data still exists internally in a readily publishable format, since the $146/night figure's own source document was not independently re-located in this review.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: independently confirming the $146/night figure's source document before citing it as evidence the data still exists; fairness and legal review.

a recommendation card — A Dedicated Search for Operator-Specific Wrongdoing Findings (LTB, Court, Licensing Records)

Card id: a recommendation card · Card class: INTERNAL · Issue: homelessness-accountability-complaints · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: carried-forward (backgrounder NEW-2026-AC-7)

Note (FIX-2, W1b cards audit, 2026-08-06): this card's Action names this project's own research process as the actor, not a public body — labelled Card class: INTERNAL, per a recorded judgment ruling.

Problem

This page’s own carried-forward sources explicitly found no independently verified, named wrongdoing finding against any specific shelter operator (as distinct from the City itself), and flagged this as a genuine open item rather than evidence such findings don't exist, recommending a search of Landlord and Tenant Board records, Ontario civil court records, and any applicable provincial congregate-care licensing regime [NEW-2026-AC-7]. This review did not close that gap either. This card proposes the research action itself, not a policy demand, since no finding yet exists to act on.

Action

A dedicated our accountability-capture track/our claim-mining track research pass searches Landlord and Tenant Board records (where a shelter operator is also a registered landlord), CanLII and Ontario court dockets for civil suits naming specific shelter operators, and any provincial licensing/inspection regime applicable to congregate care settings, to determine whether this page’s own flagged gap reflects a genuine absence of findings or an under-searched record.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: not applicable — this is a research action for this project itself, not a City policy ask.
  • City demands of Province: not applicable in this card's own action, though a substantiated finding (if one is located) could generate a future card with real jurisdictional asks.
  • City demands of Feds: not applicable.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low — staff/researcher time for a targeted records search using existing free-tier access (CanLII query-only search, per this project's own accountability-tooling notes); comparator: the Accountability Observatory's own similarly-scoped capture-backlog tasks (this library's internal records), several of which are costed at the same low, staff-time-only scale.

Funding path

Existing research-project capacity; no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

This project's own research integrity, via closing a flagged gap rather than leaving it open indefinitely; shelter clients and the public, if a genuine finding is located and surfaced; POS shelter operators, symmetrically, if the search confirms no substantiated finding exists — a negative result is also a legitimate, useful output.

Who bears the cost, and how

This research project's own capacity, via researcher time — no external payer.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified — this card's action is research, not a policy change, so there is no status-quo beneficiary in the L6 template's sense.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Not applicable — a research action, not a spending or savings program; no comparator needed. Confidence: low — not the right unit for this action.

(b) Economic ROI

Not applicable; no comparator needed. Confidence: low.

(c) Social ROI

Not separately estimable — this card's value is instrumental (closing a research gap), contingent entirely on what, if anything, the search finds; no comparator needed. Confidence: low.

(d) Environmental ROI

Genuinely neutral; no comparator needed. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-AC-7 · source quote (this review) · page’s own flagged open item on operator-specific wrongdoing

Confidence & uncertainties

High confidence that the gap is real and explicitly flagged by the page’s own carried-forward sources; zero confidence on what, if anything, such a search would find — stated as a genuine unknown, not a prediction.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: assignment to a our accountability-capture track/M research pass; not yet attempted.

a recommendation card — A Combined TSSS/TESS/MLS Complaint Dashboard, Modeled on the Ombudsman's Own "By the Numbers" Format

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: homelessness-accountability-complaints · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (backgrounder NEW-2026-AC-1, NEW-2026-AC-2)

Problem

Ombudsman Toronto publishes clear, office-wide annual figures (cases handled, closure rate, fairness-problem categories, contact-channel breakdown) [NEW-2026-AC-1], and its case stories (Anthony, Petro) show individual divisions (TESS, TSSS) responding constructively once a complaint reaches the Ombudsman's office [NEW-2026-AC-2] — but no published, division-specific, shelter/housing-precarity-focused complaint dashboard comparable to the Ombudsman's own office-wide summary was located for TSSS, TESS, or MLS individually in this review. This card addresses the absence of a division-level equivalent, not a criticism of the Ombudsman's own reporting, which this card treats as the model to extend.

Action

TSSS, TESS, and MLS each publish an annual, division-specific complaint summary (volume, category, resolution rate, communication-accessibility metrics) in the same clear format Ombudsman Toronto already uses for its own office-wide figures, cross-referenced to any Ombudsman findings involving that division in the same period.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: entirely within existing municipal authority — each division already collects this data internally for its own complaint-handling process; this asks for its publication in a standardized format, not new data collection.
  • City demands of Province: none required.
  • City demands of Feds: none required.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low — a reporting-format standardization using data each division already collects; comparator: the Ombudsman's own office-wide "2025 by the Numbers" page, which this card proposes as the direct template [NEW-2026-AC-1].

Funding path

Existing divisional reporting capacity; no new funding mechanism required.

Who benefits, and how

Shelter and OW clients, via clearer visibility into how their own division handles complaints before needing to escalate to the Ombudsman; Council, via division-level accountability data comparable to what it already receives office-wide from the Ombudsman.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers, negligibly, via existing divisional staff time for report compilation — no new payer class.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Not directly estimable — a reporting action, not a spending program; no comparator needed. Confidence: low.

(b) Economic ROI

Not yet estimable; no comparator identified. Confidence: low.

(c) Social ROI

Directional: the Anthony and Petro case stories both show that even a functioning complaint-resolution outcome depended on escalation to the Ombudsman rather than the division's own process working smoothly the first time [NEW-2026-AC-2] — a published division-level dashboard is a plausible, though unquantified, mechanism for catching communication-accessibility gaps (like the one Anthony experienced) before escalation is needed. Comparator: the two case stories themselves, cited directly. Confidence: low-medium — directional, not quantified.

(d) Environmental ROI

Genuinely neutral; no comparator needed. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-2026-AC-1 · source quote (this review) · Ombudsman's own office-wide reporting format and figures
  • NEW-2026-AC-2 · source quote (this review) · Anthony and Petro case stories, division-level complaint handling

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence. The Ombudsman's own reporting format is directly sourced and real; whether TSSS/TESS/MLS already produce an internal equivalent not currently published was not independently confirmed in this review — a real possibility this card does not rule out.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming whether an internal, unpublished divisional complaint report already exists; fairness and legal review.
