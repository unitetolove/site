RC-CSPG-01 — Standing, Public Reconciliation of Crime-Trend Data and Perception Polling — Recommendation Card

Card id: RC-CSPG-01 · File: research/cards/community-safety-perception-gap.md#rc-cspg-01 Status: DRAFT Issue slug: community-safety-perception-gap · Backgrounder: community-safety-perception-gap Trust class: NEW-LOAD-BEARING (problem framing) / NEW (evidence anchors) · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

A directly and independently fetched December 2025 Liaison Strategies survey found that 76% of Torontonians believe crime in Canada has increased over the past year, and 72% believe homicides specifically are rising, even as the same period's homicide and auto-theft figures moved sharply downward by multiple accounts [NEW-CG01–CG05, backgrounder]. This leaf's own discovery could not resolve the exact 2025 homicide count (sources disagree: 39 vs. 42) or auto-theft decline percentage (sources disagree: roughly 24%–34%) [NEW-CG06, NEW-CG07] — meaning even a resident or journalist motivated to check the "actual" numbers against the perception-gap finding encounters inconsistent public figures rather than one clear, authoritative source. Separately, the City's own Safe Cities Index citation page (toronto.ca) was found, on direct fetch, to display stale 2017 data rather than any current ranking [NEW-CG08] — a small but concrete example of the City's own public-facing safety-data communication lagging behind the safety trends it could be citing. This card addresses the data-clarity gap underlying the perception gap, not the perception gap's psychological or media-driven causes, which this leaf's sources do not analyze.

Action

Require the City of Toronto to publish and maintain a single, standing, plain-language "safety trends dashboard" page — sourced directly and only from the Toronto Police Service's own Public Safety Data Portal and Statistics Canada — updated at least quarterly, that states the current year-to-date homicide, shooting, and auto-theft trend in one authoritative place, replacing or supplementing the currently stale Safe Cities Index citation page this pass found displaying nine-year-old data.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: entirely within municipal authority — the Toronto Police Service and City communications/data teams already produce the underlying data (per the Public Safety Data Portal identified in this leaf's sources); this action requires only that it be consolidated and kept current in one authoritative public location, and that the existing stale Safe Cities Index page be updated or retired.
  • City demands of Province: none required.
  • City demands of Feds: none required, though Statistics Canada's own published data (used elsewhere in this repo's gun-gang-youth-violence-prevention backgrounder for firearm-violence trends) could be cross-linked as an independent corroborating source.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low tens of thousands of dollars CAD for the dashboard build and ongoing maintenance, anchored to the comparator that this consolidates data the City and Toronto Police Service already collect and publish separately (the Public Safety Data Portal already exists) — this is a presentation/consolidation cost, not a new data-collection program.

Funding path

Existing City of Toronto communications/open-data operating budget; no new funding mechanism required, since the underlying data infrastructure (the Toronto Police Service's own Public Safety Data Portal) already exists per this leaf's sources.

Who benefits, and how

Toronto residents generally, via one clear, current, authoritative answer to "is crime actually up or down" rather than the inconsistent figures this leaf's own discovery encountered across multiple otherwise-credible sources; journalists and researchers (including future passes on this leaf), via a stable citation point instead of having to reconcile conflicting secondary reporting.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via the existing communications/open-data budget; no disproportionate cost to any named group.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder — the Cui Bono table in research/backgrounders/community-safety-perception-gap.md is empty; the backgrounder notes media-coverage economics as a plausible general structural factor in perception gaps but does not name a specific entity, so no row is populated here either.

ROI (four dimensions) — schema v2

(a) Financial ROI

Not separately modeled — this is a transparency/communication measure. No source in this leaf quantifies a financial return from clearer public safety-data communication specifically. Comparator source: not applicable to this dimension. Confidence: not applicable — stated plainly rather than forced.

(b) Economic ROI

Range: not yet estimable. Comparator source: a live-discovery search this pass (economic impact of perceived-safety improvements on local business/tourism activity) found general commentary but no rigorously quantified, Toronto-specific study connecting perception-gap closure specifically (as distinct from actual crime reduction, a different question) to measurable economic activity. Confidence: low — genuine gap; this card does not borrow general "fear reduces economic activity" intuitions without a specific citable source.

(c) Social ROI

Directional: if a persistent perception gap drives avoidance of public space, transit, or the night economy (a mechanism this document does not independently verify for Toronto but which is a commonly discussed general pattern), then closing the gap with clearer public data is plausibly protective of civic/social participation — but no source in this leaf quantifies this effect for Toronto specifically. Confidence: low-medium — directional only.

(d) Environmental ROI

Range: genuinely environmentally neutral — a data-dashboard consolidation has no emissions, land-use, water, or waste mechanism. Comparator source: not applicable — plain neutrality claim. Confidence: high — no plausible environmental mechanism attaches to a public-data dashboard.

Evidence

  • NEW-CG01–CG05 (2026-07-14, CONFIRMED via independent direct fetch) · Liaison Strategies survey · perception-gap figures
  • NEW-CG06, NEW-CG07 (2026-07-14, VERIFY) · conflicting homicide/auto-theft figures across sources
  • NEW-CG08 (2026-07-14, CONFIRMED via independent direct fetch) · City of Toronto Safe Cities Index page · found stale (2017 data)

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the problem statement (the data-inconsistency and stale-page findings are directly, independently confirmed this pass); this card does not claim that better data communication alone would close the perception gap — general public-opinion research (cited generally, not from a specific study this pass verified) suggests media coverage and salience effects likely play a role independent of data availability, a limitation this card does not overstate.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: identifying the City's specific current data-publication workflow in more detail; Lane R pass.

RC-CSPG-02 — Recurring, Independent Re-Evaluation of TCCS as It Scales — Recommendation Card

Card id: RC-CSPG-02 · File: research/cards/community-safety-perception-gap.md#rc-cspg-02 Status: DRAFT Issue slug: community-safety-perception-gap · Backgrounder: community-safety-perception-gap Trust class: NEW-LOAD-BEARING (problem framing) / NEW (evidence anchors) · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

The Toronto Community Crisis Service's foundational evaluation — a 78% no-police-involvement diversion rate, independently conducted by CAMH-affiliated third-party evaluators — covers a specific six-month pilot window (March–September 2022) across four pilot areas, independently and directly confirmed from the City's own primary release this pass [NEW-CG10, backgrounder]. Search-surfaced (not independently fetched) figures suggest TCCS has since grown substantially — a reported $31.7 million 2025 budget rising to a proposed $35.3 million in 2026, over 200 staff, and planned TTC subway-platform expansion — while continuing to cite the same 78% figure [NEW-CG11]. This card addresses the risk that a headline metric from a small, specifically-evaluated pilot is being carried forward as if automatically still accurate at roughly 10x the original program's scale and into new operating environments (e.g., transit platforms) the original evaluation did not cover, not a claim that the figure is wrong.

Action

Require a fresh, independently-conducted (third-party, CAMH-affiliated-or-equivalent) outcome evaluation of TCCS at its current scale and current service areas — including any new transit-platform deployment specifically, since that is an operating context the original 2022 pilot evaluation did not cover — published on a recurring basis (at minimum, every time the program undergoes a major scale-up) rather than relying on the original pilot's figures indefinitely.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: entirely within municipal authority — the City already funded and commissioned the original 2022 evaluation and can commission a follow-up through the same or a comparable third-party evaluator relationship.
  • City demands of Province: none required directly, though continued provincial mental-health-system funding coordination (referenced in the original 2023 evaluation release's own quotes calling for "long-term sustainable investments into mental health care") is a relevant adjacent ask this card does not duplicate.
  • City demands of Feds: none required for the evaluation itself.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: low hundreds of thousands of dollars CAD for a rigorous, current-scale re-evaluation, anchored to the comparator that the original six-month pilot evaluation was itself commissioned and delivered by an established third-party evaluator (Provincial System Support Program/Shkaabe Makwa at CAMH) at a much smaller program scale — a comparable or somewhat larger evaluation budget is a reasonable estimate for a ~10x-larger current program, though no specific cost figure for the original evaluation was located in this leaf's sources to anchor a precise multiple.

Funding path

Existing TCCS program budget (reported at $31.7–35.3 million, VERIFY) could reasonably absorb an evaluation cost that is a small fraction of that total; alternatively, the City's existing Social Development evaluation infrastructure (per the gun-gang-youth-violence-prevention backgrounder's own documented $1.176M "Advanced Statistical Analytics" and $1.946M "Program Staffing, Evaluation and Support" CPAF lines) could potentially be leveraged, though TCCS itself is a different program stream than that leaf's CPAF/BSCF-funded portfolio and this card does not assume automatic budget transferability between them.

Who benefits, and how

Toronto residents and Council, via confidence that TCCS's headline effectiveness metric reflects its current scale and operating contexts rather than a legacy pilot figure; TCCS itself and its delivery partners, via updated evidence that could strengthen continued funding cases if the program continues performing well at scale.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide (or the TCCS program budget specifically), via existing or modestly expanded program/evaluation budget lines; no disproportionate cost to any named group.

Who benefits from the status quo

No beneficiary identified in the backing backgrounder — the Cui Bono table in research/backgrounders/community-safety-perception-gap.md is empty.

Financial ROI

Not separately modeled — this is an evaluation/governance measure, not a cost-avoidance one directly.

Economic ROI

Range: not yet estimable. Comparator source: none identified this pass — no third-party study quantifies the economic value of re-evaluating a scaled crisis-response program specifically. Confidence: low — genuine gap.

Social ROI

Directional: if TCCS's diversion rate has held up at scale, confirming this strengthens the case for a program this leaf's sources show already reduces police involvement in mental-health calls by a substantial majority — a plausible social benefit (reduced over-policing exposure for people in crisis) documented directionally in the original 2023 evaluation's own quoted partner statements (e.g., CMHA Toronto's quoted statement that the model "contributes to reducing the over policing of racialized and other equity seeking communities"), though this document treats that as the partner organization's own stated view, not an independently modeled outcome. Confidence: low-medium — directional, resting on a 2022-era evaluation whose current-scale validity is exactly what this card proposes to re-test.

Environmental ROI

Range: genuinely environmentally neutral — an evaluation-commissioning action has no emissions, land-use, water, or waste mechanism. Comparator source: not applicable — plain neutrality claim. Confidence: high — no plausible environmental mechanism attaches to commissioning an evaluation.

Evidence

  • NEW-CG10 (2026-07-14, CONFIRMED via independent direct fetch) · City of Toronto 2023 news release · original pilot evaluation figures, methodology, evaluator identity
  • NEW-CG11 (2026-07-14, VERIFY) · current-era TCCS budget/scale figures, not independently fetched

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the problem statement (the multi-year gap between the original evaluation and the program's current scale is directly evidenced by comparing a confirmed 2022 pilot-scope evaluation against unverified but consistently-reported much-larger current figures); this card does not claim TCCS is failing at scale — only that the evidence to confirm or disconfirm that has not been independently re-established in this leaf's sources since the original pilot.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming whether a more recent independent (non-original-pilot) TCCS evaluation already exists and was simply not located in this pass's discovery; Lane R pass.
