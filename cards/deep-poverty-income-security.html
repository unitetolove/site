RC-DPIS-01 — Council resolution advocating provincial social-assistance rate adequacy, backed by Toronto's own frontline data — Recommendation Card

Card id: RC-DPIS-01 · File: research/cards/deep-poverty-income-security.md#rc-dpis-01 · Status: DRAFT Issue slug: deep-poverty-income-security · Backgrounder: research/backgrounders/deep-poverty-income-security.md §"The structural link between income adequacy and homelessness" / §"The evidence on income transfers as an anti-poverty tool"

Trust class: INHERITED · Date: 2026-07-14

Problem

Ontario Works and ODSP, per this leaf's evidence base, pay substantially below the province's own official Market Basket Measure poverty line — a structural fact, not a disputed one, though this card does not restate the specific current dollar rates, which belong to the separate ontario-works-rate-history-erosion leaf [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (deep poverty income)]. The backgrounder's evidence base frames this gap as a primary structural driver of homelessness risk: when the maximum shelter allowance available is well below market rental cost, remaining housed while on social assistance requires either additional income, doubling up, substandard housing, or diverting non-shelter benefit dollars to rent — each of which risks homelessness or produces severe material deprivation [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (deep poverty income)]. Toronto's own child poverty rate (25.3%, independently corrected 2026-07-16) is the highest among Canada's large cities [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (deep poverty income)].

Action

Council passes a resolution formally requesting that the Province of Ontario raise and index Ontario Works and ODSP rates toward the Market Basket Measure poverty line, citing Toronto's own frontline homelessness and child-poverty data as evidence of the local cost of current rate inadequacy.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: pass the resolution and compile the supporting frontline data (shelter-system demand, child-poverty figures) the City already collects; this is the entirety of the City's direct authority on rate-setting itself.
  • City demands of Province: raise and index Ontario Works and ODSP rates toward the Market Basket Measure poverty line — the province sets these rates; the City has no authority to set or override them.
  • City demands of Feds: none directly for provincial rate-setting; the federal government's role here runs through the separate Canada Disability Benefit and CCB/GIS-adjacent levers, covered in this batch's basic-income-universal-services RC-BIUS-02, not duplicated in this card.

Cost

Order of magnitude: not stated as a specific figure — the underlying cost of a provincial rate increase is not modelled in this leaf's evidence base, and this card does not invent one. The cost of the advocacy action itself (the resolution) is negligible.

Funding path

No City funding mechanism is required for the resolution itself; the fiscal decision belongs entirely to the Province.

Who benefits, and how

Ontario Works and ODSP recipients in Toronto, via a higher, indexed income floor closer to the Market Basket Measure line — directly reducing the structural pressure toward homelessness or severe material deprivation the backgrounder documents. Toronto's shelter system and other downstream service systems, indirectly, if the backgrounder's own "we are already paying for deep poverty" reasoning holds — though this document notes that reasoning is analytical rather than tied to a specific quantified Toronto cost-offset study.

Who bears the cost, and how

Provincial taxpayers, via whatever revenue mechanism the Province chooses — entirely outside City control or this card's ability to specify.

Financial ROI

Not quantified — no source in the evidence base models a specific dollar cost or offsetting saving for a provincial rate increase. The backgrounder's own "already paying for deep poverty" framing is qualitative, not a cited figure, and this card does not manufacture one from it.

Economic ROI

Range: Not yet estimable — no source in this leaf's evidence base models the local or provincial economic effect (induced spending, reduced downstream service costs) of a social-assistance rate increase specifically. Comparator source: The backgrounder cites U.S. economic-security-program poverty-reduction figures (~47% overall, ~46% child poverty) as evidence income transfers work at scale, but this is a U.S. federal tax-credit/benefit context, not an Ontario social-assistance rate-increase scenario, and this card does not treat it as a Toronto-specific economic-impact estimate. Confidence: Low — genuine gap, stated plainly rather than borrowing a differently-structured program's figure as if it applied directly.

Social ROI

The backgrounder's strongest available evidence — that income transfers are among the most effective anti-poverty tools available, evidenced by Canada's own CCB/GIS record and the U.S. economic-security-program findings — supports this card's directional social case: raising the income floor for OW/ODSP recipients would be expected to reduce material deprivation and housing-instability risk for a population currently held well below the poverty line [INHERITED: this leaf's inherited master briefing (deep poverty income)]. No Toronto-specific or Ontario-specific magnitude for this exact policy change is quantified in the evidence base.

Environmental ROI

Range: Genuinely not applicable in any direct sense — this is an income-transfer-rate resolution with no construction, land-use, or emissions component. Comparator source: None needed; stated directly per the template's allowance for a genuinely neutral/not-applicable action. Confidence: High for "the resolution itself has no direct environmental effect."

Evidence

  • INHERITED this leaf's inherited master briefing (deep poverty income) · structural income-adequacy/homelessness link, Toronto child-poverty figure (25.3%, independently corrected 2026-07-16), U.S. economic-security-program poverty-reduction figures, CCB/GIS record

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on the structural argument (the mechanism connecting income inadequacy to housing instability is well-reasoned and consistent with independently-documented income-transfer poverty-reduction evidence) and low confidence on political feasibility, since this is advocacy toward a provincial government the City cannot compel, and the evidence base itself frames the persistence of below-poverty-line rates as a political rather than evidentiary choice. No Financial or Economic ROI figure exists for a specific Ontario rate-increase scenario; this card does not manufacture one.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: provincial political will (outside City control), a specific costed rate-increase scenario (not yet modelled in the evidence base), and a Lane R pass before any public-facing use.
