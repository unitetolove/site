Immigration and the Diaspora Economy — Playbook

How immigrant communities' trade ties and remittances actually turn into economic opportunity for Toronto.

DRAFTThe playbookThe evidence file

What Toronto can actually do on its diaspora economy — each move with its costs, its beneficiaries, and its receipts.

v2.0 · 2026-08-11

---

The honest bottom line

The immigrant-trade link is well-established economics literature, not a hopeful talking point: across many countries and studies, immigrant communities have been shown to lower the cost of doing business across borders, because they carry the language, trust, and relationships that make a foreign market less foreign. Toronto, one of the most immigrant-dense major cities in the world, should in theory have an unusually large version of this asset — but nothing found in this research shows the City has built a specific program around it. Separately, Statistics Canada confirms immigrants are more likely than Canadian-born residents to become business owners (about 12% vs. about 8% for Canadian-born residents whose families have been here three generations or more), but immigrant-owned businesses are, on average, smaller — less revenue, fewer employees — than Canadian-born-owned ones, a genuine nuance the more celebratory version of this story tends to leave out.

Scope a diaspora trade and entrepreneurship facilitation function within the City's existing Economic Development office. (a recommendation card) No City of Toronto program specifically facilitating diaspora trade connections or targeting diaspora entrepreneurship supports, distinct from general small-business services, was identified in this review. This card directs a scoping exercise, not a new program outright — including whether existing supports already reach diaspora-owned businesses effectively, and what a modest pilot connecting Toronto diaspora business networks to trade-promotion missions might look like.

Publish an annual public accounting of how existing programs reach diaspora-owned businesses. (a recommendation card) The honest first move isn't launching a new diaspora-trade program — it's finding out whether Toronto's existing small-business supports already reach diaspora-owned businesses well, or are quietly missing them. There's no public reporting that answers that question either way right now. A basic annual accounting — who's actually using existing programs, broken down in a privacy-respecting way — would tell the City whether it has a real gap to close or is already doing fine, and it directly informs whether a recommendation card's proposed function would be solving a real problem or a hypothetical one.

Real diaspora-engagement models exist elsewhere, worth a look but not yet independently checked here: Ireland has run a formal, named government strategy for engaging its diaspora for decades, and India has issued government bonds specifically marketed to overseas Indians as an investment channel. Neither was verified down to current details in this review — they're named as leads worth someone checking, not confirmed models ready to copy. Nobody has calculated how much bigger Toronto's trade relationships get because of its diaspora communities specifically; the one comparable figure available — a 2018 study finding a 10% increase in immigrant population linked to a 1.7% rise in manufacturing exports — is from Italian provinces, not Toronto, and hasn't been recalculated for this city. The economics behind "Toronto's diaspora is an economic asset" is sound and well-documented; the City-level follow-through isn't there yet, and the one hard number available — that immigrant businesses tend to be smaller — deserves an honest look before anyone builds a bigger program on an assumption that hasn't been tested.

a recommendation card — Scope a Diaspora Trade and Entrepreneurship Facilitation Function Within Toronto's Existing Economic Development Office — Recommendation Card

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: immigration-diaspora-economy · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: New load-bearing findings (problem statement anchored in independently corroborated economic literature and StatsCan finding)

Problem

The immigrant-trade link — immigration's association with increased bilateral trade via reduced information and trust costs — is a genuine, established finding in international economics literature, independently corroborated this review [backgrounder NEW-1]. Separately, Statistics Canada research confirms immigrants in Canada are more likely than the Canadian-born population to become business owners (11.9% vs. 8.4% overall ownership/self-employment rate; about 5.2% vs. 4.0% for incorporated-business ownership specifically, 2016 Census-based data), though immigrant-owned businesses tend to be smaller on average [backgrounder NEW-2, Statistics Canada, Picot & Ostrovsky 2021, Catalogue no. 36-28-0001-2021009]. No City of Toronto program specifically facilitating diaspora trade connections or targeting diaspora entrepreneurship supports (as distinct from general small-business services) was identified in this review [backgrounder, "Current state"] — a gap this card addresses directly, while explicitly not claiming the general small-business-size nuance identified at NEW-2 has been resolved by anything proposed here.

Action

Direct the City's Economic Development & Culture division to conduct a scoping exercise on whether a dedicated diaspora trade-facilitation and entrepreneurship-support function (distinct from, and additive to, existing general small-business supports) would be additive — including whether existing programs already reach diaspora-owned businesses effectively, and what a modest pilot connecting Toronto diaspora business networks to trade-promotion missions might look like.

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: commission the scoping exercise using existing Economic Development & Culture division capacity — within existing municipal authority over local economic development.
  • City demands of Province: none required for the scoping exercise itself; a secondary note that trade missions and export-promotion supports often involve provincial economic development agencies as co-participants, named as a coordination point rather than a formal demand.
  • City demands of Feds: none required for the scoping exercise; trade and foreign policy remain federal jurisdiction, and any actual trade-mission or export-facilitation activity would need to work within, not around, federal trade frameworks — a distinction this card preserves rather than implying municipal authority over trade policy.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: negligible to low tens of thousands of dollars CAD for a staff-conducted scoping exercise — anchored to the general order of magnitude of a single-division policy-scoping action elsewhere in this corpus, not a newly modeled figure.

Funding path

Existing City of Toronto Economic Development & Culture division operating budget for the scoping exercise — no new program or budget line proposed at this stage.

Who benefits, and how

Diaspora-owned businesses in Toronto, via a clearer future picture of whether dedicated trade-facilitation or entrepreneurship support beyond general small-business services would be valuable; City policy staff, via an evidence base for future program design rather than acting on the master briefing's conceptual framing alone.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via the negligible scoping-exercise staff-time cost.

Financial ROI

Not applicable in the standard sense — a scoping exercise has no direct fiscal-return pathway; any downstream fiscal effects would depend on what the scoping exercise recommends and whether Council acts on it.

Economic ROI

Not yet estimable for the scoping exercise itself — the master briefing's broader claim that diaspora trade facilitation could meaningfully diversify Toronto's trade base [master briefing, ⚠️ still being checked] is cited only as motivating context, not a modeled return. No comparator specific to a municipal diaspora-trade-facilitation scoping exercise was identified; the StatsCan finding on immigrant business-size gaps [backgrounder NEW-2, Picot & Ostrovsky 2021, Catalogue no. 36-28-0001-2021009] is the nearest real, directly-verified data point in this page’s evidence, though it describes the current state of immigrant entrepreneurship generally, not a modeled return on this card's specific action. Confidence: low — genuine gap; a future pass should look for a Canadian municipal diaspora-trade-facilitation program's own outcome evaluation, if one exists, before citing a figure here.

Social ROI

Not the primary dimension for this action; no source in this review quantifies a social-wellbeing case specific to diaspora trade facilitation, as distinct from the general economic case addressed above.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a scoping exercise and any resulting trade-facilitation coordination function has no material emissions, land-use, water, waste, or resilience footprint distinct from ordinary economic-development office operations. No comparator sought; the action's nature makes one unnecessary rather than merely unfound. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-1 · source quote, directly verified to primary source (accessed/fetched 2026-07-14) · Bratti, De Benedictis & Santoni, CEPII Working Paper 2018-01 · immigrant-trade link as established economic literature, establishing the problem's underlying economic logic (specific magnitude: 10% increase in immigrant stock associated with 1.7% increase in manufacturing exports, Italy-specific data)
  • NEW-2 · source quote, directly verified to primary source (accessed/fetched 2026-07-14) · Statistics Canada, Picot & Ostrovsky 2021, Catalogue no. 36-28-0001-2021009 · immigrant entrepreneurship rate and business-size nuance, exact figures
  • master briefing · ⚠️ still being checked (carried-forward) · diaspora-led development examples and base-of-the-pyramid framing, cited as motivating context only, not as this card's own load-bearing evidence

Confidence & uncertainties

Medium confidence on feasibility (a scoping exercise is a low-friction research step within existing municipal authority). Low confidence on whether a dedicated diaspora-trade function would actually prove additive over existing general small-business supports — this card does not prejudge that outcome. The StatsCan business-size nuance [backgrounder NEW-2] is a genuine complicating factor this card does not resolve: a scoping exercise should explicitly examine whether smaller average immigrant-business size reflects a market-access gap this card's proposed function could address, or a different underlying dynamic outside this card's scope.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming current reach of general City small-business supports into diaspora-owned businesses; a cost comparator; fairness and legal review.

a recommendation card — Publish an Annual Public Accounting of How Toronto's Existing Economic-Development Programs Reach Diaspora-Owned Businesses — Recommendation Card

Card id: a recommendation card · Issue: immigration-diaspora-economy · Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Trust: CONTEXTUAL

Problem

This page’s evidence base has a specific, named data gap: no source in this review confirms whether, or how effectively, Toronto's existing general small-business and economic-development supports currently reach diaspora-owned businesses [backgrounder, "Current state" and "Open questions"]. This matters because the directly-verified StatsCan finding that immigrant-owned businesses tend to be smaller than Canadian-born-owned businesses [backgrounder NEW-2, Picot & Ostrovsky 2021, Catalogue no. 36-28-0001-2021009] could reflect several different underlying dynamics — a genuine market-access gap existing programs don't close, a different business-model distribution, or something else entirely — and without basic reporting on program reach by ownership demographic, this question cannot currently be answered from public information.

Action

Direct the City's Economic Development & Culture division to begin publishing an annual public accounting of program participation and outcome data (grant/loan uptake, business-support program enrollment, where currently and voluntarily collected) disaggregated by immigrant/diaspora business ownership status where privacy-compliant disaggregation is possible, modeled on the level of outcome-reporting specificity this corpus's own building-retrofit leaf found the City already applies to other program types (e.g., the High-Rise Retrofit Improvement Support Program's reported 15-building/21%-reduction outcome data).

Jurisdiction split

  • City does: the reporting function itself, using existing Economic Development & Culture division data infrastructure — entirely within municipal authority, contingent on privacy-compliant data collection already existing or being feasible to add.
  • City demands of Province: none required.
  • City demands of Feds: none required, though a note that Statistics Canada's own linked-administrative-data research [backgrounder NEW-2] demonstrates this kind of disaggregation is methodologically feasible at the national level, which the City could draw on as a design reference.

Cost

Order-of-magnitude: negligible to low tens of thousands of dollars CAD for the reporting function itself, assuming underlying program-participation data is already collected in some form — this card does not assume new data-collection infrastructure is required, but flags that cost could rise materially if it is, a genuine uncertainty stated rather than hidden.

Funding path

Existing City of Toronto Economic Development & Culture division operating budget for report preparation — no new spending proposed beyond staff time, contingent on the data-availability caveat above.

Who benefits, and how

Toronto residents, City Council, and future policy researchers, via a factual answer to whether existing programs already serve diaspora-owned businesses proportionately or whether a genuine gap exists — directly informing whether a recommendation card's proposed dedicated function would be solving a real problem or a hypothetical one.

Who bears the cost, and how

City taxpayers city-wide, via the negligible reporting-function staff-time cost, with the stated caveat that cost could rise if new data-collection infrastructure proves necessary.

Financial ROI

Not applicable — an information/reporting action has no direct fiscal-return pathway of its own.

Economic ROI

Not applicable — a reporting action's economic impact, if any, would flow entirely through whatever program changes it later informs, which is outside this card's own scope to model; no comparator sought. Confidence: high, in the sense that no plausible independent economic-impact pathway exists for the reporting action itself, distinct from downstream decisions it might inform.

Social ROI

Directional case only: transparent program-reach reporting is a governance/accountability good in its own right, consistent with this page’s own Cui Bono finding that no extraction dynamic was identified here but that basic program-reach transparency was also not confirmed to exist — closing that gap has standalone value independent of what it reveals.

Environmental ROI

Genuinely environmentally neutral — a reporting function has no material emissions, land-use, water, waste, or resilience footprint. No comparator sought. Confidence: high.

Evidence

  • NEW-2 · source quote, directly verified to primary source (accessed/fetched 2026-07-14) · Statistics Canada, Picot & Ostrovsky 2021, Catalogue no. 36-28-0001-2021009 · business-size nuance motivating the need for disaggregated reach data
  • backgrounder "Open questions / data gaps" · genuinely uncovered gap on existing program reach into diaspora-owned businesses

Confidence & uncertainties

High confidence on feasibility if underlying data is already collected; genuinely uncertain feasibility and cost if it is not, a caveat this card states explicitly rather than assuming away. No claim is made about what such reporting would find — the City's existing programs may already reach diaspora-owned businesses proportionately, and this card does not presuppose otherwise.

Status

DRAFT — blocked on: confirming current data-collection practices in the Economic Development & Culture division; fairness and legal review.
