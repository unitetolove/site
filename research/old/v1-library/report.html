FROZEN EDITION — July 2026. This page is part of the v1 Toronto homelessness research library, a complete, sealed project frozen at its July 2026 figures (92% of its 770 claims individually verified against primary sources). It is a product of its July-2026 campaign context, published as a citable historical artifact. Where our newer research disagrees, a dated margin note points at the living page — the frozen text is never silently rewritten. Why there are two editions.

TL;DR

Toronto spends $897.957 million a year on homelessness and is currently running one of the most expensive, least effective systems in the democratic world. That is not a funding problem. It is a configuration problem — and the fix is cheaper than what the City is doing now.

The easy part first, because it requires no new money at all: Toronto's own Auditor General found, in an audit Council already adopted, that simply fixing vacancy management in the City's existing social housing stock — Toronto Community Housing Corporation — would house 2,200 additional people and recover $7 million a year, without building a single new unit. That fix is sitting there, already audited, already approved in principle, waiting to be finished. It is the single highest-return action available to whoever is elected mayor in October 2026, and it costs nothing to start.

The financial case, in three numbers. A Toronto shelter bed costs the City $49,640 a year. Housing that same person under a Housing First model — permanent housing plus voluntary support, tested in a real randomized controlled trial run in Toronto itself — costs a net $6,311 a year after accounting for the emergency health, justice, and shelter costs it prevents. Leaving that same person unhoused with serious mental illness costs the public $58,972 a year in downstream costs. In every comparison this research examined, the humane option is also the cheap one. This is not a report asking anyone to choose between compassion and fiscal responsibility. The City's own numbers say they point the same direction.

Who is accountable, and for what, specifically. The City of Toronto's own Auditor General found 12,742 funded shelter bed-nights sitting empty in a single winter while 174 people a night were turned away — a closeable operational gap, not a resource shortage. The provincial government has frozen Ontario Works at $733/month since 2018 while inflation ran 25% ahead of it, exempted all post-2018 rental units from rent control, and in November 2025 passed Bill 60, further weakening tenant protections at eviction hearings over the City's own formal objection. Both governments have real, documented accountability here. Neither gets a pass because the other one exists.

The way out is not a mystery. Every recommendation in this report is either already-adopted City policy waiting on implementation, an already-audited fix to an existing program, or a request Toronto City Council has already formally made to the province. Housing First was tested in this city and it worked. Houston cut its homeless population 63% by building a real-time bed-tracking system Toronto could build with authority it already has. What's missing is not evidence, and in the central case, it is not money either. What's missing is the decision to implement what has already been proven, and fund it for longer than one political cycle — because the clearest lesson in the entire evidence base, from Finland's own recent reversal, is that this model works exactly as long as the funding behind it lasts, and not one day longer.

Read the rest of this report for exactly how, with the receipts.

THE TORONTO HOMELESSNESS REPORT

How We Got Here, Who's Accountable, and the Fastest, Cheapest Way Out

A Research Report for Mayor and Municipal Council Candidates, Journalists, and Civil Society

Prepared by The Unknown Soldier | The Campaign for a Regenerative Ontario | July 2026

Document ID: MAIN-REPORT-001

A note on method, kept brief and moved here rather than left to interrupt the argument: every factual claim in this report traces to a primary source — the City's own budget documents, the Auditor General, the Ombudsman, Statistics Canada, peer-reviewed academic research, or direct Council records — cited inline. Where a figure is contested, uncertain, or sourced only to an advocacy claim rather than an official finding, this report says so explicitly rather than presenting it with false confidence. This report holds two governments accountable by name. It does so with primary sources, not adjectives.

PART ONE — HOW WE GOT HERE

Toronto did not arrive at its current homelessness crisis by accident. It arrived by a specific, traceable sequence of policy choices, made by two orders of government, over roughly three decades — and the receipts for nearly every one of those choices are sitting in the City's own public record.

The download that started it

In 1998, the provincial government under Mike Harris restructured how Ontario funded social services, shifting significant responsibility for shelters, social housing, and social assistance administration onto municipalities — a policy shift historians and researchers still refer to as "the download." Toronto absorbed this cost. By the 2000 program year, the City was already funding thirteen separate shelter and housing agencies through what would become the Purchase-of-Service (POS) system that still structures the shelter sector today — the earliest documented per-diem allocations in this research (Homes First Society: $582,164; Dixon Hall Neighbourhood Services: $176,499) date to this exact moment, confirming that Toronto was managing a downloaded, provincially-designed homelessness response a full generation before the current crisis.

Rates frozen while rents rose

Ontario Works, the province's core social assistance program, provides a single adult $733 per month — a rate that has not increased since 2018. Over the same period, the Consumer Price Index rose more than 25%. A rent of $733 does not cover a bachelor apartment in any part of Toronto; it does not, on its own, cover the CMHC-defined "core housing need" threshold in any Ontario municipality. This is not a gap that opened gradually — it is a rate the provincial government has held flat, by policy choice, through an inflationary period that made every other cost of living rise around it. The Ontario Disability Support Program (ODSP) tells the same story at a different rate.

A rent-control carve-out with a real, growing population behind it

Since November 15, 2018, any residential unit first occupied after that date has been legally exempt from Ontario's rent control regime — meaning a landlord can raise rent on those specific units by any amount, at any time, once a tenant leaves. CMHC's own 2023 data found this drove a 31% rent increase on unit turnover in Toronto specifically that year — a real, if since-moderated, figure describing a genuinely tight rental market moment (2025 data shows the market has since softened). The scale of the exempt population itself remains genuinely difficult to quantify precisely — this report does not claim a number it cannot support — but City Council itself formally requested, twice, that the province reinstate rent control on these units, most recently in November 2025 alongside its formal opposition to Bill 60 (below). The City is on record believing this exemption is contributing to the crisis it is simultaneously being asked to shelter the consequences of.

The Landlord and Tenant Board, and a further weakening in 2025

The Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB), a provincial adjudicative body, has for years carried a documented, severe backlog — tenants facing eviction and landlords seeking possession both routinely wait months for a hearing. In November 2025, the Ford government passed Bill 60 ("Fighting Delays, Building Faster Act"), stated to address this backlog. Its actual provisions, read directly from the bill text: the notice period for non-payment eviction applications was cut from 14 days to 7; landlords using "own use" evictions are no longer required to compensate the displaced tenant or offer a comparable unit; tenants raising a maintenance defense at a hearing must now pay at least 50% of disputed arrears before that defense can even be heard; and the window to appeal an LTB decision was cut from 30 days to 15. Toronto City Council formally opposed this bill, on the record, before it passed. A coalition of 132 organizations — student unions, shelters, legal clinics, labour unions, and food banks — stated in a public letter that "the largest contributor to homelessness in Ontario is the province's own policies... not mental health or addiction, as the Ford government has purported." This is a real, powerful, and squarely advocacy-sourced claim — this report presents it as exactly that, an argument 132 organizations are making, not an independently adjudicated finding. City Council itself formally directed its own Housing Secretariat to produce an official study answering the same underlying question — what share of Toronto's homelessness is attributable to federal versus provincial decisions specifically — due in the first quarter of 2026. As of this report's writing, that study has not been located as published. If it exists, it would be the single most authoritative document available on this exact question, and every reader of this report — councillor, journalist, or resident — has standing to ask the City directly where it is.

A shelter system asked to absorb what treatment centres, not shelter beds, are for

Ontario replaced its network of supervised consumption sites with a new program, HART Hubs (Homelessness and Addiction Recovery Treatment), beginning April 2025. Nine sites opened province-wide, including four in Toronto. Independent journalism found the fourth Toronto site — the only one directly operated by the City itself rather than a community health centre — delivering, as of mid-2025, only a single narrow service (opioid agonist treatment), far short of the full "24/7 intake, medical monitoring, and supportive housing connection" the program promised. That site has since closed entirely, with no permanent replacement location found as of this report's writing. Separately, the same provincial restriction on supervised consumption sites forced the closure of 10 of 17 such sites across Ontario. The Association of Municipalities of Ontario — representing all 444 Ontario municipalities, not an advocacy group — called the province's subsequent $75.5 million funding response "a drop in the bucket" against what it estimates is an actual $11 billion, ten-year need for supportive housing province-wide, plus a further $2 billion specifically to house people currently living in encampments.

A decade of oversight, offered and declined, before it finally arrived through a worse door

Two specific, documented Council decisions illustrate a pattern worth naming precisely, because it is stronger evidence than either instance alone. In 2013, a City staff report recommended that Council formally request the City Ombudsman conduct a comprehensive investigation of Toronto's shelter and social housing system. Council did not adopt this recommendation — a floor amendment specifically deleted it, on the stated basis that "these activities are currently underway by staff." A full decade later, in 2023, the Ombudsman did conduct a major investigation into the shelter system, finding "significant unfairness" in the City's 2021 encampment clearings and, separately, a substantiated finding of discriminatory practice in how refugee claimants were excluded from base shelter beds. That investigation did not happen because anyone revisited the 2013 request — it happened because conditions deteriorated badly enough, through an entirely separate chain of events, to force the same kind of scrutiny through a different door, a decade later than it could have arrived.

A second, parallel pattern: in December 2017, facing a documented shelter overcrowding crisis (system usage up 35% in two years; occupancy running at 95%, above the City's own 90% target; nearly 100 recorded homeless deaths that year alone), Councillor Kristyn Wong-Tam moved a motion directing the City to formally request that the federal government open military armouries for emergency shelter — a mechanism that had already worked, successfully, on four prior occasions when a Toronto mayor made the same request. The federal government had already indicated, in writing, that it was prepared to say yes again. Council voted the motion down, 17 to 25. Toronto's Moss Park armoury was opened to the public months later anyway, after sustained public pressure forced the same outcome the December vote had specifically declined to authorize directly.

Neither of these findings alleges bad faith. Council's stated reasoning in each case was a real, defensible position at the time it was given. What both findings establish, on the record, is that the City has repeatedly had the option of faster, more direct action available to it, and repeatedly chosen a slower path — arriving, eventually, at something similar to what was declined, but later, and usually only after the human cost had already mounted further.

PART TWO — THE SCALE OF THE CRISIS TODAY

Toronto's shelter and homelessness response system had a 2025 gross operating budget of $897.957 million — one of the largest municipal homelessness budgets in North America relative to population. At its most recently confirmed point-in-time count (October 2024), 15,418 people were experiencing homelessness across all settings in the city, using the City's own methodology (a figure this report treats carefully — see the methodology note on undercounting below).

Seventy-eight percent of people accessing Toronto's shelter system have stayed long enough to meet the City's own definition of chronic homelessness — a 180-day threshold, broader than the federal three-year standard, but even under the narrower national methodology, Toronto's chronic-homelessness rate remains alarming. The City's own stated goal is that homelessness in Toronto should be "rare, brief, and non-recurring." By its own numbers, it is currently the opposite of all three.

The human cost is measured in years of life, not just dollars. Toronto Public Health's most recent full-year data confirms the median age at death for a person experiencing homelessness in Toronto is 50 for men and 38 for women — against a median age at death of 78 and 85, respectively, for the general Toronto population. A homeless woman in Toronto dies, on median, 47 years younger than a housed woman. Drug toxicity is the leading cause of death, accounting for 55% of recorded deaths in the most recent full year of data.

The system's own official count of unmatched demand is precise and damning. Toronto's own Auditor General, in a 2025 audit, found that in a single winter, 12,742 publicly-funded shelter bed-nights sat completely empty — while an average of 174 people a night were turned away with nowhere to go. This is not a hypothetical "the system is under-resourced" claim. It is the City's own Auditor General finding empty, funded capacity sitting unused on the same nights people were denied a bed. The audit's own stated causes: administrative timing rules around when seasonal warming and respite sites wind down (accounting for the larger share of the gap), and, at one specific site — Better Living Centre — beds the City's own Shelter, Support and Housing Administration division chose to hold back on purpose. The same audit found $2.9 million in operator surpluses the City had not recovered as of its October 2024 fieldwork, eight months after the winter season in question had ended.

The undersupply is not evenly distributed across the city. A ward-level analysis of the City's own official shelter locator data finds 7 of Toronto's 25 wards have zero base shelters at all, while three downtown wards together hold 53% of the city's entire shelter capacity — a concentration pattern that shapes both service delivery and political accountability for how the crisis is managed.

PART THREE — MUNICIPAL ACCOUNTABILITY: WHAT THE CITY CONTROLS, AND HOW IT HAS MANAGED IT

This section holds the City of Toronto to account using the City's own records — its Auditor General, its Ombudsman, its own Council minutes. It does not allege corruption. Where a finding is a real, documented failure of process, oversight, or urgency, it is named as exactly that.

Empty beds, sitting alongside turned-away people, in the same report. Already established above — 12,742 empty funded bed-nights, 174 people a night turned away, in the same audit, the same season. The mechanism (deactivation timing, and, at one site, deliberate withholding) is a solvable operational and policy problem, not a resource-scarcity problem. The City does not need new money to close this gap. It needs a rule: no funded bed sits empty by choice or by schedule while anyone is being turned away the same night.

An unrecovered surplus, and an honest gap in what happened next. The Auditor General's $2.9 million unrecovered-surplus finding, above, came with 15 formal recommendations for tighter financial oversight of third-party shelter operators. This report could not confirm, from any public source, whether those 15 recommendations were implemented for the following winter season. That is not a criticism disguised as a question — it is a genuine, specific, and answerable one. Any candidate or journalist reading this report has a direct, concrete question to put to TSSS: what is the current implementation status of all 15 recommendations from your own 2025 Auditor General audit?

A shelter siting process that keeps the public and elected councillors in the dark until decisions are finalized. City staff have confirmed, on the record, that new shelter site locations are deliberately withheld from the public — and from the local councillors whose wards will host them — until siting plans are finalized, explicitly to "de-politicize the selection process." There is a real, documented reason behind this practice: public and political input into individual site selection has historically produced delay and paralysis, evidenced by this library's own findings of 100-month average project timelines and specific, sustained opposition campaigns at individual sites. Both things are true at once — the practice has a real justification, and it is also a genuine transparency gap for the elected representatives whose wards are directly affected. This report does not resolve which value should win. It records that the tension is real and currently unresolved.

A procurement and security-contracting picture that, on close review, does not show a pattern of wrongdoing — and that finding is itself worth stating precisely. This research examined every named security contractor holding Toronto shelter or encampment-related contracts, checking each for allegations of wrongdoing. Two came back with clean records. One (Northwest Protection Services) had a real legal record running in the opposite direction from a worker-harm allegation — the company was the plaintiff suing former employees, and lost. One case — a wage-theft class action against a staffing contractor, One Community Solutions — is real, live, and unresolved; the contractor's confirmed City contract value ($19.5 million across two contracts) is substantial but roughly half the $40 million the plaintiffs' law firm alleges across the full relationship. The honest finding, after checking every named contractor in this system: no evidence of a systemic pattern of wrongdoing across the sector, and one specific, live, unresolved legal case that deserves its own continued scrutiny rather than being generalized into a broader indictment the evidence doesn't support.

A single largest capital project that has taken, and will take, over 20 years. The George Street Revitalization project — Toronto's largest shelter capital undertaking, redeveloping the aging Seaton House site — was first advanced by the City in 2009. After a since-abandoned public-private-partnership model added years of delay, the City-led project is now targeting occupancy in 2029: twenty years from conception, sixteen years from formal Council approval, and still four years from completion as of this report.

PART FOUR — PROVINCIAL ACCOUNTABILITY: THE FORD GOVERNMENT'S ROLE

Municipal accountability does not excuse provincial accountability, and this report treats both with the same evidentiary standard. Several of the provincial-level findings above bear direct repeating here as the specific case against the current provincial government:

  • A social assistance rate frozen since 2018 while inflation ran more than 25% ahead of it — a policy choice, renewed by inaction every year it has not been increased.
  • A rent-control exemption on all post-2018 units, which the City itself has twice formally asked the province to reverse.
  • Bill 60, passed November 2025 over the City's own formal objection, which measurably weakens tenant protections at eviction hearings specifically.
  • A HART Hub program whose one City-operated Toronto site has already closed, delivering a fraction of its promised service before doing so — while the broader provincial restriction on supervised consumption sites forced 10 of 17 such sites closed across Ontario.
  • A $75.5 million funding response to the resulting capacity crisis that the body representing all 444 Ontario municipalities — not an advocacy group — publicly called "a drop in the bucket."
  • A directed, Council-commissioned study on the province's own share of responsibility for Toronto's homelessness, due in Q1 2026, not yet located as published as of this report.
  • A policing-first pattern with a Toronto-specific enforcement record: provincial legislation enabling municipal encampment removal has coincided with documented ticketing enforcement against homeless individuals in Toronto rising roughly 2,000% over a comparable period, concentrated overwhelmingly on a small repeat-ticketed population (6.2% of ticketed individuals received 51.4% of all tickets issued) rather than spread evenly — consistent with a pattern of enforcement functioning as harassment of a known population rather than crime prevention.

None of this requires speculation about motive. Every item above is a documented policy choice, a documented vote, or a documented City position taken in direct response to a provincial decision. The provincial government's own public framing — that homelessness is primarily a mental health and addiction problem, not a housing and income-support problem — is a real, stated position, and 132 civil society organizations have publicly and directly disputed it. This report does not resolve that dispute by assertion. It notes that every specific, traceable policy lever available to the province in this period — rates, rent control, tenancy law, treatment infrastructure, funding — has moved in the direction the 132-organization coalition's framing predicts, not the direction the government's own stated framing would predict.

PART FIVE — WHAT WORKS: THE EVIDENCE BASE

The evidence for what actually reduces homelessness is not contested, not new, and not foreign to Toronto specifically.

Housing First is not a theory — it was tested in Toronto, at scale, in a randomized controlled trial, and it worked. The At Home/Chez Soi study, funded by the Mental Health Commission of Canada, ran a genuine randomized controlled trial across five Canadian cities, including a 575-participant arm in Toronto (MAP Centre, Unity Health Toronto). Its finding, published in JAMA: Housing First — permanent housing provided immediately, without requiring sobriety or treatment compliance first, paired with voluntary support services — produced significantly better housing stability and health outcomes than the "treatment first" model Toronto's shelter system still largely operates under. The benefits were sustained through years of follow-up.

Medicine Hat, Alberta became the first Canadian city to functionally end chronic homelessness, in June 2021 — maintaining that status for roughly seven months before losing it, not because the model failed, but because of an affordable-housing supply shortage constraining how many additional people could be housed. The lesson is precise: Housing First works as an intervention; it still requires enough actual housing supply behind it to sustain the result at scale.

Houston reduced its homeless population by 63% without new municipal spending, by fixing coordination — a single, shared, real-time system tracking every available bed and every person on a by-name waitlist, matching supply to need directly instead of leaving each shelter and agency to manage its own waitlist in isolation. This is the exact mechanism Toronto's own POLICY-001 recommendations (below) propose building.

Finland reduced national homelessness by roughly 80% between 2008 and 2023 through a national Housing First strategy — a real, substantial, internationally studied achievement this report presents honestly, including the less convenient recent chapter: Finnish homelessness has risen for two consecutive years (2024-2025) following cuts to the same prevention services and income supports that built the original success. The lesson, stated plainly: Housing First works, and it stops working the moment the funding behind it is cut — which is precisely the risk every recommendation in this report is designed to avoid repeating.

PART SIX — THE FINANCIAL CASE

This is the argument every fiscally serious council candidate and every skeptical journalist should read most closely, because it does not ask anyone to spend more money to do the right thing. In the central case, it costs less.

Toronto's true cost per shelter bed, per year, is approximately $49,640 — the City's own confirmed $136/night average shelter cost, annualized.

The same population, housed under a Housing First model with wraparound supports, costs a confirmed net $6,311 per person per year — after subtracting the reduced emergency health, corrections, and shelter costs the same population would otherwise generate. This is not a theoretical projection: it is the actual net cost recorded in the Toronto arm of the At Home/Chez Soi randomized controlled trial (Latimer et al., JAMA Network Open, 2019), the gross program cost ($20,367/year for high-need participants) minus the confirmed offsets ($14,056/year in reduced downstream costs).

Margin note (2026-08-12): the $6,311 figure stands, but our living library’s audit pass corrected this citation to Latimer et al. 2020, Psychiatric Services (including a DOI correction) — see the economics of homelessness.

The same population, left unhoused with serious mental illness, costs the public purse a confirmed $58,972 per person per year in combined health, justice, and social service spending (Latimer et al., CMAJ Open, 2017, Toronto-specific figure) — nearly ten times the net cost of housing them.

Margin note (2026-08-12): the living library does not carry this $58,972 figure; its current full-cost case is built on audited shelter costs plus modelled municipal lines instead — see full-cost accounting. The frozen figure is kept here as published in July 2026.

Toronto's own Auditor General has independently confirmed emergency shelters cost seven to ten times more than the housing-first alternatives available.

And the single highest-return action available to the City requires no new spending at all. Toronto's own Auditor General found, in a 2019 audit Council itself adopted, that fixing vacancy management within Toronto Community Housing Corporation — the City's existing social housing stock — would house 2,200 additional people and recover $7 million per year, without building a single new unit or spending a single new dollar. This is not a five-year plan. It is an operational fix to an existing City agency's existing housing stock, available on a 6-12 month timeline.

The pattern across every comparison in this section is the same: the housed alternative is cheaper than the unhoused status quo, in every single case this research examined, without exception. This is not primarily a moral argument, though it is also that. It is a fiscal one, made with the City's own numbers.

PART SEVEN — THE WAY FORWARD: WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER

The following recommendations are drawn from this campaign's full 50-recommendation policy platform, curated here to the actions with the clearest evidence, the fastest timeline, and — where costed — a specific, defensible number attached. Full detail, authority, and timeline for every recommendation is available in the companion policy document.

Immediate, no-new-spending actions (6-12 months): 1. Fix TCHC vacancy management — 2,200 people housed, $7M/year recovered, zero new spending. The single highest-ROI action available to the City, already audited and adopted in principle by Council in 2019, not yet fully implemented. 2. Implement the City's own already-adopted Auditor General recommendations on winter shelter capacity and financial oversight of third-party operators — closing the exact 12,742-empty-bed-nights and $2.9M-unrecovered-surplus findings above, using authority Council already has. 3. Publish TSSS's implementation status against all 15 of the Auditor General's 2025 recommendations — a transparency action, not a spending one.

Structural fixes (2-3 years, existing authority): 4. Build a real-time, centralized shelter-bed dashboard — the exact mechanism behind Houston's 63% reduction, designating the TSSS General Manager as system conductor with mandatory daily data from every Purchase-of-Service operator. 5. Convert Purchase-of-Service shelter contracts to outcome-based funding — a blend of operational base funding and housing-exit performance bonuses, with union-negotiated worker protections built in from the start, redistributing the existing $897M budget rather than requiring new money. 6. Extend the same wage floor already required in new shelter contracts to existing operators — closing a documented gap where new contracts pay staff more than existing ones for the same work.

Provincial-level asks, with municipal Council already on record supporting them: 7. Reinstate rent control on post-2018 units — a request Toronto City Council has now made twice, on the record. 8. Raise Ontario Works and ODSP to housing-adequate levels, indexed to inflation going forward, so the rate never again falls this far behind the cost of living by simple inaction. 9. Fully implement the Ontario Auditor General's 2021 provincial homelessness strategy recommendations, still substantially outstanding four years later. 10. Deliver the Q1 2026 federal-versus-provincial attribution study City Council itself directed and has not yet received — the single most useful document that could exist for settling, with the province's own commissioned analysis, exactly how much of this crisis is a provincial policy choice.

CONCLUSION — THE WAY OUT

This report has been direct about who is accountable, by name, with primary sources. It closes by being equally direct about what the way out actually looks like, because a report that only assigns blame is not a governing document — and every recommendation above is written to be adopted by whoever holds these offices next, regardless of party or persona.

The way out does not require inventing anything new. Every recommendation in Part Seven is either already-adopted-but-unimplemented City policy, an already-audited fix to an existing program, or a request the City itself has already formally made to the province. The evidence base — Housing First, coordinated real-time bed management, adequate income support — has been tested in Toronto specifically and found to work. The financial case does not require choosing between compassion and fiscal responsibility; in every comparison this research examined, they point the same direction.

What the way out requires is willingness — from whoever is elected in October 2026, and from the provincial government regardless of who holds it — to implement what has already been found to work, funded at the level already proven necessary, and sustained past the political cycle that approved it. Finland's recent reversal is the clearest warning available anywhere in this evidence base: the model works exactly as well as the funding behind it, for exactly as long as that funding lasts. Toronto has spent nearly $900 million a year proving it can afford to respond to this crisis. It has not yet spent that money in the configuration its own Auditor General, its own adopted evidence, and its own randomized controlled trial data all say would cost less and work better. That gap — between what the City already knows and what the City is currently doing — is the entire distance left to close.

SOURCES AND METHODOLOGY

Every claim above traces to a primary source: City of Toronto budget documents and Council records, Auditor General and Ombudsman reports, Statistics Canada and CMHC data, and peer-reviewed academic research (JAMA, JAMA Network Open, CMAJ Open, BMC Public Health). Advocacy-sourced claims are explicitly labeled as such throughout rather than presented with the same confidence as official findings. This report draws on a research library of over 700 individually verified claims, cross-checked against primary sources, with an active correction-tracking system designed specifically to prevent a fixed error from persisting in one place after being corrected in another. A full source index and the complete underlying research library are available on request.
