FROZEN EDITION — July 2026. This page is part of the v1 Toronto homelessness research library, a complete, sealed project frozen at its July 2026 figures (92% of its 770 claims individually verified against primary sources). It is a product of its July-2026 campaign context, published as a citable historical artifact. Where our newer research disagrees, a dated margin note points at the living page — the frozen text is never silently rewritten. Why there are two editions.

Five Facts

CARD 1 — THE AUDITOR'S VERDICT

Ford has had five years to build a provincial homelessness strategy. He hasn't.

In 2021, Ontario's Auditor General told the Ford government to create a provincial homelessness strategy with specific actions, targets, and timelines. A 2023 follow-up found 40% of the AG's homelessness recommendations — including the strategy requirement — showed "little or no progress." Ontario remains one of the only provinces without a legislated homelessness strategy. In 2025, opposition MPPs (Green MPP Aislinn Clancy and Liberal MPP Lee Fairclough) brought Bill 28, the Homelessness Ends with Housing Act, to a vote after debate (clarified July 4, 2026 for precision — the bill did not pass; it was brought to a vote and defeated). The PC majority voted it down.

Source: AG 2021 (auditor.on.ca) ✅; AG 2023 follow-up (auditor.on.ca) ✅; Bill 28, OLA, Oct/Nov 2025 ✅

Their rebuttal: "We have $700M HPP and 28 HART Hubs."
Your answer: Neither is a strategy with targets and timelines — which is exactly what the AG required, and what Ontario lacks.

CARD 2 — THE POLICY THAT PENALISES HOMELESSNESS

Ontario Works pays tent dwellers ~$343/month — not $733 — because they can't prove rent.

Ontario Works pays a single adult up to $733/month: $343 basic needs + up to $390 shelter allowance. The shelter allowance requires proof of paid rent. A person living in a tent cannot prove rent. They receive approximately $343/month. The Province's income support system applies a financial penalty to homelessness at exactly the moment of greatest vulnerability. The rate has been frozen since 2018 and is now about a quarter of full-time minimum-wage income — down from roughly 70% in 1990.

Source: Ontario Regulation 134/98 (OW Act) ✅; Feed Ontario 2019 ✅

Their rebuttal: "OW is for transition, not long-term support."
Your answer: Precisely — but the formula penalises the people furthest from transition. You can't claim to want people off the street while paying them more to have an address than not to.

CARD 3 — THE 7-MONTH POLICY THAT COST 1,583 HOUSING PATHWAYS

The Province suspended the housing benefit for 7 months in 2024. That year, 1,583 fewer people got housed.

The Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit (COHB) was suspended April to October 2024 during an intergovernmental dispute. Toronto's shelter-to-housing exits fell from 5,927 (full year 2023) to 4,344 (full year 2024) — a 26.7% drop. The suspension is the most visible policy change in that window. The cost of lost throughput runs into the tens of millions at $136/night.

Source: TSSS 2026 Budget Notes (backgroundfile-261532.pdf) ✅

Their rebuttal: "There were other factors — refugee population, rental market."
Your answer: Possibly — and the TSSS budget notes themselves link the two. But a government that suspended a housing subsidy for seven months and then watched exits fall 27% has explaining to do.

CARD 4 — THE HEALTH SYSTEM BILL NOBODY COUNTS

Homeless Torontonians cost the public health system 6.9× what housed people cost — $12,209 vs $1,769 per year.

An ICES-linked administrative data study of 2021-2022 Toronto health records found people experiencing homelessness incurred average public healthcare costs of $12,209 per year — 6.9× the $1,769 average for housed people. That represents an estimated $69.8M–$99.7M in excess annual healthcare costs in Toronto alone. CIHI's 2022-23 hospital data shows homeless patients are hospitalized at costs 2.1× the national average ($16,785 vs $7,803) and 93% are admitted via the emergency department.

Source: ICES/Homeless Hub 2024 (homelesshub.ca) ✅; CIHI 2022-23 (cihi.ca) ✅

Their rebuttal: "That's a health system problem, not a homelessness program problem."
Your answer: Housing First costs $6,311 per person per year net (Latimer 2020). Every year Ontario's system doesn't house someone, it pays ~$43,329 more in shelter costs — before adding the healthcare bill. This isn't a housing problem. It's an accounting problem.

CARD 5 — THE SECTOR'S OWN VERDICT

Only 6% of 176 stakeholders surveyed by the City said homelessness services have improved. 66% say worse.

In 2024, the City of Toronto's contracted consultants surveyed 176 stakeholders for the 2025-2030 Strategic Plan consultation. The group included residents' associations (27%), health and mental-health organizations (20%), homelessness service providers (17%), and people with lived experience. Only 6% said services have improved in the past five years. 66% said worse. 47% named reducing chronic homelessness as the system's top priority. The resulting Strategic Plan — promised to Council in 2025 — has been delayed to 2027.

Source: What We Heard, April 2025, Appendix A, Figure 2, p.58 ✅

Their rebuttal: "Toronto homeless count dropped 21% in 2025."
Your answer: That drop is driven by fewer refugee claimants — a federal immigration shift, not provincial housing policy. Province-wide homelessness rose 7.8% in the same period. Fewer tents you can see is not the same as fewer people without homes.

HOW TO USE THESE CARDS

Social media: Each card's bold claim line is the post; the source is the footnote; the rebuttal pre-emption is your reply to hostile comments. Don't wait for the counter — include it.

Door-knocking: One card per conversation. Leave the card behind. Ask: "Did you know this? What do you think your councillor should do about it?"

Deputation: Open with the card's bold claim. Give the source immediately. Spend your time on the rebuttal pre-emption — show you know what the City/Province will say, and show why it doesn't answer the question.

Candidate questionnaires: Each card maps to one of the eleven questions in DERIV-003 (Election Toolkit). Use the card as the predicate; use the DERIV-003 question as the ask.
