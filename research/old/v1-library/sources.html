SECTION A — TORONTO CITY AUDITOR GENERAL

The Toronto AG is an arm's-length officer of City Council. AG reports carry the highest evidentiary weight of any City document: they are based on original access to financial records, contracts, and internal emails, and they are publicly tabled before Council, meaning the auditee cannot quietly bury them.

A-001 | Toronto AG 2025 — Audit of TSSS Warming Centres and Winter Respite Sites

What it is: Value-for-money audit by the Toronto Auditor General (AG Tara Anderson), tabled February 2025. Examines TSSS warming centres and winter respite sites for financial accountability, service quality, and demand management.

Why it matters: This is the most recently tabled primary source on Toronto shelter unit economics. It gives per-night costs, occupancy data, and financial compliance findings directly from audited records — not estimates.

Key findings: 1. The average cost per night of a base shelter space is $136 — the authoritative unit cost for all ROI calculations in this library. 2. During winter 2023-24, 12,742 bed-nights went unfilled at warming centres even while people were sleeping outside — a capacity-access mismatch, not a capacity shortage. 3. 174 people per night were turned away from warming centres at peak demand despite unfilled spaces elsewhere in the system — reflecting intake, referral, and transportation failures rather than bed shortages. 4. Warming centres cost roughly $359 per person per night (press-cited figure; the AG's own chart reports a $336–491 actual range, $266–323 budgeted — see HMK-038's precision note) — more than 2.5× base shelter — due to per-diem leasing, staffing spikes, and short notice. 5. Shelter operators held $2.9 million in unrecovered surpluses — excess revenue over approved budgets that was not returned to the City. 6. The City paid approximately $320M on hotel-shelter operations in 2021 during COVID (AG 2022 follow-up context).

Key statistics: - $136/night base shelter cost ($49,640/year) ✅ - ~$359/night warming centre cost (AG's own reported range: $336–491 actual) ✅ - 12,742 unfilled bed-nights (winter 2023-24) ✅ - 174 nightly turn-aways at peak ✅ - $2.9M unrecovered operator surpluses ✅

Recommendations: Improve demand forecasting; recover unspent surpluses; create minimum performance standards for operator contracts; improve data collection on shelter-seeker needs.

Library claims supported: CL-001, CL-002, CL-003, CL-004, CL-005

Accountability use: The $136/night figure is the foundation of every ROI calculation. Quote: "the average cost of providing emergency shelter … was approximately $136 per person per night." Used to show that Housing First ($6,311/year) saves $43,329/year per person vs. shelter. Also cites the 12,742 unfilled beds as evidence the system is managing wrong, not just underfunded.

URL: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/au/bgrd/backgroundfile-252902.pdf
AG summary page: https://www.torontoauditor.ca/report/[short-url]
Page-level confirmation: The $136/night figure appears in the Background section (pp. 9+) of the full report (backgroundfile-252902.pdf). Independent media confirmation: "a standard shelter bed costs about $136 per night" — blogto.com, Feb 11, 2025, explicitly citing this AG report. The "Audit at a Glance" document (backgroundfile-252938.pdf) confirms winter respite programs start at $135/night (budgeted), consistent with the $136 base shelter figure as the comparison benchmark.
Additional confirmed finding from AG report footnote 9: "Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit average monthly benefit for recipients who entered the program in 2024 was $1,300."

SECTION B — CITY OF TORONTO: SHELTER BUDGETS AND OPERATIONS

These documents are the City's own internal planning and accountability records for the shelter system. They are the primary financial evidence chain.

B-001 | TSSS 2024 Annual Report

What it is: Toronto Shelter and Support Services Annual Report for the 2024 calendar year, published May 2025. This is the division's own self-assessment of its programs, finances, and outcomes.

Why it matters: This is the most important new primary source confirmed in this research cycle. It provides actual 2024 operating figures — not budget projections — and includes a funding breakdown by source that reveals the structural dependency on federal refugee money.

Key findings: 1. TSSS received $796.40M in operating funds in 2024 — a figure dominated by temporary federal emergency funding. 2. The Interim Housing Assistance Program (IHAP) — federal funding for refugee claimant sheltering — contributed $261.87M (33% of the entire budget) in 2024. This is the baseline against which the 2026 drop to $97M must be measured: a $164.87M cliff, not the $100M often cited. 3. The City of Toronto's own tax base contributed only $194.74M (24%) — less than either federal or provincial shares. 4. Emergency shelters consumed $471.79M (59%) of operating spending; refugee claimant response consumed $290.31M (36%). 5. A shelter bed in 2024 served half as many people as it used to. This is the General Manager's own characterization of chronification: beds designed for short stays are now permanently occupied because people have nowhere to exit to. 6. The system provided overnight accommodations to 24,200 unique individuals — but only 4,300+ moved into permanent housing, a housing exit rate of approximately 18%. 7. 12,600+ of 24,200 unique users (52%) were refugee claimants — confirming the structural distortion of the system by a population that belongs in federal immigration settlement, not municipal shelter.

Key statistics: - $796.40M total 2024 operating funds ✅ - $261.87M IHAP (federal, refugee claimant, 33%) ✅ - $194.74M City tax base (24%) ✅ - $200M provincial "New Deal" (25%) ✅ - $114.76M provincial HPP base (14%) ✅ - $471.79M on emergency shelters (59%) ✅ - $290.31M on refugee claimant response (36%) ✅ - 9,700 average nightly users ✅ - 24,200 unique individuals served ✅ - 12,600+ refugee claimants ✅ - 4,300+ housed in permanent housing ✅ - 323,000+ calls to Central Intake ✅ - "Shelter bed in 2024 served half as many people as historically" ✅

Library claims supported: CL-179 through CL-188

Accountability use: The $261.87M IHAP figure is the campaign's strongest evidence for the IHAP cliff argument — it's the City's own accounting, not an advocacy estimate. Pair it with the 2026 TSSS Budget Notes (B-002) showing IHAP drops to $97M. The funding breakdown also demonstrates that Toronto ratepayers are not the primary funders of the shelter system — federal and provincial governments together provide 76% — which directly challenges the "Toronto problem" framing.

URL: https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/903c-2024-TSSS-Annual-ReportAODA.pdf

B-002 | TSSS 2026 Operating Budget Notes

What it is: The City's 2026 budget notes for Toronto Shelter and Support Services, the internal planning document submitted to the Budget Committee. Published February 2026.

Why it matters: This is where the IHAP cliff is first formally documented, and where the COHB suspension's impact on housing exits is quantified.

Key findings: 1. IHAP drops from ~$300M (2025 projected) to $97M (2026) — a structural cliff driven by the end of the emergency federal refugee shelter agreement. 2. Shelter-to-housing exits fell from 5,927 (full year 2023) to 4,344 (full year 2024) — a 26.7% drop correlated with the COHB suspension period (April-October 2024); COHB is funded through the federal NHS Bilateral Agreement, of which Ottawa suspended ~$357M over missed provincial housing targets the same month — the SNA itself names multiple contributing factors, not COHB delays alone. 3. 11 HSCIS sites have been acquired as of 2026 — more than the 7 publicly announced, with 4 additional sites in various stages of confidential acquisition. 4. The 2026 budget projects nightly shelter demand at approximately 9,800 people. 5. "Release a Council-approved Strategic Plan" is listed as a 2026 workplan deliverable — confirming the plan was not approved in 2025 as promised.

Key statistics: - IHAP 2026: $97M (vs $261.87M actual 2024) ✅ - Housing exits: 5,927 (2023) → 4,344 (2024) = -26.7% ✅ - HSCIS sites acquired: 11 ✅ - Nightly demand 2026: ~9,800 ✅

Accountability use: The housing exit collapse is the most clearly documented policy correlation between provincial policy (COHB suspension) and measurable shelter system deterioration. The 1,583 people who did not get housed represent approximately ~$78M (upper bound) in additional annual shelter costs at $136/night.

SECTION C — CITY OF TORONTO: POPULATION COUNTS AND STRATEGIC PLANS

C-001 | 2024 Street Needs Assessment Report

What it is: Toronto's comprehensive point-in-time count and survey of people experiencing homelessness, conducted October 23, 2024 — the largest SNA in the City's history. Published July 2025. Methodology: trained volunteer counters plus surveys of 3,800+ individuals.

Why it matters: The SNA is the primary source for all population-level homelessness data. No other document provides this combination of counts, demographics, and service-use patterns.

Key findings: 1. 15,418 people were experiencing homelessness on October 23, 2024 — the highest count in Toronto's recorded history, and more than double the 2021 figure of ~7,300. 2. 78% meet the definition of chronic homelessness — meaning the system is increasingly warehousing people rather than cycling them through. 3. Black people are profoundly overrepresented: 58-58% of shelter users identify as Black, compared to approximately 10% of Toronto's general population. 4. Indigenous people are profoundly overrepresented in outdoor homelessness: 31% of those sleeping outdoors identify as Indigenous, compared to approximately 3% of Toronto's population. 5. 2SLGBTQ+ representation doubled compared to the 2021 SNA. 6. 1,615 people were sleeping outdoors (including in encampments) on the count night (corrected July 5, 2026 — this bullet previously said "1,446," contradicting the already-corrected 1,615 figure in this same entry's "Key statistics" section below). 7. 9,026 people were in base shelter and overnight sites. 8. 4,409 people were in COVID-related hotel shelters still operating. 9. The outdoor population had shifted geographically: Etobicoke-York and North York showed disproportionate growth relative to the downtown core.

Key statistics: - Total count: 15,418 ✅ - Chronic homelessness: 78% ✅ - Black representation: 58% vs 10% population ✅ - Indigenous outdoor: 31% vs 3% population ✅ - Unsheltered/living outdoors (including in encampments): 1,615 ✅ (corrected July 4, 2026 — a previously-cited "1,446/646" sub-breakdown summed to 2,092, not 1,615, and could not be confirmed for this specific count) - Base shelter: 9,026 ✅

October 2025 update (SNA page): A follow-up count in October 2025 showed approximately 12,180 people — a 21% decrease driven primarily by a 47% drop in refugee claimants in the shelter system. Chronic homelessness was not resolved; geographic spread continued.

Accountability use: The racial overrepresentation data is the equity framing for the entire campaign. The 2× from 2021 to 2024 is the system-failure headline. The outdoor geographic shift to Etobicoke and North York is the ward-level story.

C-002 | What We Heard: 2025-2030 Strategic Plan Stakeholder Engagement Report

What it is: Consultation report prepared by SN Management (Sonja Nerad, Emily Pascual, Tarik Semiz) for TSSS, published April 2025. Based on 20 consultation sessions (September–December 2024) reaching approximately 400 people, plus 176 survey respondents. This is the input document for the 2025-2030 Strategic Plan — which has not yet been approved.

Why it matters: This document contains the shelter sector's self-assessment — what operators, case workers, advocates, and people with lived experience said about the system. It is primary-sourced and produced for the City by the City's own contracted consultants. Its criticisms of the system are therefore not "advocacy" — they are findings.

Key findings: 1. Only 6% of stakeholders said homelessness services are better than five years ago. 66% said they are worse. This is the most powerful single statistic in the library. 2. 47% identified "reducing chronic homelessness" as the system's top priority — above all other stated objectives. 3. The central finding on system culture: "Shelter sector policies, processes, approaches and/or shelter environments reinforce a dependency on shelters and are not sufficiently driving staff or clients to be outcome/housing focused." This is the internal evidence for system chronification — the City's own consultant saying the system is designed to maintain people in shelter, not move them out of it. 4. The SMIS (Shelter Management Information System): "does not sufficiently enable interdivisional or intersectoral coordination and information sharing." Central Intake is "under-resourced and does not have the capacity to systematically match clients to programs that align with their needs." 5. Stakeholders called for purpose-built shelters capped at 100 beds maximum — a direct critique of large congregate shelter models. 6. Stakeholders demanded no one be discharged from correctional, child welfare, or health systems without a confirmed housing plan. 7. Serious shortage of affordable housing identified as the primary structural driver — not individual pathologies. 8. VAW shelter capacity has not kept pace with increased gender-based violence post-pandemic.

Key statistics: - 6% said services improved; 66% said worse ✅ - 47% prioritize chronic homelessness reduction ✅ - 20 consultation sessions; ~400 participants; 176 survey respondents ✅

Accountability use: The 6%/66% numbers are the single most devastating self-indictment in any document the campaign has. They are from the City's own consultation, produced for the City by a City-contracted firm. No advocacy organization said these things — the shelter system said them about itself. Quote the percentages and attribute them correctly: "Toronto's own consultation found…"

C-004 | HousingTO 2024-2025 Annual Progress Report

What it is: Annual progress report on the HousingTO 2020-2030 Action Plan, presented to the Planning and Housing Committee October 30, 2025 (Item PH25.4); adopted by Council November 2025.

Why it matters: This is the definitive mid-point assessment of Toronto's 10-year affordable housing plan. It shows the gap between what the City has approved and what has been built.

Key findings: 1. As of December 31, 2024, the City has approved 29,744 new rent-controlled homes — 46% of the 65,000 target with 60% of the plan period elapsed. 2. Of those, only 632 affordable rental homes were completed in 2024. The pace of completions is far below what is needed to build out the approved pipeline by 2030. 3. The City explicitly states: "the rate of affordable housing starts and completions are not keeping pace with the rate of approvals." 4. City-led and supported housing projects accounted for 65% of all Toronto housing starts in January-August 2025 — despite a 34% market-wide decline in starts in 2024. The City is largely holding the market together. 5. The plan requires $7.6-8.1B in federal grants and $9.1-9.6B in provincial grants to fully achieve its targets. None has been committed. 6. 105,115 active applications (households) are on the social housing (RGI) waiting list (2025 year-end, City's own quarterly Social Housing Waiting List Reports). Fully reconciled July 4, 2026 (see HMK-025 Part 6): the previously "likely different units" hypothesis was close but not quite right — CBC's "92,965 people" (2024) and the "100,500+" figure are the same City series (applications/households) at different quarters (Q3 2024: 92,965; Q4 2024: 100,361; Q1 2025: 104,239). The unit is applications/households throughout — the true number of people waiting is higher than every figure here, since a household application can represent multiple people. 7. RGI approvals are at 42% of target with 60% of time elapsed — the deepest affordability tier is the furthest behind.

Accountability use: The approved-vs-built gap is the central HousingTO accountability argument: "The City counts approvals when reporting progress. They have approved 46% of their goal. They have built less than 5% of what needs to be completed." The $7.6B federal and $9.6B provincial funding gaps that the City says it needs — and that have not been committed — are the provincial and federal accountability arguments.

C-005 | George Street Revitalization — Advancing the Project (March 2025)

What it is: Executive Committee report tabling the status and revised delivery model for the George Street Revitalization, a $610.9M complex replacing Seaton House men's shelter at 339 George Street. Council Item EX22.2, adopted April 23-24, 2025.

Why it matters: GSR is the largest single homelessness capital project in Toronto's history. It replaces Canada's largest emergency shelter (Seaton House) with a continuum of care: emergency shelter, transitional housing, supportive housing, and long-term care.

Key findings: 1. The P3 (Public-Private Partnership) model was abandoned by Council February 6-7, 2024 (Item EX11.9) due to COVID-driven cost volatility and supply chain issues. Replaced with City-delivered Construction Management with CreateTO as project lead. 2. Phase 1 cost: $556.4M for the main building; plus $54.4M for 70 supportive housing units = $610.9M total Phase 1. 3. Phase 2 (south third of the site): unfunded; requires separate planning and financing. 4. Program: 180 transitional shelter beds, 124 long-term care (LTC) beds, 70 supportive housing units = 374 total. 5. LTC beds operated by the City's Seniors Services and Long-Term Care (SSLTC) division — not TSHC (a common misattribution). 6. Construction starts Q4 2026; Phase 1 occupancy targeted for late 2029. 7. The project was approved in principle in 2013. By 2029, it will have taken 16 years from concept to first tenant.

Accountability use: "Canada's largest men's shelter has been waiting since 2013 for its replacement. The replacement will be partially open in 2029. The first modern continuum-of-care model in Toronto's history — 16 years to build."

C-007 | Shelter System Flow Data Dashboard
