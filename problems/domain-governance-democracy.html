PROBLEMS — Governance, democracy & the meta-problems

Tier-1/2 problems in this domain have their own files: C3 Municipal fiscal capacity · F2+H4 Data, privacy & the city's own AI.

Three scored rows — the province's meta-power over the city, the governance/service-performance/ participation compact cluster, and demographic whiplash — plus the polycrisis frame (X15), which the register itself defines as a lens over the whole register rather than a competing row. It's presented last here for that reason.

X08 · Provincial power over the city (municipal autonomy)

The problem. The meta-lever: the province can and does redraw the city's powers mid-game — 1998 amalgamation over local objection, the strong-mayor regime, a council-size cut mid-election. Whatever the ranked list says the city should do, this row decides whether the city may. Full receipts: register entry X08.

What we're asking. Ontario municipalities have almost no fiscal autonomy under current law — how much of "local control" is actually available to Toronto without a provincial change, and how much is aspirational? - The honest FOR/AGAINST on subsidiarity itself — local knowledge and accountability versus local capture, NIMBYism, and problems that are irreducibly large-scale — what does the evidence from federalism and polycentric governance actually show about when decentralization helps and when it harms? (agora A41) - Pushing wealth and resource allocation down, honestly — held against the brutal reality that Ontario municipalities have almost no fiscal autonomy, depend on property tax and provincial transfers, and cannot run deficits — what can actually be decentralized under current law, and what requires provincial change? (agora A44) - The hard legal limits, stated plainly — Ontario municipalities are creatures of the province with no constitutional standing; the City of Toronto Act and Municipal Act define what council can and cannot delegate; what can be done under current law versus what requires provincial legislation, given the province's demonstrated willingness to override the city? (agora A34) - Preventing local capture — pushing power down can simply hand it to whoever already dominates locally; what safeguards keep "local control" from meaning "control by the local powerful," once any power is actually decentralized to Toronto? (agora A45) - Since 1998 amalgamation, the strong-mayor regime, and the mid-election council-size cut are all named as precedents of provincial override, is there a complete timeline anywhere of every provincial action that has redrawn city powers, or does each incident live in a separate, disconnected file?

The shelf. municipal-autonomy-new-deal (full set) · regional-governance-gta (full set).

Unexplored. No single named gaps line in the register; the entry's own framing — "whatever the ranked list says the city should do, this row decides whether the city may" — is itself the open question every other row in the register depends on.

E1 / H5 / H8 · Governance & accountability · service performance · democratic participation

The problem. Strong-mayor powers versus council; 311, permits, and service standards as the trust surface; the participation gap — sortition panels dormant since 2019, the participatory-budgeting pilot never expanded. Full receipts: register entry E1/H5/H8.

What we're asking. Sortition panels have sat dormant since 2019 and the participatory-budgeting pilot never expanded — is that a resourcing choice, a political choice, or has nobody actually decided?

E1 — Governance & accountability. - What does the COUNCILLOR become — since the honest answer to "why representatives" is probably not abolish but transform: does the councillor become less a decider who votes on your behalf and more a convener, a bridge between the deliberating public and the machinery of the state? (agora A30) - "Why elected representatives at all," held as the serious question it is, FOR and AGAINST — the case that representation was a bandwidth workaround for a pre-telegraph world, versus the durable case for the division of labour, expertise, and accountability a full-time representative brings. (agora A29) - Radical transparency as the anti-Sidewalk move — real KPIs, real spending, real service levels, real wait times, updated live, as the public trust-building product, while protecting the legitimately private. (torontoos T27)

H5 — Service performance. - The real systems map, measured not guessed — what does the City of Toronto actually run today (311, permits/building, property tax, transit, the finance backbone), which systems are aging or locked-in, and which vendors hold the keys? (torontoos T13) - Role-appropriate dashboards built on the same underlying truth — genuinely different views for the City Manager, the Mayor, a Department Head, the Public, and the Unions, so no one gets a private, flattering version of reality. (torontoos T26)

H8 — Democratic participation. - The honest evidence on participation instruments themselves — participatory budgeting, citizens' assemblies, deliberative polling — including their real, documented limits (self-selection, the professional commenter, consultation fatigue, "participation-washing"). Which instruments have actually moved outcomes, and which are theatre? (agora A3) - What specifically happened to the sortition panels that went dormant in 2019 and the participatory-budgeting pilot that never expanded — was either ever formally evaluated, or did they simply stop without a public accounting? - Is there a single tracked list anywhere of every 311 service-standard target and the city's actual performance against it, the way transit tracks on-time performance?

The shelf. municipal-governance-accountability (full set, E1) · city-hall-democratic-reform (full set) · government-corruption-transparency (full set) · government-that-works-basics (full set) · innovating-city-government-inertia (full set) · participatory-budgeting-outcomes (full set, H8) · citizens-assemblies-sortition (full set, H8).

Unexplored. No single named gaps line across the compact E1/H5/H8 entry; the 2019 sortition-dormancy and unexpanded-PB-pilot facts are stated as known, but their cause is not — an obvious gap.

X12 · Demographic whiplash

The problem. Canada swung from record intake to three consecutive quarterly population declines (Q3-2025 through Q1-2026) on a policy change; Ontario projects roughly 20.5 million people by 2051. Every growth-built system in this register — housing targets, transit ridership, school enrolment, labour supply, university finance — now has planning assumptions wrong in an unknown direction. Full receipts: register entry X12.

What we're asking. If every growth-built planning assumption in this register is now wrong in an unknown direction, which specific Toronto plans have actually been re-run against the new population trajectory — and which are still running on stale numbers? - Of the growth-built systems the register names — housing targets, transit ridership projections, school enrolment planning, labour supply, university finance — which have publicly acknowledged re-basing their assumptions since the population decline began, and which haven't? - What is the Toronto-CMA-specific 2025–26 actuals receipt the register names as missing — does the city have its own population count independent of the national/provincial figures, and does it show the same decline pattern? - Three consecutive quarterly declines is a real trend, but is it federal-policy-driven, cyclical, or both — and does the answer change how confident anyone should be re-basing a decade-long plan on it right now?

  • Which of Toronto's own plans — housing targets, school builds, transit ridership, long-term care — have actually been re-run against the new population trajectory, and which are still planning for a city that isn't coming?
  • Who does demographic forecasting for planning best — jurisdictions that plan in ranges and re-forecast annually rather than betting everything on one number — and what would it cost Toronto to plan that way?

The shelf. No dedicated backgrounder, dayone, brief, or card exists for this problem in the corpus — SHELF EMPTY. Deep surface: DEMOGRAPHIC FUTURES — aging, migration, fertility, longevity (one of this library's own project records FUTURES-EXPANSION) (⚠️ tier).

Unexplored. Register gap: Toronto-CMA 2025–26 actuals. The question bank returned zero matches for population decline, demographic whiplash, or immigration-levels-versus-planning specifically — total silence, despite this being one of the register's most consequential cross-cutting rows (it touches housing, transit, education, and labour supply all at once).

X15 · The polycrisis — the lens, not a competing row

The Cascade Institute defines a global polycrisis as "the causal entanglement of crises in multiple global systems in ways that significantly degrade humanity's prospects." The register is explicit that this problem is the frame over the whole register, not a row that competes with the others for rank or resources — so it carries no question hierarchy of its own here. Full receipts: register entry X15.

What the frame changes for this domain specifically: the THE RANKED LIST — Toronto & Torontonians’ problems, ranked entanglement map names five load-bearing chains running through the whole problem set, and the fifth — the trust chain — closes its loop directly through this domain: education decay (X05, learning domain) plus isolation (X02, health domain) plus the H8 participation gap plus X08's provincial override risk together weaken the civic capacity needed to respond to the other four chains (price, housing, fiscal, and work). Read that way, X08, H8, and X12 above are not just three more governance rows — they are the chain the register calls "the metacrisis version: the problem-solving system is itself a problem row."

The shelf. No dedicated backgrounder — the register points instead to INDEX — this library's internal records/ (entry point) and DR3 — Directory Index as its depth surfaces.

Unexplored. Register gap: a Toronto entanglement map (named as a candidate future artifact — not yet built).

Across this domain — steal from the world, collect everything, find the best minds

  • Who does city democracy and city power best — Madrid's Decide platform and Paris's participatory budget at nine-figure scale, Ireland's citizens' assemblies that settled questions parliaments couldn't, Taiwan's vTaiwan digital deliberation, the US home-rule charters that give cities real constitutional standing — what EXACTLY does each do, and which piece does Toronto steal for participation and for its provincial-power problem?
  • Collect ALL of it: every governance-reform and democratic-participation recommendation already made for Toronto — the charter-city literature, the governance reviews, the PB pilot evaluations, decades of civic-society proposals — what was recommended and what died in committee?
  • Who are the best minds and organizations on multilevel governance and democratic innovation anywhere — and who in this city's own civic-tech and academic community is already world-class and politely ignored?
