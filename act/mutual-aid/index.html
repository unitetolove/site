Nobody in Beaches–East York should face a hard week alone.

This riding lives like two different cities sharing one main street. South of the tracks: leafy streets, four residents’ associations, three-quarters of neighbours voting. North-east of Main and Danforth: the towers — Crescent Town, Teesdale, Dawes — where two-thirds of households rent, where a dozen languages share the elevators, and where participation in almost everything public runs twenty points lower. Not because people care less. Because nobody built the connections.

So neighbours are building them. Beaches–East York Mutual Aid Networks — “BEY Mutual Aid” on the street — is not a charity, not an agency, not a program, and absolutely not a political campaign. It is the oldest technology there is: people who live near each other, helping each other, on purpose, with just enough structure that nobody falls through.

Need a hand? Start here.

Meals or groceries · a ride · a prescription picked up · something heavy moved · snow, yard, small fixes · a check-in call · company · help with forms or phone calls. Small things — which are never small when you need one.

  • In person: our table at the East Lynn Park Farmers’ Market, 1949 Danforth Ave, every Thursday 3–7 p.m. — or any neighbour carrying our paper form.
  • Email: unitetolove@gmail.com — say what you need and how to reach you. That’s the whole application.
  • Today / urgent: 211 connects you to every service in the city, 24/7, in 150+ languages — and our directory lists the doors we’ve verified are actually open in this neighbourhood. Emergencies: 911.

The floor we will not cross: no eligibility tests. No proof of need. No “deserving.” Ever. The moment neighbours have to audition for help, the thing has failed. You ask; we try; that’s the contract.

Got a hand to give?

One hour or ten. Once or weekly. Cooking, driving, translating, fixing, phoning, visiting — or hosting ten neighbours in your yard once a season. Here’s how giving works →

How the network actually works

We studied the networks that worked — and the ones that died — before building anything (the research). The design that survives is three things:

  • The loop: ask → match → done. One form, one neighbour who says yes, one “done.” New York proved it at COVID scale; Los Angeles proved it holds in disaster.
  • The rhythm: a standing weekly presence (Thursdays at East Lynn), because networks with a heartbeat outlive networks with a launch. The groups that lasted in the UK had one thing in common: they kept meeting, and they were fun.
  • The cells: block circles — ten-ish households who know each other’s names. History’s verdict is blunt: cells outlive umbrellas. So the umbrella stays light and the streets hold the weight.

What makes this one different

  • Verified, not vibes: the directory lists only organizations we checked are alive and answering — because sending a hungry neighbour to a dead phone number is its own harm.
  • Receipts, not press releases: what we actually did publishes weekly — including the needs we couldn’t meet.
  • No money, full stop: we never collect a dollar. Give directly to the verified organizations doing the work.
  • Non-partisan, completely: every neighbour of every politics, and the network never wears anyone’s campaign shirt.
  • Your privacy is structural: what you share is never posted, never sold, never published, and removed the moment you ask.

Take a piece of it home — print-ready, today

  • The paper form — print a stack, put one on every fridge on your floor. It IS the network.
  • The block-circle starter card and the assembly invitation — hosts start here.
  • The sign-up sheet — for anyone running a table anywhere.

The guides — take the thinking, not just the form

Four short guides, free to anyone and delivered identically to every candidate’s team in both by-elections: Mutual Aid in One Page · the Candidate-Team Playbook · the No-Money Rule · the Pull-Together Strategy.

The assemblies — where the neighbourhood decides

Mutual aid meets the needs; neighbourhood assemblies decide what this area does about them together. Rooms are being organized now, convened by local hosts in their own names — congregations, community centres, living rooms. Want to host one? The complete Citizens’ Assembly in a Box kit is free, and the invitation is written — you add the time and place. Want to be invited? Say so and you’re on the list.

And behind all of it

This page is one door in a much bigger house, all free, all open: Free Toronto — hundreds of verified free and low-cost resources across the city · the kits shelf — run a vision dinner, a complaint clinic, a story evening, an assembly · the guides — practical, sourced, written for real life · the homelessness library — the receipts behind why the Danforth corridor is where we started.

We never handle money. No donations are taken here or anywhere — if you want to give money, give it directly to the verified organizations in the directory.

Volunteer-run and non-partisan — no party, no candidate, no memberships, nothing for sale. Beaches–East York Mutual Aid Networks (“BEY Mutual Aid”) is part of Toronto’s Mutual Aid Networks: a network of networks — nobody owns mutual aid.
