Mutual Aid, in One Page

One of four short guides written for everyone — including every candidate's team in both by-elections, delivered identically.

Mutual aid is neighbours meeting each other's needs, together. Someone needs a ride to an appointment; someone three streets over has a car and a free morning. Mutual aid is the simple machine that connects those two people — and the habit of keeping that machine running.

It is not charity. Charity has a giver and a receiver, a form to fill out, and someone deciding whether you deserve the help. Mutual aid has none of that. Nobody here will ask you to prove you need something. Nobody keeps score. The person asking for help this week may be the one offering help next week — that isn't a nice accident, it's the whole design. The old organizing phrase is "solidarity, not charity," and we mean it exactly: no eligibility tests, no proof of need, no line between the helpers and the helped.

Toronto has done this before. In fact, Toronto taught the world: CareMongering-TO started here in March 2020 and became the fastest-growing mutual aid movement ever measured — more than 25,000 members, copied in 246 chapters around the globe. And then it died. Not because people stopped caring, but because enthusiasm alone is not a structure. There was no steady rhythm, no shared roles, nobody whose actual job was looking after the volunteers. Researchers who studied thousands of similar groups found the same pattern everywhere: the groups that lasted had regular meetings, a culture of care, and real joy in the room; the groups that ran on adrenaline collapsed when the adrenaline ran out. This network is built from that lesson up — a fixed daily rhythm for the two-week launch, a weekly one for the long haul after, shared roles that rotate, and care for the carers, from day one.

Beaches–East York Mutual Aid Networks — "BEY Mutual Aid" for short — is four plain things. A directory: one page pointing to the real help that already exists here, like Community Centre 55, Eastview's food access program, the Salvation Army on Cedarvale, and 211 — we point to them, we never duplicate them. Daily Hub Hours: tables at each of the riding's TTC stations, morning to late evening, every day of the two-week launch — walk up, no sign-up. (After the sprint, a weekly Hub Hour that lasts.) An ask-and-offer loop: you say what you need or what you can give, a volunteer matches the two, done. And block circles: about ten households on a stretch of street, meeting because they chose to, doing whatever their block actually needs.

Joining takes nothing but showing up, and there are three doors. Come to a Hub Hour — just walk in, no sign-up. Join the Discord, where the asking and offering happens online. Or simply talk to a neighbour who's already in — a paper form or a phone call counts exactly as much as anything on a screen. No app is required to belong here. The front door is a neighbour.

Nobody owns mutual aid — this is a network of networks, and it becomes yours the moment you show up.
