The Unity Lexicon

Ten ideas about union. What English’s warmest words are actually made of. And a hypothesis about one word that keeps appearing, in language after language spoken in this city — which we need you to check.

Research draft v0.1 — being verified with Toronto’s communities
Every entry on this page is an invitation to correct us. If one of these is your language, tell us what we got wrong, at the Harvest.

Why this page exists

The Day 7 letter ends on a practice its author calls unity — yoga, in Sanskrit, from the root yuj, to yoke. Two things joined, and both still there.

That one word opened a question we could not put down: what do Toronto’s other languages call this? This page is our first attempt at an answer. It is early, some of it is wrong, and that is why it is here: we would rather publish unfinished and be corrected by the people whose words these are, than publish late and polished by strangers.

Ten things English needs ten words for

Start in English. Here are ten ideas that sit very close together, circling one centre. English keeps them apart — some in a spiritual register, some in a civic one, with almost nothing crossing between.

ConceptWhat we mean by it here
UnionTwo or more becoming one without either ceasing to exist. The hinge.
WholenessMind, body, breath and spirit as one integrated person, not four systems sharing a skin.
KinshipThe other recognised as a version of me. Not sympathy for; identity with.
BelongingMembership in a place. Not ownership of it, and not stewardship from above.
CommunionContact with the whole — however you name it.
TogethernessThe plain fact of being with people. Presence, not project.
ConnectionThe tie itself: the thread, the knot, the graft, the mooring line.
CommunityNot people who live nearby. People who owe each other something.
Mutual aidHorizontal and reciprocal. Not help for the needy — help among people who will each need it.
SolidarityNot a feeling. Exposure. Your loss is collectible from me.

This grouping is ours. It is a frame for asking a question, not a finding about anything.

What English’s warmest words are made of

Now look at what those English words are built from. Every line below is a standard dictionary etymology — check any of them.

WordComes fromLiterally
solidarityRoman law, in solidumeach debtor liable for the entire debt
fellowshipOld Norse félagifé (livestock, wealth) + lag (to lay down) — one who puts money in the same pot
communityLatin com- + munustogether under a shared duty, or a shared gift
companyLatin com- + panisthose who share bread
unionLatin unusone
yogaSanskrit yujto yoke — and English yoke is its cousin, from the same ancient root

Five of those six are contracts, debts, meals or farm equipment. English’s tenderest vocabulary for human closeness is built almost entirely out of legal and agricultural instruments. The one abstraction in the set — union — is the one that aged into a slogan.

Which is the best answer we know to the charge that a message about unity is soft. It was never soft. It was enforceable.

A hypothesis: the word for unity might be the name of a day

Here is the idea this page exists to test. We are calling it a hypothesis because that is all it is.

In English, unity is a noun for a state or a feeling. In a great many other languages spoken in this city, the word that seems to carry the whole cluster at once is not an abstract noun at all. It is the name of an occasion: the day the neighbours turn up and work. The roof gets raised, the harvest comes in, the well gets dug, the body gets buried. Nobody is paid. Everybody will need it.

If that is right, then the most widely shared human word for unity is not we are one. It is we showed up.

Eighteen words we are trying to get right

We have written each of these in the Latin alphabet only. Not because the scripts do not matter — because printing somebody’s script wrong is worse than not printing it, and we have not earned these yet. If this is your language, send us the spelling in your own writing and we will use it.

Every gloss below is how we currently understand the word. Assume we have flattened it.

Some of these come from communities of tens of thousands in Toronto, some from a few hundred. We chose for spread, not size, and we left out far more than we put in — including every word we were not confident enough to write down.

The strongest argument against all of this

An honest page prints the objection at full strength. Here it is, and it may well be right.

Every pre-industrial farming society faced the same problem. Harvests and house-building need more hands than one household has, on dates nobody controls, in an economy with no cash to hire with. Reciprocal labour is the only available answer. Finding the same institution in dozens of unrelated places is therefore about as remarkable as finding granaries in dozens of unrelated places. It may tell you something about agriculture and nothing at all about the human heart.

We think that is a serious argument, and we are not going to pretend we have beaten it. What we would say back is narrower. Whatever caused it, dozens of communities in this one city each carry a word for this. Many carry a living memory of it. Several are still running the institution here, now — the savings circles, the burial societies, the block that digs itself out. Even if the cause is mundane, the inheritance is real, and it is local.

Which reading is right is an open question. We would like your argument on it, too.

What this page still owes

Several traditions have looked hard at the idea of merging into one and deliberately declined it, with centuries of argument behind them. They are not oversights here; they are the part we have not built yet, and they deserve to be set out in their own words rather than summarised in ours.

And not every work party in history was freely joined. Reciprocal labour has carried caste, debt, and obligations you could not refuse without ruin. A page that prints only the warm version of this is propaganda. We would rather say that ourselves than have it said to us.

Toronto

There is one hard number underneath all of this. Toronto’s 2021 census counts more than seventy languages with over a thousand mother-tongue speakers each, inside the city boundary alone (City of Toronto, 2021 Census Backgrounder: Language). This page currently reaches eighteen of them, imperfectly. That ratio is why it is published as a draft.

Correct us

If you speak one of these languages, teach it, pray in it, or grew up hearing it from an elder — you are the reason this page exists. Three things we need:

  1. The word your community actually uses. Not the dictionary word. The one people say.
  2. What we got wrong above. Spelling, meaning, tone, who says it and when — or the whole idea.
  3. Whether your community wants its word here at all. Any word comes down on request, no explanation needed.
