Scope

This backgrounder's scope, per the page’s own scope line (that page's own internal recordsthis page’s own scope note): pedestrian/cyclist collisions and traffic calming, read together with the "Vision Zero" framing in the page’s slug — Toronto's and Ontario's road-safety policy architecture (target-setting, infrastructure countermeasures, enforcement technology) as it bears on collision, injury, and fatality outcomes for all road users, with particular attention to vulnerable road users (pedestrians, cyclists, motorcyclists). This document covers: Ontario- and Toronto-specific collision/fatality/injury statistics and trends; the Vision Zero policy framework and its adoption by Toronto and peer Ontario municipalities; automated and traditional enforcement tools and their current legal status; and traffic-calming/infrastructure countermeasures. It does not cover, and hands off by name: cycling network scale, prioritization, and mode-share policy as its own subject (active-transportation-cycling, though collision data involving cyclists is covered here as a road-safety outcome); on/off-street parking supply and pricing (parking-policy-management); broader congestion/transit service levels (mobility-congestion-transit); and the specific mechanics of Bill 212's bike-lane-removal provisions themselves (covered by active-transportation-cycling and, per the existing mobility-congestion-transit backgrounder, cited here only insofar as the litigation and its underlying safety evidence bear on road-safety outcomes directly).

Current state

The scale of the problem: national and provincial trends

Canada recorded 1,964 motor vehicle fatalities in 2023, up 1.7% from 1,931 in 2022; pedestrians accounted for 298 of these fatalities (15.2% of the total) and cyclists for 47 (2.4%), per Transport Canada's collision statistics [CL-0136]. Statistics Canada found more than 300 pedestrian fatalities occurred in Canada on average each year from 2018 to 2020, with the death rate 1.7 times higher among males (1.01 per 100,000 population) than females (0.61 per 100,000), and particularly high among men aged 70 and older (2.65 per 100,000) [CL-0135]. At least one risk factor was reported in 65% of these pedestrian fatalities, and at least two risk factors in a further share; more than 1 in 4 (26%) occurred at night; and alcohol, cannabis, or other drug consumption was reported in 1 in 5 (20%) [CL-0720, CL-0721, CL-0722]. Between 2006 and 2017, 890 cyclists died in Canada, an average of 74 deaths per year, with the age-standardized cycling death rate falling over the period [CL-0139].

Longer-run national trends are genuinely improving on several dimensions even as they worsen on others. From 2011 to 2020, fatality and serious-injury rates per billion vehicle-kilometres travelled in Canada declined by 22% and 35% respectively [CL-0726], and fatal-collision involvement rates for young drivers under 20 decreased 34% over the same period [CL-0730]. Canada's Road Safety Strategy 2035 and Beyond reports that, compared with 20 years earlier, national road fatalities had declined roughly 28% [CL-0732]. But the trend is not uniform by road-user class: from 2011 to 2020, pedestrian fatalities decreased 11% and cyclist fatalities also decreased, while motorcyclist fatalities increased 40% [CL-0731]. Distraction was a factor in 21% of Canadian road fatalities in 2020, unchanged from the share in 2010 — a persistent risk factor that has not improved over a decade [CL-0729]. Transport Canada estimated the social cost of motor vehicle collisions in Canada at $36 billion for 2020 [CL-0728], and in 2020 collisions on Canadian roads resulted in an estimated 104,286 injured victims and 1,746 fatalities [CL-0725]. Internationally, Canada's road fatality rate ranked 12th among 22 OECD member countries compared in a 2018-era report [CL-0727], and 12th globally in road fatalities per billion vehicle-kilometres travelled in 2023 (roughly 4.7 fatalities per billion VKT) [CL-0734] — a mid-pack performance, neither a leader nor a laggard among wealthy peer countries.

Ontario's own record is comparatively strong within North America: the province recorded 623 road fatalities in 2022, a rate of 0.56 fatalities per 10,000 licensed drivers — the second-lowest fatality rate in North America after the Northwest Territories [CL-0134] — and in 2020, Ontario's fatality rate of 0.50 per 10,000 licensed drivers (530 total fatalities) was the lowest in North America that year [CL-0735]. Ontario's road-safety annual reports page states the province has ranked among the top five North American jurisdictions for road safety for 26 consecutive years [CL-0724]. Serious injuries on Ontario's roads fell to 1,351 in 2020, a decrease of 45.3% over the preceding decade [CL-0736]; per the province's own 2020 report, on average one person was killed on Ontario roads every 16 hours that year [CL-0737]. Within that overall improvement, vulnerable road users bore a disproportionate and in some cases worsening share: Ontario pedestrian fatalities fell from 129 in 2019 to 116 in 2020 (down 10%), but pedestrians' share of all road fatalities rose over the same period [CL-0738], and motorcycle rider fatalities rose from 60 to 81 (up 35%) while bicycling fatalities held flat at 23 in both years [CL-0744]. Impairment-related trends were mixed: drinking-and-driving fatalities fell from 108 to 98 (down 9%) [CL-0741], but fatalities attributed to drugs other than alcohol rose from 64 to 77 (up 20%) [CL-0742], and inattentive-driving deaths fell from 95 to 74 (down 22%) [CL-0740]. Despite a 96% seatbelt-usage rate in a Transport Canada survey, about one in five vehicle occupants killed on Ontario roads was unbelted [CL-0743]. Large-truck-involved fatalities fell from 126 to 85 (down 32%), with 3.5% of examined large trucks found with some form of defect [CL-0739].

Toronto's Vision Zero record

Toronto adopted its Vision Zero Road Safety Plan in 2016. Thirty-nine individuals were killed on Toronto's roads in 2025, a 50% reduction from 2016 and the second-lowest total since municipal amalgamation [CL-0745; corroborated independently, NEW-1]. Of the 39 deaths in 2025, 19 were pedestrians, 12 were motorists, five were motorcyclists, two were cyclists, and one was a micromobility-vehicle rider [NEW-1] — meaning pedestrians alone accounted for roughly half of all 2025 road deaths despite comprising a much smaller share of trips, consistent with the national and provincial pattern of vulnerable-road-user overrepresentation described above. As of February 19, 2026, Toronto's provisional 2025 serious-injury collision count stood at 228, estimated to rise to about 255 once outstanding reports are finalized — itself a 24% reduction in serious-injury collisions since 2016 and the third-lowest total since amalgamation, excluding the pandemic years 2020 and 2021 [CL-0746; NEW-1]. Toronto's average annual traffic fatality rate from 2020 to 2024 was 1.69 people killed per 100,000 population [CL-0747]. ⚠️ Still being checked: 2025 figures are explicitly provisional per the City's own dashboard methodology, with up to a one-year delay before serious-injury data is officially verified and reported [NEW-1] — the 255 estimate and the "second-lowest"/"third-lowest" framings should be read as subject to later revision.

Toronto's own collision data shows the safety problem is sharply concentrated rather than diffuse. From 2021 to 2025, 83% of the city's serious injuries and fatalities occurred on arterial roads, which make up only 21% of Toronto's total road network [CL-0748] — a striking concentration that identifies arterials as the highest-leverage target for infrastructure countermeasures. Demographically, 29% of pedestrian collisions in the same period involved seniors aged 65 and older, who make up only 17% of the population, while a further disproportionate share (23%, per the same claim) involved another named group [CL-0749] — both findings consistent with the national pattern of age-related vulnerability documented above [CL-0135].

Peer Ontario municipalities

Ottawa's 2020-2024 Road Safety Action Plan reports 631 fatal or major-injury (FMI) collisions over that five-year period, down from 743 over 2013-2017; pedestrians were involved in 156 of the 2020-2024 collisions (25% of the total, down from 26%) and cyclists in 60 (10%, down from 11%) [CL-0137]. Over the preceding five years, Ottawa averaged 24 fatal and 124 major-injury collisions annually [CL-0750]. Ottawa's plan cites research finding pedestrians struck by a vehicle at 30 km/h have roughly a 10% chance of dying, a figure that rises sharply at higher speeds [CL-0752], and modelling finding a 15% increase in vehicle operating speed is associated with a 65% increase in fatal-collision risk [CL-0753] — the empirical basis Ontario Vision Zero plans generally cite for prioritizing speed reduction over other interventions. A 2017 survey of Ottawa youth found 16% of Grade 7-12 students had been a passenger in the past year with a driver who had been drinking [CL-0751].

Mississauga's Vision Zero Action Plan (2021) outlines 99 actions toward zero fatal and serious-injury collisions, including automated speed enforcement, a crossing-guard program, and temporary neighbourhood traffic-calming ("Slow Streets") [CL-0138], and proposed reducing default speed limits from 50 km/h to 40 km/h on local roads [CL-0759]. Mississauga's plan itself cites the Vision Zero approach as originating in Sweden in 1997 [CL-0758]. Hamilton recorded an average of approximately 8,200 total collisions a year from 2013 to 2017, 95% of which were vehicle-only [CL-0754], with 2,078 collisions involving cyclists or pedestrians over the same period (1,236 pedestrian, 842 cyclist) [CL-0755]; from 2011 to 2015 Hamilton averaged 16 fatal collisions and over 1,800 non-fatal injury collisions per year [CL-0756]. A Hamilton road-safety survey found over 90% of respondents agreed or strongly agreed that Hamilton roads could be safer [CL-0757].

Enforcement technology: automated speed enforcement, its evidence base, and its provincial ban

Automated speed enforcement (ASE) — camera-based systems ticketing speeding vehicles without an officer present, typically deployed in school and community safety zones — has been a central plank of Ontario municipal Vision Zero programs, including Toronto's, since Toronto City Council approved its ASE contract with Redflex Traffic Systems (Canada) Limited in July 2019 [NEW-2]. Redflex became a subsidiary of Verra Mobility Corporation (NASDAQ: VRRM) following a 2021 acquisition [NEW-2]. As directed by Council, an additional 75 ASE cameras were deployed across Toronto in the first quarter of 2025 [NEW-2], reflecting continued municipal investment in the tool immediately before its legal footing changed.

The evidence base cited for ASE's effectiveness is direct and recent: a study by the Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids) and Toronto Metropolitan University found automated speed cameras reduced speeding by 45% in Toronto school zones, cut the 85th-percentile maximum speed by 10.7 km/h, and cut the share of vehicles travelling 20 km/h or more over the posted limit by 87% [independently re-verified 2026-07-16, direct fetch of Toronto Metropolitan University's own press release, "Automated cameras cut speeding by 45 per cent in Toronto school zones, study finds," torontomu.ca, 2025-07-25, and of the underlying study itself, Howard et al., "Automated speed enforcement reduced vehicle speeds in school zones in Toronto: a prospective quasi-experimental study," Injury Prevention, published online 2025-07-23, https://injuryprevention.bmj.com/content/early/2025/07/23/ip-2024-045561 (study monitored 250 Toronto school zones, July 2020–December 2022); study first authored by Dr. Andrew Howard (SickKids) and senior-authored by Prof. Linda Rothman (TMU), funded by CIHR and a City of Toronto evaluation grant]. Note the figure is 87%, not 88% — the 88% figure traced to Verra Mobility's own press release paraphrase of the study (see NEW-4 below) is a minor transcription slip from the study's/TMU's own 87% figure; corrected here per direct fetch of the primary source. Public support for the tool was also high: a Canadian Automobile Association-affiliated survey found 73% of Ontario drivers supported automated speed enforcement, per Verra Mobility's press release citing CAA/CAA South Central Ontario's own published spotlight piece (caasco.com) [NEW-3].

Despite this, the Ontario government passed Bill 56, the Building a More Competitive Economy Act, 2025, which received Royal Assent November 3, 2025 and took effect November 14, 2025, repealing Part XIV.1 of the Highway Traffic Act — the provision that had authorized municipalities and police services to operate automated speed enforcement systems [NEW-2]. The province's stated rationale was that municipalities had overused ASE for revenue generation rather than genuine safety purposes [NEW-2]; the Ontario government's own reducing-speeding-in-real-time page states a single municipal speed camera in the City of Toronto generated $7 million in revenue, and CP24 reported the same camera (on Parkside Drive) issued more than 65,000 tickets for nearly $7 million in fines prior to 2025 [independently re-verified 2026-07-16, direct fetch of ontario.ca/page/reducing-speeding-real-time and CP24, "Ontario invests $210M in traffic calming measures to replace speed cameras," 2025-11-13]. The legislative process was fast-tracked, with critics noting it limited debate and skipped public hearings before passage [NEW-4]. Unlike the earlier characterization in this review, the province did name a specific, dated, costed replacement commitment: on November 13, 2025, the Ontario government announced $210 million for traffic-calming infrastructure (speed bumps, raised crosswalks, roundabouts, signage) and increased police enforcement in school and community safety zones through the new Road Safety Initiatives Fund (RSIF), with $42 million released immediately to municipalities and the remaining $168 million to be applied for by eligible municipalities beginning February 19, 2026 [independently re-verified 2026-07-16, direct fetch of CP24, "Ontario invests $210M in traffic calming measures to replace speed cameras," Nov. 13, 2025, and ontario.ca/page/reducing-speeding-real-time]. ⚠️ Still being checked: the specific figure of "$10 million to Toronto" from the immediate $42 million tranche was not confirmed by direct fetch of the CP24 article in this review — the article states only that "the amount for each city will be shared with them individually before cameras are turned off," not a Toronto-specific figure; a WebSearch synthesis (not independently fetched) suggests $10 million to Toronto, but this remains unconfirmed against a primary source and should be treated as provisional pending direct confirmation. Verra Mobility announced November 21, 2025 that it was exiting Ontario as a direct result of the ban — not a voluntary business decision, per the company's own framing — with an expected $7 million in lost annual Ontario revenue, already factored into its 2026 outlook [independently re-verified 2026-07-16, direct fetch, exact quote verified verbatim: "Exiting Ontario was not a decision that Verra Mobility made; instead, it was mandated by provincial law that overrides municipalities' ability to use speed cameras, effective November 14, 2025," Verra Mobility press release, "Verra Mobility exits Ontario as new speed camera ban takes effect," ir.verramobility.com, 2025-11-21, fetched 2026-07-16].

Municipal and road-safety-professional reaction was critical: mayors and councillors from Toronto, Ottawa, and Hamilton called on the province to reverse course or exempt school zones, several municipalities explored legal options challenging the ban as an overreach into local traffic-safety authority, and the Ontario Association of Chiefs of Police publicly opposed the ban, calling ASE "a proven life-saving technology" [NEW-5]. More than 20 mayors across the province asked the Ford government to tweak rather than scrap the speed-camera program; the province denied the request [independently re-verified 2026-07-16, per direct fetch below].

A report considered by Toronto city council (reported by The Canadian Press, republished by CHCH and other outlets, and originally covered by The Trillium) found a dramatic, measured increase in speeding in Toronto in the months after the province pulled speed cameras: the report found vehicle speeds increased at 101 of 104 locations with comparable before-and-after data; the share of drivers travelling 16 km/h or more over the posted limit surged nearly 400% on some streets; the share travelling 11 km/h or more over the posted limit rose from 2% to 8.1%; the increase was "generally more prominent" on streets posted at 30 km/h; and Toronto recorded 25 fatal collisions in the six months after camera removal, higher than the same period in the previous three years but on par with 2021-22 — though the report itself cautions that multiple factors affect fatality counts and more time is needed to isolate the camera-removal effect specifically [independently re-verified 2026-07-16, direct fetch, The Canadian Press, "Speeding spiked in Toronto after province pulled speed cameras: report," first published 2026-06-24, republished by CHCH News, https://www.chch.com/chch-news/speeding-spiked-in-toronto-after-province-pulled-speed-cameras-report/ — the original Trillium URL (thetrillium.ca) remains JS-gated/returns no content on direct fetch, consistent with an earlier finding that several Ontario news outlets' own sites are JS-gated even when not CBC; the CHCH/Canadian Press republication carries the same wire-service text and was fetched successfully]. This upgrades and confirms NEW-6, previously flagged as unconfirmed because direct fetch returned no content.

The revenue-sharing structure ASE operated under

Municipal ASE programs retained only a modest share of gross ticket revenue after paying the vendor, the provincial offences court system, and city processing/staffing costs — though the exact share is time-period-dependent and contested rather than a single stable figure. Reporting on a comparator municipality (Guelph) for the program's first five months (August–December 2023) found the city retained just 15.6% ($70,000 of $447,120 in total revenue) after these costs, with officials citing vendor-contract confidentiality as the reason exact cost breakdowns were not disclosed [independently re-verified 2026-07-16, direct fetch, GuelphToday, "City keeps very little of money generated by automated speed cameras," accessed 2026-07-16 — figure and both dollar amounts confirmed verbatim against the article's own text]. Needs a judgment call, not independently resolved this review: a later WebSearch snippet (not independently fetched from a primary Guelph source) indicates 2024 accounting showed Guelph retaining 68% of speed-camera revenue, with 32% going to the joint Toronto processing centre and the vendor — a materially different figure from the 15.6% first-five-months snapshot. This document does not resolve which figure better represents the program's steady-state revenue-sharing structure; both may be independently true of their respective periods (an early, high-fixed-cost startup phase vs. a later, more revenue-mature phase), but this backgrounder's use of 15.6% as if representative of the general ASE revenue-sharing structure should be read with this caveat, and a future pass should independently fetch and reconcile both Guelph figures against primary municipal accounting before either is cited as the general/steady-state number [NEW-5]. This structure is directly relevant to the province's stated "cash grab" rationale for the ban: while headline fine totals were large, the municipality's own net retained revenue was, at least in the program's early period, a small fraction of that headline figure, with the vendor and the court system absorbing the majority.

Speed and traffic-calming as the underlying design philosophy

The "safe systems" premise underlying Vision Zero — that the road system's designers, not only individual road users, bear responsibility for crash outcomes, and that infrastructure should be forgiving of human error — rests substantially on the speed-risk relationship Ottawa's plan cites directly [CL-0752, CL-0753]: pedestrian survivability falls steeply as impact speed rises, and even modest speed increases are associated with disproportionate increases in fatal-collision risk. This is the evidentiary basis for the traffic-calming, speed-limit-reduction, and physical-infrastructure emphasis common across the Toronto, Mississauga, Ottawa, and Hamilton plans reviewed here, as distinct from — and, per the province's stated Bill 56 rationale, now explicitly preferred over — camera-based enforcement.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

The province's own road-safety strategy commits to an aggressive fatality-reduction target while, in the same period, removing a tool with direct, recent, locally-produced evidence of effectiveness. Canada's Road Safety Strategy 2035+ supports the UN Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021-2030's goal of a 50% reduction in road deaths and injuries [CL-0733], and Ontario's own ASE cameras were found by a SickKids/TMU study to cut Toronto school-zone speeding by 45% and cut severe speeding (20+ km/h over) by 87% [NEW-3, independently re-verified 2026-07-16]. Bill 56 removed the tool that produced that specific, measured result, on revenue-motive grounds, while committing $210 million to a physical traffic-calming replacement ($42 million released immediately, balance contingent on municipal plans) [independently re-verified 2026-07-16] — a genuine tension between a stated harm-reduction commitment and a policy action removing a directly-evidenced harm-reduction tool, and this tension is now sharpened by direct evidence rather than left as a plausible inference: Toronto's own post-ban data shows a measured speeding increase at 101 of 104 comparable locations [NEW-6, independently re-verified 2026-07-16]. The replacement commitment is not unspecified: it is named, dated, and costed, even though its area-by-area/school-zone allocation and full disbursement timeline were not independently traced in this review.

Toronto's own Vision Zero results are real and specific, but arrive alongside a worsening pattern for particular road-user classes the aggregate figures can obscure. The city's 50%-since-2016 fatality reduction [CL-0745] and 24%-since-2016 serious-injury reduction [CL-0746] are genuine, falsifiable, and independently corroborated results. But nationally, motorcyclist fatalities rose 40% over a decade even as pedestrian and cyclist fatalities fell [CL-0731], and in Ontario specifically, pedestrians' share of fatalities rose even as their absolute numbers fell [CL-0738] — meaning an aggregate "Vision Zero is working" headline can be true while a vulnerable-road-user-specific picture is more mixed. This backgrounder does not resolve which framing is more representative; both are independently sourced.

A tool's cost-effectiveness for the public purse and its safety effectiveness are two different questions this evidence base answers differently. ASE's safety evidence (45%/87% school-zone speeding reductions) is a genuinely different question from whether the fiscal structure around it — a majority of gross ticket revenue reportedly going to the vendor and the court system rather than the municipality, at least in Guelph's early-program period [NEW-5; needs a judgment call on later-period figures, see above] — represented a defensible use of an enforcement mechanism. The province's stated "cash grab" framing addresses the second question; the safety evidence addresses the first; conflating the two would misstate either side's actual position, and this document keeps them separate rather than allowing one to stand in for the other.

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. The UN Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021-2030 — a resolution and coordinated global programme adopted by the UN General Assembly, not a binding treaty — set a global target of a 50% reduction in road traffic deaths and injuries by 2030, with the WHO and UN regional commissions publishing a Global Plan in 2021 as its implementation blueprint [existing claims register claim CL-0733 already cites Canada's own Road Safety Strategy 2035+ as supporting this same UN Decade of Action target — named here precisely as a resolution/programme, not a treaty, per this template's own discipline]. No binding international human-rights instrument was identified in this review as directly engaging road-safety policy specifically (as distinct from, e.g., a general right-to-life framing too diffuse to cite meaningfully here).

2. Best global comparators. Oslo, Norway achieved zero pedestrian and cyclist traffic deaths in 2019 — and again in 2023, per the city's own reporting — with the city's sole 2019 traffic fatality a motorist [independently re-verified 2026-07-16, direct fetch of Transport for London (TfL), "Achieving Vision Zero — Case study: Road safety in Oslo," content.tfl.gov.uk/vision-zero-case-study-oslo.pdf, an institutional case study built on content the City of Oslo itself provided, quoting Marit Kristine Vea, Vice Mayor of Environment and Transport in Oslo: "Oslo achieved zero pedestrian and cyclist fatalities in both 2019 and 2023." The same source states Oslo built 155km of new/upgraded cycle infrastructure 2015-2023, achieved a 47% reduction in fatal/serious cycling-collision risk since a 2015 baseline, has a 2030 target of a 30% traffic reduction versus 2015, and that nearly 70% of Oslo streets already have a 30 km/h speed limit]. Helsinki, Finland achieved the same zero-pedestrian/cyclist-death milestone in 2019, the first such year since 1960 when the city's traffic-death records began, with three total traffic deaths that year (one motorist, two motorcyclists) [independently re-verified 2026-07-16, direct fetch of the City of Helsinki's own press release, "Zero pedestrian and cyclist fatalities in Helsinki in 2019," published via Cision 2020-02-17, https://news.cision.com/city-of-helsinki/r/zero-pedestrian-and-cyclist-fatalities-in-helsinki-in-2019,c3036993 — quoting Deputy Mayor Anni Sinnemäki attributing the result to street-environment improvements, increased traffic control, vehicle-safety technology, better rescue services, and consistently lowered speed limits since the 1970s (30 km/h in most residential/central areas, 40 km/h on major thoroughfares, 50 km/h in suburbs); the release also notes the annual average fell to seven deaths/year in the 2010s, down from as many as 84 in the worst year on record (1965)]. New York City's Vision Zero program (adopted 2014, the first major North American Vision Zero adoption predating Toronto's by two years) reported record-low annual traffic fatalities in a recent year per its own Department of Transportation announcement, cited in this project's pre-mined seed-sources CSV as a named comparator [this library's government-document registry, "Traffic Deaths Reach All-time Low," NYC DOT, 2026] — not independently fetched in this review, flagged as such.

3. What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. Oslo's and Helsinki's results were achieved through a specific, nameable combination — segregated cycling infrastructure investment plus reduced central-area car access — rather than through enforcement technology; this is directly relevant given Bill 56's stated intent to substitute physical traffic-calming for camera enforcement [NEW-2]: Oslo's own case is evidence that physical infrastructure and access restriction, done at sufficient scale, can independently drive vulnerable-road-user fatalities to zero without primary reliance on automated enforcement, addressing the same gap Toronto's arterial-road concentration finding [CL-0748] identifies, though neither Oslo's nor Helsinki's specific engineering standards were catalogued in enough detail in this review to state a specific transferable design element beyond the general "segregate + restrict access" pattern.

Cui Bono — who profits from this problem persisting
