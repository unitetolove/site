Indigenous Services and Learning Traditions

Hard selection rule for this guide: an entry belongs here only if it is an Indigenous-specific service, program or space — Indigenous-led, Indigenous-serving, or a learning opportunity in Indigenous knowledge and traditions — available in Toronto and free. A mainstream service that happens to serve Indigenous people among everyone else does not belong here; it gets a pointer at most, not a full entry. That rule cut a real number of near-misses.

This guide has two distinct readers, and they are kept visibly apart on purpose:

  • (a) An Indigenous person in Toronto — First Nations, Inuit or Métis — looking for health, housing, legal, family, employment, education and cultural services from their own community's organisations. This is the guide's primary reader, and everything from "If you're fleeing violence" through "Benefit facts that change if you're Indigenous" below is written for them.
  • (b) Anyone in Toronto who wants to learn — public tours, cultural education, First Story Toronto, the Indigenous Canada course, and the language classes that are stated open to everyone. This is the "Learning Traditions" half, and it starts at the heading of that name below. If you are a non-Indigenous reader who came here to learn, start there — the services above it are not addressed to you, and browsing them as if they were is exactly the kind of blurring this guide exists to prevent.

The single most important thing in this guide is never blurring open-to-all with community-specific, in either direction. Where a service is for First Nations, Inuit, Métis or urban Indigenous people specifically, this guide says so exactly as the provider's own row states it — including, where the row says, whether you need to be a registered/status Indigenous person or whether self-identifying is enough. Most rows are silent on that specific question, and this guide does not guess when they are. Where a service run by an Indigenous organisation is explicitly open to everyone, this guide says that too, in those words — a Toronto shelter was once wrongly labelled Indigenous-only in an earlier wave of this project when its own page said otherwise, and under-inviting someone in danger is a harm, not a safe default. Pan-Indigenous and nation-specific services are different things and are not merged here — this wave adds Toronto's first Inuit-specific row, and it is kept distinct from the First-Nations/Métis-facing organizations around it rather than folded into general "Indigenous services" language.

FT-9212, FT-9619 and FT-9716 are excluded from this guide. None of the three is an Indigenous-specific row (Somali settlement services, Deaf/hard-of- hearing services, and a general meal program, respectively), so none was a candidate for this guide in the first place.

If you're fleeing violence or unsafe at home

Anduhyaun Inc. — Emergency Shelter & Nekenaan Second Stage Housing Emergency shelter and second-stage transitional housing for women and their children fleeing violence, with crisis intervention and sexual assault counselling, culturally-rooted programming, and safe spaces running since 1973. The provider's own Emergency Shelter page states this plainly: "Anduhyaun Shelter is a Violence against Women (VAW) Emergency Shelter servicing Indigenous and Non-Indigenous women with or without children fleeing violence." This is an Indigenous-led, culturally-specific shelter — not a closed door to non-Indigenous women. anduhyaun.org Shelter: 416-920-1492 ext 221; Nekenaan Second Stage Housing: 416-243-7669 ext 226. 1296 Weston Rd. No documents required. Eligibility beyond the open-to-all statement above is not further detailed on the row.

If you need a crisis line right now: the Assaulted Women's Helpline (GTA 416-863-0511, toll-free 1-866-863-0511, 24/7, up to 154 languages via interpreter) is a general-population crisis and shelter-intake line, not Indigenous-specific, but it explicitly refers Indigenous women to Talk 4 Healing as a dedicated onward resource — the row does not give Talk 4 Healing's own contact details, so ask AWHL for the referral. Full entry in Guide 01 In Crisis Now.

9-8-8, the national Suicide Crisis Helpline (call or text 988, 24/7/365, free), gives callers a First Nations/Inuit/Métis option that connects to Hope for Wellness — the row does not give Hope for Wellness's own number either, so use the 988 routing option to reach it. Full entry in Guide 01 In Crisis Now.

If you need a bed tonight, or housing that lasts

Na-Me-Res (Native Men's Residence) 71-bed emergency shelter, transitional housing program, and long-term independent housing initiative for homeless Indigenous men, with cultural/spiritual supports, case management, and street outreach. Na-Me-Res's own Mission page states its purpose in full: "to provide outreach services and permanent housing to Indigenous men experiencing homelessness in Toronto while providing outreach and support services to the broader population." Read plainly, that is two different access bases in one mission statement: the residence/housing itself is for Indigenous men specifically, while outreach and support services are explicitly extended beyond that population too — a distinction the row's wording alone did not carry before this review. nameres.org Call the toll-free line, 1-866-626-3737 — the only number published on Na-Me-Res's own current Contact page. A second number, 416-652-0334, reaches the Emergency Shelter directly at 14 Vaughan Rd per an independently-maintained directory, but could not be confirmed on the provider's own site — lead with the toll-free number. No documents required. The 71-bed count and Sagatay/ life-skills program details were not reverified this review — carried forward from an earlier fetch lane.

Na-Me-Res — Programs Beyond Shelter Beyond the shelter itself, Na-Me-Res runs: Street Outreach & Support (help finding an ID Clinic for a SIN/birth certificate/OHIP card, doctor connections, food bank and government-form help); a year-long Housing Aftercare Program; intensive case management for mental health/addiction; Native Inmate Liaison Officers (NILO) supporting incarcerated Indigenous people's access to sweat lodge ceremonies, healing circles, smudging and drumming; a life-skills program with a Medicine Wheel Garden and Cree language classes; and the Espaniel/Menzies Education Fund ($500 bursaries). nameres.org/programs Most of this is for existing Na-Me-Res clients, not a general walk-in door — access is through Na-Me-Res's own Outreach team. Two programs name a specific bar: the life-skills program requires residing at Sagatay, and the bursary requires 6+ months as a Na-Me-Res program participant. Community-specific, same population as the shelter above.

The provider's own page states this is open to Indigenous and non-Indigenous applicants. A related free Aboriginal Housing Support Centre (AHSC) helps any Aboriginal family or individual with the housing-application process, whether or not they end up housed by Wigwamen. wigwamen.com Apply via the Housing Locations page. Subsidized (geared-to-income), income-tested. The 865+ units/10+ buildings figure was not reverified this review — carried forward from an earlier fetch lane.

Gabriel Dumont Non-Profit Homes (Metro Toronto) Inc. An 87-unit apartment and townhouse housing complex for the Indigenous community in southeast Scarborough, established 1985 — the provider's own homepage says it in those words ("an 87 unit housing complex for the Indigenous community. Located in South East Scarborough and established in 1985"). Beyond the housing itself, the provider's Services page describes referrals and resource options for its community, with Indigenous-agency partnerships for on-site workshops and ceremonies. Named for the Métis leader, but not a Métis-only door: the provider's own Rental Application Process page names all three peoples — an applicant "wanting to be considered for rent-geared-to-income assistance must submit" either "a) Status card (First Nations, Metis, Inuit) OR b) Sworn affidavit" — so the rent-geared-to-income stream is Indigenous-specific, and a sworn affidavit is accepted where there is no status card. gabrieldumont.org 4201 Kingston Rd, Unit 114A, Scarborough; 416-286-5277. Food

There is no single Indigenous-specific, no-questions-asked, any-day food bank in this atlas — what exists is folded into the community hubs below, each with a real access gate the guide states plainly rather than implying an open door:

  • Toronto Council Fire's food bank is reservation-only (Thursdays biweekly, call ahead) — see the full entry under "Community, culture and everyday support" below.
  • Native Youth Resource Centre serves three meals a day, but only to Indigenous youth 16–24 — see the full entry under "Family and children" below.
  • Native Women's Resource Centre of Toronto's drop-in lunch serves Indigenous women and their children, per third-party directories (not the provider's own current site) — see the full entry under "Family and children" below.
  • Tungasuvvingat Inuit's Toronto office runs a weekly food hamper program, Mondays 12–3pm, as part of its Inuit-specific programming — see the full entry under "Community, culture and everyday support" below.

For a food bank open to anyone regardless of Indigenous identity, see Guide 03 Food Today and Groceries.

Tungasuvvingat Inuit (TI) — Toronto Office Toronto's first and only dedicated Inuit-specific service organization documented in this atlas: cultural workshops, community gatherings, health and social-systems navigation, and food-security programming, including a weekly food hamper program (Mondays, 12–3pm). TI describes itself elsewhere on its own site as "the only Inuit-specific service organization of its kind in urban Canada." Ontario Federation of Indigenous Friendship Centres (OFIFC) The umbrella body for Ontario's urban Indigenous Friendship Centres — a find-a-Friendship-Centre directory plus province-wide programs in Children & Youth, Education & Employment, Health & Wellness, Homelessness & Housing, Justice, and Ending Violence. Kapapamahchakwew - Wandering Spirit School (TDSB) A TDSB public school — free, like any public school — founded in 1977 by Pauline Shirt and Vern Harper, who, TDSB's own school page recounts, "were looking for a safer school for their own child," searched for one "that was culturally safe and nurtured their son's Indigenous identity," and founded their own when none could be found: Wandering Spirit Survival School. Enrolment runs through TDSB registration.

Health and healing

Anishnawbe Health Toronto (AHT) The only facility in Toronto combining Western and traditional Indigenous approaches to health care (est. 1984): free primary care, chiropody, physiotherapy, mental-health/psychiatric services, child and family therapy, substance-use supports, and traditional Indigenous healing (ceremony, medicines, ties to Elders/healers) for Indigenous clients across Toronto. aht.ca 425 Cherry Street; 416-360-0486. Access-model flag: Emergency Counselling Services run Wed and Fri only, 10am–12pm and 1–3pm — not a standing walk-in; scheduled appointments outside those windows are bookable via reception. Community-specific — for Indigenous clients; the row does not state whether documents or status proof are required.

Call Auntie Clinic Free, Indigenous-led interprofessional healthcare (family doctors, nurses, midwives, pediatricians, psychiatrists, psychotherapists, counsellors, community health workers), run under Seventh Generation Midwives Toronto — grew out of a COVID-19 Indigenous Pathways Hotline. Weekly clinic plus a drop-in for lunch, visiting, and booking future appointments, with a harm-reduction/holistic approach. Explicitly serves "Indigenous Community members and people who are experiencing barriers to accessing mainstream healthcare" — Indigenous-led and Indigenous-focused, but its own stated eligibility is broader than Indigenous-only. callauntieclinic.com 525 Dundas St E, 2nd Floor; book via 437-703-8703. Open Wednesdays 1–4pm.

Auduzhe Mino Nesewinong (Place of Healthy Breathing) Free/OHIP-billed, culturally-safe primary healthcare for First Nations, Inuit and Métis community members in Toronto, blending Western medicine and traditional healing: illness diagnosis and management, cancer screening (including Pap smears), mental health support, bloodwork, sexual health care, vaccines, smoking cessation, traditional medicines, care referrals, home visits, and outreach clinics. Grew out of a collaboration between Na-Me-Res, Seventh Generation Midwives Toronto, Well Living House, the Centre for Wise Practices in Indigenous Health, and Inner City Health Associates. auduzhe.org Appointment-based with walk-ins welcomed. No phone number found on the row — use the website. Community-specific — for First Nations, Inuit and Métis community members.

Seventh Generation Midwives Toronto (SGMT) Full-scope midwifery care through pregnancy, labour, birth and 6 weeks postpartum, with privileges at Sunnybrook and Toronto Birth Centre. Incorporates traditional teachings and ceremonies for Aboriginal families who choose them — the provider's own page describes this as open to the broader downtown Toronto community, not restricted to Indigenous families. sgmt.ca 525 Dundas St E, 2nd Floor; 416-530-7468. Referral-required/phone. Catchment is south of the 401, east of Jane St, west of Galloway Rd, with flexibility for Indigenous clients outside that area. Midwifery care in Ontario is OHIP-covered; the provider's own page does not explicitly say "free" — this is a standard inference from Ontario's OHIP-funded midwifery model, not a page-quoted fact.

Toronto Birth Centre A midwife-led birth centre providing culturally-integrated, Aboriginal-midwifery-informed birthing care. The provider frames itself as a culturally safer space for Indigenous and other communities affected by unjust systems, not exclusively Indigenous-restricted. torontobirthcentre.ca 525 Dundas Street East; 416-366-8080. Referral-required/phone. Midwifery care in Ontario is OHIP-covered; "free" here is the same OHIP-model inference as above, not an explicit page statement.

2-Spirited People of the 1st Nations — Health, Family & Cultural Support Services Free primary care, family support, and counselling for all ages; comprehensive HIV/STBBI education, testing (including HIV self-testing since 2021) and treatment-referral support through the Posi+ive Living program; an Indigenous-led crisis-response/wellness-check partnership ("Kamaamwizme wii Naagidiwendiiying," delivered with ENAGB and Parkdale Queen West Community Health Centre) operating 24/7 within Toronto Police Division 14's boundaries as part of the citywide Toronto Community Crisis Service; and Indigenous cultural programming — sweat lodge, cultural teachings, traditional arts and crafting. A separate 2-Spirit Bursary Program runs annually (self-determined, low-barrier bursaries; the 2024 cycle listed 16 named bursaries totalling $17,000, open generally to the 2-Spirit community, including friends/family of staff, though staff themselves are not eligible — one of the named bursaries is specifically for 2-Spirit seniors). Serves Two-Spirit and Indigenous LGBTQ+ people and the broader 2SLGBTQ+ community — Indigenous-led, not Indigenous-only. 2spirits.org Call 416-944-9300 or email info@2spirits.org. The row states no status-card or band-registration requirement, so this reads as open by self-identification, not gated to registered status. Correction 2026-07-31 (harm reduction, employment/housing/legal help, and Elders support are programme-gated, not community-gated): the org's own current "Services we offer" page lists a shorter general list than this guide's earlier text stated — primary care, family support, counselling for all ages, cultural programs, and the crisis-response partnership. It does not list harm reduction, help finding employment/housing/legal services, or Elders support as general services. Those specific supports are described, on the org's own separate Posi+ive Living Programs page, as delivered through that HIV/AIDS-focused program — whose stated population is community members "at risk of or living with HIV, hepatitis C, and related co-infections," plus (per the org's Mission page) those "facing the effects of historic and ongoing colonial violence." This is a programme gate, not a community gate: the organisation itself remains Indigenous-led and open by self-identification to the Two-Spirit and broader 2SLGBTQ+ community for the general services listed above — it is specifically harm reduction, employment/housing/legal help, and Elders support that require enrollment in Posi+ive Living, not Two-Spirit or Indigenous identity itself. Call to ask whether your specific need qualifies rather than assuming an unconditional door for those four supports — the primary care, family support, counselling, cultural programming, crisis-response, and bursary facts above remain general and are not HIV-gated per the org's own current pages. Office location, reconfirmed 2026-07-31: the org's own Office Locations page and homepage contact listing currently show 105–145 Front St E, Suite 105 as "open to public," alongside a second location, 2126 Danforth Ave, with no flooding-closure notice found on any current page fetched. The downtown office's prior closure (previously stated here as dated to a May 2026 site notice) could not be independently reconfirmed — call ahead before travelling rather than treating either version as certain; 2126 Danforth Ave remains a confirmed second public location either way. This organisation is dual-homed in full here and in Guide 21 LGBTQ2S+ — a Two-Spirit reader may arrive from either guide and should find the same real entry, not a bounce to the other guide. Both copies now agree in substance.

Legal help

Aboriginal Legal Services Free community legal clinic for Indigenous people in the GTA (formerly Aboriginal Legal Services of Toronto, established 1990): courtworkers, Gladue report writing, referral to lawyers, victim rights advocacy, a Community Council restorative-justice program, and landlord/tenant, social assistance, victim-rights and child-protection representation. aboriginallegal.ca 211 Yonge St, Suite 500; 416-408-3967 or 416-408-4041, toll-free 1-844-633-2886. Community Legal Clinic intake Mon/Wed/Fri 10am–3pm, other times by appointment. No documents required.

Also see: Na-Me-Res's Native Inmate Liaison Officers, who support incarcerated Indigenous people's access to sweat lodge ceremonies, healing circles, smudging and drumming, are described under "If you need a bed tonight, or housing that lasts" above.

One fact worth knowing about Legal Aid Ontario's general Legal Aid Certificate Program (full entry belongs to a future general Legal Aid guide, not repeated here): payments from Residential Schools, Sixties Scoop, Indian Day Schools, First Nations water/housing/child-and- family-services settlements and similar Indigenous reconciliation funds are explicitly excluded from the financial-eligibility test used to decide who qualifies for a certificate. legalaid.on.ca

Employment and training

Miziwe Biik Aboriginal Employment and Training Free 1-on-1 Employment Advisors, academic upgrading, post-secondary/apprenticeship funding for books/supplies/transportation, targeted wage subsidies for employers, self-employment grants, and skilled-trades training (concrete formwork, painting, electrical pre-apprenticeship) for unemployed/underemployed Indigenous people in the GTA. miziwebiik.com 433 Cherry St. Register online at form.jotform.com/MiziweBiik/join — self-registration, not a third-party referral. Client status expires annually (April 1). No documents required.

Post-Secondary Student Support Program (PSSSP) Non-repayable federal funding for First Nations students registered under the Indian Act pursuing post-secondary education: tuition, books, travel support and living allowances, up to $53,000/yr (up to $90,000/yr in extraordinary cases for advanced/professional/graduate programs). This one states its own eligibility test plainly: status registration under the Indian Act, not self-identification. Inuit students apply through a separate program, the Inuit Post-Secondary Education Strategy, which does not have its own row in this atlas. Post-Secondary Student Support Program — Government of Canada Not a direct-apply portal — administered through your own First Nation or a First Nations- designated organization; contact your band office or the relevant Indigenous Services Canada regional office. Funding is limited; not all eligible students are funded, and applications are valid for one school year only.

Benefit facts that change if you're Indigenous

These live in guides built around the benefit itself, not here — but each one changes specifically for Indigenous applicants, and a reader of this guide should know the fact even if the full entry is elsewhere:

  • Ontario Electricity Support Program pays a higher monthly credit tier — $52–$113/mo instead of the standard $45–$75/mo — for Indigenous households, electric-heat households, or households using electricity-intensive medical devices. Full entry: Guide 08 Utilities and Bills.
  • Healthy Smiles Ontario (free dental care for kids/youth) auto-enrolls a child with First Nations status with no income test, the same carve-out given to families already on OW/ODSP. Full entry: Guide 10 Health Extras.

  • The Art Gallery of Ontario gives free general admission to Indigenous Peoples, on top of its separate free-under-25 and free-under-9 policies. Full entry: Guide 06 Free Fun.

  • Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit applications give priority to, among other groups, Indigenous persons. Full entry: Guide 07 Housing.

  • The Legal Aid Ontario certificate carve-out for reconciliation-settlement payments is repeated in full under "Legal help" above.

PART B — Learning Traditions: for anyone in Toronto who wants to learn

If you arrived at this guide as a non-Indigenous reader wanting to learn about Indigenous cultures, traditions or languages in Toronto, this is your section. Everything below is stated by its own provider as open to the public — tours, courses, streaming, festivals, and the specific language sessions that say so. Nothing above this heading is addressed to you; it is written for Indigenous readers looking for their own community's services, and treating it as a browsable list is the exact blurring this guide is built to avoid.

Public tours and teaching lodges

The provider's own page states sessions "are open to both Indigenous and non-Indigenous peoples" and "welcomes participants from all nations, ancestry and backgrounds." ncct.on.ca — Dodem Kanonhsa' 655 Bay St, 3rd Floor; 416-952-9272. Some sessions require registration due to capacity. NCCT Free Public Tour A free guided tour (individuals, small groups up to 10, or large groups up to 50) of the Native Canadian Centre of Toronto exploring the city's Indigenous cultural roots, the largest collection of Canadian Indigenous artwork in the city, key phrases from local Indigenous languages, and NCCT's 60+ year history. The page states the Centre is "always open to everyone." Language classes

Native Canadian Centre of Toronto — Anishinaabemowin Language Classes This atlas's only row documenting an active Indigenous-language class in Toronto — and its access basis is not one thing. NCCT's own program page describes three weekly sessions, each stated separately:

  • Monday, 12:30–1:30pm — virtual "Open Lunch & Learn." Stated on NCCT's own page as "open to everyone" — a casual virtual space to learn and practice Anishinaabemowin during lunch.
  • Thursday, 6:00–8:00pm — in-person Beginner Class, held in NCCT's Talking Room and paired with a social night (big drum, community connection). Also stated as "open to everyone."
  • Tuesday — Early Intermediate Class. Stated on NCCT's own page as "a closed program for Indigenous community members only," including cultural discussions connected to spiritual practices and beliefs. This session is not for a non-Indigenous reader of this section — it is listed here only so the class's full schedule is accurate, not as an invitation. (Its Part A mention is under "Community, culture and everyday support," above.)

Do not compress this into one access statement — two of three sessions are open to everyone; one is not. ncct.on.ca To join: Lena Recollet, 416-964-9087 ext. 1003, lena.recollet@ncct.on.ca. Courses, apps and streaming

Indigenous Canada (University of Alberta, via Coursera) A free-to-audit 12-module online course from the University of Alberta's Faculty of Native Studies covering Indigenous worldviews, the fur trade, treaties, the Indian Act and residential schools, urbanization, self-determination, land and title, gender, and contemporary Indigenous art. Free "audit" access to all lectures, readings and quizzes; only the paid certificate costs money. Open to anyone, anywhere. coursera.org/learn/indigenous-canada

First Story Toronto A free smartphone app (iOS/Android) mapping the city's Indigenous history through archival photos, documents, audio and video, housed within NCCT since 1995. Built explicitly for the general public. Honesty flag: the app itself is reliably available, but this project's search found no confirmation of currently scheduled public walking tours — the most recent tour-specific posts are from 2013–2017, with one further, non-tour post (a May 2018 note about accessing First Story content via the Driftscape app) — reconfirmed on a fresh fetch this review (2026-07-31), which still shows no tour listing more recent than 2017. Contact NCCT to ask before expecting a live tour calendar. First Story Toronto

Toronto Public Library — Indigenous Initiatives Free Indigenous-focused book/DVD/CD/language-kit collections (the Native Peoples Collection), curated reading lists, and free public programs across TPL branches — bead art workshops, Indigenous medicine workshops, documentary screenings, hand drumming and singing, youth beading circles. The page states explicitly: "Both Indigenous and non-Indigenous peoples are invited to explore." tpl.ca — Indigenous Initiatives Most programs don't require a TPL card to attend; borrowing collection items does.

NFB Indigenous Cinema Free streaming of 200+ films by Indigenous directors from the National Film Board's Indigenous Cinema collection, searchable by director, subject or Indigenous nation. No account required to watch. nfb.ca/indigenous-cinema

Honest gaps

Beyond language classes, no Toronto Indigenous organization's own page states that its core services can be received in an Indigenous language. This wave's research lane checked Anishnawbe Health Toronto, Toronto Council Fire, and Native Child and Family Services of Toronto's own current pages specifically for this. None make that claim. Anishnawbe Health's homepage uses the Ojibwe greeting "Boozhoo," and NCCT's homepage uses "Aanii, Tansi, She:kon, Bindigen" — but a greeting is not a statement that a client can conduct an appointment, counselling session or intake in that language. Say this plainly: language access, beyond the classes above, is not something Toronto's major Indigenous organizations currently state exists on their own pages.

Leads that could not be verified — named here as unconfirmed, not listed as entries: - TDSB "Language Nests" — a WebSearch snippet described weekly multigenerational Ojibwe sessions at the Urban Indigenous Education Centre and Eastview PS, but TDSB's own current live pages (Home, Programs, Resources, and the specific Indigenous-Languages URL, which now redirects to the UIEC homepage) do not list this program. A Google-Doc-hosted resource linked from TDSB's Resources page could not be opened. Historical or unconfirmed, not current. - A Native Women's Resource Centre of Toronto "Language Classes" event page — referenced in an old search snippet, but NWRCT's own core Programs and Services page (9 named programs, reconfirmed this review) does not list one, and the specific event URL would not load. - Toronto Indigenous Harm Reduction — a real, community-documented organization, but its site could not be read by this project's fetch tooling after repeated attempts; per standing rule, an unfetchable provider page cannot become a published row, however real the org may be.

No Inuit-specific housing provider. Tungasuvvingat Inuit's Toronto office (above) is a community-support and cultural hub with food-security programming, not a housing program — this remains an open gap.

No Inuit Child First Initiative row — the Inuit-specific parallel to Jordan's Principle (above), which is First-Nations-specific per its own row.

No dedicated status-card or Indian-registration help clinic.
