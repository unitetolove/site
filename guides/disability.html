Disability

Hard selection rule for this guide: an entry belongs here only if a disabled person in Toronto can use it because of their disability — a program, benefit, service or accommodation gated to or designed for disability — and it is free or explicitly labelled subsidized. A mainstream service that is merely wheelchair-accessible does not qualify; accessibility is a baseline, not a service. That rule cut a real number of near-misses — general employment agencies with one disability stream among many, a seniors' friendly-visiting program that also admits disabled adults, a general walk-in counselling clinic, an addiction-recovery job program.

"Disability" is not one reader, and this guide does not pretend it is. A blind reader, a Deaf reader, a person with a developmental disability, someone with a mental-health disability, someone with a mobility impairment, and someone with an episodic or invisible illness share a legal category and almost no doors. Below, find the section written for you and start there — the sections are organized by what kind of disability you have, not by what kind of program exists, because that is how you already think about your own situation. Three sections are the exception, and they come first: money, getting around, and getting the equipment you need are each gated by proof of disability or functional need, not by kind of disability — a Disability Tax Credit approval, a Wheel-Trans application, and an Assistive Devices Program stream don't ask whether your disability is physical, sensory, cognitive or psychiatric, so any reader may need any of those three sections regardless of which by-kind-of-disability section is theirs further down.

FT-9212, FT-9619 and FT-9716 are excluded from this guide. FT-9619 (Bob Rumball Canadian Centre of Excellence for the Deaf) is the one most likely to be reached for while building this guide's Deaf/hard-of-hearing section — it is not cited anywhere below, on purpose, and no detail from its row appears in this guide. FT-9212 (Somali settlement services) and FT-9716 (a general meal program) were never disability-specific rows and were not candidates here in the first place. This lane re-confirmed the same three ids remain excluded and did not reach for FT-9619 while sealing this guide's Deaf/hard-of-hearing-adjacent equipment rows.

Money: income and benefits gated by proof of disability

This is the one place in this guide organized by gate, not by kind of disability — every program below is unlocked the same way, by documented proof (usually a Disability Tax Credit approval or an ODSP Disability Determination Package), regardless of whether your disability is visible, episodic, physical, sensory, developmental or psychiatric. If you only read one section of this guide, and money is what you need most right now, read this one.

Ontario Disability Support Program (ODSP) income support Monthly financial assistance for basic needs and shelter, plus health and disability benefits (drugs, dental, vision) — single max shown on the province's own page is $1,408/month (a 1.9%, July 1 2026 rate increase is announced on the same page but not yet reflected in that figure; third-party trackers report the post-increase amount as roughly $1,436/month — this guide states both rather than guessing which is current). The Canada Disability Benefit below does not count as income against ODSP. ontario.ca — Ontario Disability Support Program Citizens, permanent residents, and in-land refugee claimants with an official claim from the date of the claim are eligible; visitors are not, unless they've made a refugee claim or applied for permanent residence. Requires ID, proof of income, proof of address, and a medical Disability Determination Package completed by a health-care professional — this last document is the actual proof-of-disability gate behind every benefit in this section. Reconfirmed 2026-07-31, directly against ontario.ca's own page: the $1,408 figure, the "not yet reflected" rate-increase caveat, and the CDB/ODSP no-interaction rule ("If you receive the federal government's Canada Disability Benefit, it will not affect your eligibility for ODSP or the amount of your payment") all still hold, word for word. Contact method, added this review because this guide's own rule asks for one: apply online, by phone (toll-free 1-888-999-1142), or by toll-free TTY (1-800-387-5559) — a non-voice option is already built into ODSP's own application process, unlike several entries flagged below.

The extra ODSP supplements, dental coverage, and post-ODSP health benefit are already fully written up in Guide 02 Claim Everything — this guide won't repeat them, only name what exists so you know to look: the Mandatory Special Necessities Benefit (medical transport and supplies), the Pregnancy and Breastfeeding Nutritional Allowance, the Special Diet Allowance, the Guide Dog Benefit, dental coverage (an ODSP statement or government ID to a participating dentist is enough — no referral), and the Extended/Transitional Health Benefit that lets you keep drug/dental/vision coverage for a while after you leave ODSP for higher income. Full entries: Guide 02 Claim Everything.

Canada Disability Benefit — a federal monthly payment, up to $204.20/month (July 2026–June 2027 period), for low-income persons with disabilities aged 18–64 who hold Disability Tax Credit approval; reduced by 20 cents for every dollar of adjusted family net income above $23,000 (single) or above $32,500 (couple, if only one partner is CDB-eligible) — or by 10 cents per dollar, per partner, above $32,500 if both partners are eligible. This is a gradual reduction, not a cliff — a dollar over the threshold does not zero out the payment. Corrected: this guide previously implied the benefit falls straight to $0 immediately above the threshold; it does not. Canada.ca's own worked calculation examples are still dated to the prior July 2025–June 2026 benefit year (not yet updated for the current period), so this guide does not state a specific current-year dollar figure for exactly where the benefit reaches zero, rather than guess at a number the government hasn't itself published for July 2026–June 2027 yet. A one-time $150 payment starting September 2026 helps offset the cost of getting DTC-approved in the first place; per canada.ca's own page, you do not need to apply separately for it. Full entry: Guide 02 Claim Everything.

Assistance for Children with Severe Disabilities (ACSD) — $25–$678/month to a parent or guardian of a child under 18 with a severe disability, and it also unlocks Assistive Devices Program coverage, OHIP+ drugs, Healthy Smiles dental, and hearing/vision help for that child. Full entry: Guide 02 Claim Everything.

Canada Disability Savings Grant and Bond (RDSP) — if you're Disability Tax Credit-approved and open a Registered Disability Savings Plan, the government matches your own contributions up to $3,500/year (lifetime $70,000, the Grant), and — if your income is low or modest — deposits money into the plan with no contribution required at all, up to $1,000/year (lifetime $20,000, the Bond). Up to 10 years of missed grants/bonds can be caught up. Full entry: Guide 02 Claim Everything.

The Disability Tax Credit (DTC) — the approval quietly required by two programs above, and a door this guide can now open further than before. The Canada Disability Benefit and the RDSP Grant/Bond above both require DTC approval before a cent flows — and separately, the Assistive Devices Program streams later in this guide give a full coverage boost to people already receiving OW, ODSP, or ACSD, all of which sit downstream of the same kind of proof-of-disability paperwork. A reader who doesn't know DTC approval is the gate behind the Canada Disability Benefit and the RDSP is standing in front of a toll booth they cannot see.

Getting around: Wheel-Trans

This is the single most consequential mobility-disability-specific program in the city, and as of this wave it finally has a row. It lives here, up front, rather than inside the physical/mobility section below, for the same reason Money does: eligibility turns on functional ability to use transit, not on which kind of disability you have — physical, sensory, cognitive and mental-health disabilities can all qualify.

Wheel-Trans (TTC) TTC's specialized shared-ride, door-to-door transit service for people whose disability prevents them from using conventional transit for all or part of a trip; also books trips to a subway/bus stop (Family of Services) so a rider can complete part of a journey on conventional transit. Service area: City of Toronto plus up to 1 km into adjacent Peel, York and Durham regions, 24/7, using regular buses and contracted accessible taxis. ttc.ca — Wheel-Trans Application Open to anyone whose disability — physical, sensory, cognitive or mental health, under the Ontario Human Rights Code — prevents or limits conventional-transit use; no age floor stated. Toronto plus up to 1km into Peel, York and Durham.

Eligibility is not all-or-nothing, and this is the most commonly misunderstood thing about Wheel-Trans — do not read the paragraphs below and conclude you don't qualify because you can do some things on your own. Applicants sort into one of three categories, in TTC's own words: Unconditional (your disability prevents conventional-transit use at all times), Conditional (environmental or physical barriers limit your use in some circumstances only — bad weather, a non-accessible destination — so you use conventional transit for part of a trip and Wheel-Trans for the rest, the Family of Services model), and Temporary (a time-limited disability). A Conditional rider is a full Wheel-Trans user, not a partial or lesser one — the category exists specifically so that partial ability doesn't disqualify you.

How you apply: a written mobility questionnaire (you or a representative describe your everyday mobility and transit ability), plus certification from a regulated health-care professional (physician, psychiatrist, physiotherapist, optometrist, audiologist, psychologist, chiropractor, occupational therapist, speech-language pathologist, or registered nurse/MSW). Where TTC needs more detail, a functional mobility assessment is done in person by a TTC-contracted occupational therapist — it tests real-world transit-relevant ability (walking distance, stairs, weather tolerance, wayfinding, sensory/cognitive demands), not your diagnosis on paper. If you disagree with the decision, you can request an independent appeal.

The fare is the ordinary TTC fare — there is no separate, higher Wheel-Trans surcharge. PRESTO, cash, debit or credit are all accepted, and a support person travels free with a valid Support Person Assistance Card.

Reconfirmed directly, from two different TTC pages (not a repeat of the application page above): TTC's own Eligibility Categories page states, in these exact words, "Eligibility is not based on disability type, income or age. It is based solely on the presence of a disability that impacts a person's ability to travel on conventional transit" — independent confirmation, from TTC itself, that a Conditional rider is not a lesser category by TTC's own design, not just by this guide's reading of it. TTC's own General Information page confirms the fare claim just as directly: "Service is provided anywhere within the City of Toronto under the regular TTC fare structure." Service area (Toronto plus up to 1km into Peel, York and Durham) and 24/7 hours were also reconfirmed word for word on the General Information page. ttc.ca — Eligibility Categories · ttc.ca — General Information

Also see: Toronto Ride, in the "If you have a physical or mobility disability" section below, is specifically for adults with disabilities who are not eligible for Wheel-Trans.

Getting the equipment you need: the Assistive Devices Program and home/vehicle modification

Only one Assistive Devices Program stream had a row in this atlas before this wave — hearing devices (FT-18202, in the Deaf/hard-of-hearing section below). The much larger set of streams that fund wheelchairs, communication devices, visual aids, prosthetics, orthotics, respiratory equipment and diabetes supplies now has rows too. They sit here, together, up front, for the same reason Wheel-Trans does: ADP eligibility is gated by the specific device you need, not by which kind of disability you have, and grouping the streams together lets you compare the funding share across all of them in one place — which matters, because the shares are not all the same.

ADP funding share, per stream, exactly as published — do not generalise "ADP covers 75%," because it is wrong for some of the streams that matter most: - Mobility aids, communication aids, visual aids, most orthotic/prosthetic/respiratory items: 75% of ADP's set price, rising to 100% if you're on Ontario Works, ODSP, or ACSD. - Diabetes equipment is different: 100% (not 75%) for an insulin pump and for a real-time continuous glucose monitoring system, regardless of OW/ODSP/ACSD status. - Breast prosthetics are a flat grant, not a percentage — reimbursed after you pay the full cost upfront, not split at point of sale like most other streams. - Several streams carry their own dollar caps (noted per entry below) even within the percentage model. No stream has an income test — the 100% boost is specifically tied to OW/ODSP/ACSD status, not to income generally, so don't rule ADP out on cost before applying.

Every share and cap below was re-confirmed directly against ontario.ca's own page for that specific stream on 2026-07-31 — all eight ADP streams cited in this guide, including hearing devices, were fetched, and every figure held exactly as published, with one exception (the respiratory supply grants, corrected below). ADP does not publish a single "program year" the way ODSP or the Canada Disability Benefit do, so instead each entry below now states the date ontario.ca itself last updated that stream's page — the closest available freshness signal a reader has for judging how current a figure is.

Assistive Devices Program (ADP) — Mobility Aids Ontario government funding toward manual and power wheelchairs, power scooters, positioning devices (cushions, tilt/recline supports), forearm crutches, and adult and pediatric walkers/standers/ strollers. Excludes all-terrain vehicles, non-wheeled walkers, transport chairs, ramps/lifts and standard canes. ontario.ca — Mobility Aids Physical disability requiring the aid for 6+ months, Ontario. Assessment by an ADP-registered occupational therapist or physiotherapist, then a registered vendor helps finish the application — must be done before purchase; ADP does not reimburse retroactively. Funding: 75% of ADP's set price, 100% if on OW/ODSP/ACSD. (ontario.ca updated July 10, 2025; reconfirmed unchanged 2026-07-31.)

Assistive Devices Program (ADP) — Communication Aids Ontario government funding toward voice prosthetics, voice amplifiers, electrolarynges, communication display boards, writing aids, quick-messaging devices, and speech-generating devices, for people unable to speak clearly or write by hand due to physical disability. ontario.ca — Communication Aids Physical disability affecting speech or writing, Ontario. First application needs a family doctor/nurse-practitioner referral to an ADP-registered speech-language pathologist or occupational therapist. Funding: 75%, with set maximums for some items ($3,993.75/3yr for voice restoration/speaking valves, $990/3yr for stoma filters/supplies); 100% if on OW/ODSP/ACSD. (ontario.ca updated February 18, 2026; reconfirmed unchanged 2026-07-31.)

Assistive Devices Program (ADP) — Visual Aids Ontario government funding toward specialized glasses/magnifiers and other optical aids, audio players for reading, Perkins/manual braillers, CCTV and computer-based reading/writing systems, and white canes, for people with long-term low vision or blindness (roughly 20/70 or less, corrected, in the better eye) that surgery, glasses or contacts can't fix. ontario.ca — Visual Aids Long-term low vision or blindness, Ontario. Assessment by an ADP-registered ophthalmologist, optometrist, vision rehabilitation worker, specialist teacher of the blind, or orientation-and- mobility instructor, depending on the aid. Funding: 75% for most aids, capped at a set dollar amount for optical aids (your share can exceed the usual 25%); up to 100% if on OW/ODSP/ACSD. Excludes regular eyeglasses, braille watches, GPS units, talking clocks/calculators and scleral lenses. (ontario.ca updated February 18, 2026; reconfirmed unchanged 2026-07-31.)

Assistive Devices Program (ADP) — Prosthetic Limbs and Breast Prosthetics Ontario government funding toward conventional and externally-powered (upper-limb only) prosthetic limbs for people born without, or who lost, part or all of an arm or leg (non-work-related); and external silicone breast prosthetics for people with an absent breast from mastectomy, lumpectomy, failed reconstruction, or congenital deformity. ontario.ca — Breast Prostheses and Artificial Limbs Limb absence/loss, or breast absence, Ontario. For limbs: assessment by an ADP-registered amputee team, plus a written cost estimate before purchase. For breast prosthetics: fitted at a certified business, then the full cost is paid upfront and reimbursed after — unlike most other ADP streams. Breast prosthetics — a flat grant, $195/side full or $105/side partial, rising to $260/$140 on OW/ODSP/ACSD — not a percentage. (ontario.ca updated July 10, 2025; reconfirmed unchanged 2026-07-31.)

Assistive Devices Program (ADP) — Orthotic Braces, Compression Garments and Lymphedema Pumps Ontario government funding toward custom arm, leg, spinal and cranial orthotic braces; compression garments/sleeves for hypertrophic burn scarring or chronic lymphedema; and extremity pumps for primary lymphedema. ontario.ca — Garments, Pumps and Braces Physical disability needing a brace for 6+ months, or lymphedema/burn scarring for garments/pumps, Ontario. Assistive Devices Program (ADP) — Respiratory Equipment and Supplies Ontario government funding toward apnea/cardiorespiratory monitors, air compressors, airway clearance devices, Positive Airway Pressure systems (CPAP/APAP/BPAP), suction devices, and tracheostomy equipment, for people needing it 6+ months (an exception exists for infants at SIDS risk needing an apnea monitor). ontario.ca — Respiratory Equipment and Supplies Respiratory condition needing the equipment 6+ months, Ontario. Assistive Devices Program (ADP) — Diabetes Equipment and Supplies Ontario government funding, for people with type 1 diabetes meeting clinical criteria, toward insulin pumps and pump supplies (infusion sets, cannulas) and real-time continuous glucose monitoring systems. Seniors 65+ needing daily insulin at home can separately get $170/year toward syringes and needles, regardless of diabetes type. ontario.ca — Diabetes Equipment and Supplies Type 1 diabetes meeting clinical eligibility, Ontario (any age for pumps/CGM; 65+ for the syringes/needles grant).
