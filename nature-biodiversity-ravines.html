Nature, Biodiversity, and the Ravine System

Toronto's ravines are a hidden forest running through the city — how well they're actually being protected and grown.

Scope

This backgrounder covers Toronto's ravine system, urban forest/tree canopy, and biodiversity programming (Pollinator Protection Strategy, Biodiversity Strategy, invasive-species management) as living municipal infrastructure — distinct from, and coordinating with, two sibling pages: climate-resilience-floods-heat (which owns heat-health and flood-resilience planning; ravines' stormwater-conveyance role is named here only where the Ravine Strategy's own capital reporting requires it, never re-derived) and climate-adaptation-stormwater-flooding (deep-20, which owns the corrected Basement Flooding Protection Subsidy Program figures — not restated here since ravines are not that program's subject). Development pressure at ravine edges (planning/zoning disputes) is covered here only through the lens of ravine protection, not as a general housing-supply backgrounder.

Current state

The ravine system: scale, ownership, and value

Toronto's ravine system stretches more than 300 kilometres and covers 11,000 hectares — 17 percent of the city's land area. More than half is publicly owned parkland; the remainder is held by public institutions and private property owners, including about 30,000 private addresses. Ravines contain 87 percent of the city's Environmentally Significant Areas (ESAs) and provide an estimated $822 million each year in ecological and recreational benefits. About 30 percent of Torontonians live within 500 metres of a ravine [City of Toronto, "Ravine Strategy 2026 Implementation Update," Report for Action to Infrastructure and Environment Committee, February 10, 2026].

[source quote: City of Toronto, "Ravine Strategy 2026 Implementation Update," February 10, 2026. "Toronto's ravine system stretches more than 300 kilometres and covers 11,000 hectares, representing 17 percent of the city's land area... The ravines provide an estimated $822 million each year in ecological and recreational benefits." Source: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/ie/bgrd/backgroundfile-264848.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14]

This is a direct, load-bearing upgrade over the master briefing's own figure (~17% of land area, cited without a specific hectare or dollar figure) — the master briefing's percentage is confirmed, and this review adds the hectare total, the ecological-services dollar value, and the private-ownership breakdown, none of which the master briefing carried.

Governance

Toronto's ravine and natural-area management involves the City (Environment, Climate and Forestry Division — which absorbed Urban Forestry from Parks and Recreation in 2025, with full budget integration in 2026 — plus Parks and Recreation for trails/capital, and Toronto Water for stormwater/erosion infrastructure in ravines) and the Toronto and Region Conservation Authority (TRCA), which owns much of the ravine land and leads watershed-based management and erosion-risk monitoring. City Council adopted the original Ravine Strategy in 2017 (2017.EX27.8), with implementation updates in 2020 (2020.EX12.1), 2022 (2022.IE30.9), and most recently February 10, 2026 [City of Toronto, February 10, 2026].

[source quote: City of Toronto, "Ravine Strategy 2026 Implementation Update," February 10, 2026. "In 2025, Urban Forestry (UF) transitioned from the Parks and Recreation Division (PR) to the Environment, Climate and Forestry (ECF) Division. In 2026 all budgets assigned to UF will be fully integrated with ECF." Source: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/ie/bgrd/backgroundfile-264848.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14]

Capital investment and Priority Investment Areas

The 2020 Ravine Strategy Implementation Report identified an estimated $104.9 million capital investment need across the first ten Priority Investment Areas (PIAs). Since then, 16 capital projects have been completed (wetland restoration, multi-use trail upgrades, wayfinding), totalling $12.3 million ($10 million in federal and provincial grants, $2.3 million from the City). Through annual budget submissions, cross-divisional coordination, and external funding, the outstanding PIA capital need has been reduced to $50.2 million in the 2026-2035 Capital Budget and Plan. The 2026 Operating Budget and 2026-2035 Capital Budget and Plan include $83.3 million total for Ravine Strategy implementation across both Parks and Recreation and Environment, Climate and Forestry divisions, including $14.8 million in remaining federal grants [City of Toronto, February 10, 2026].

[source quote: City of Toronto, "Ravine Strategy 2026 Implementation Update," February 10, 2026. "The outstanding capital need for Priority Investment Areas has been reduced to $50.2 million in the 2026-2035 Capital Budget and Plan... The 2026 Operating Budget and 2026–2035 Capital Budget and Plan include $83.3 million for Ravine Strategy implementation." Source: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/ie/bgrd/backgroundfile-264848.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14]

The 2026 Operating Budget's $3.6 million breaks down as: $0.7M litter picking, $2.1M invasive species management (including in ESAs), $0.3M student internships/InTO the Ravines/Indigenous engagement, and $0.5M for volunteer engagement/stewardship through the Community-led Restoration Program (per 2025 Budget item 2025.BU8.1) [same source].

Ecological restoration since 2020

Since 2020, the Ravine Strategy has achieved: 3,760 hectares of land restored (including invasive-species management), 521 tonnes of garbage and metal removed from 681 hectares of ravine land, 181 students engaged through employment/training programs, 265 Ravine Days events, and more than 20,000 participants in City and partner events [City of Toronto, February 10, 2026].

In 2025 specifically, approximately 960 hectares were managed for invasive species — a 300 percent increase in land managed by City staff and a 1,000 percent increase by contractors since 2020, when enhanced funding began phasing in [same source].

[source quote: City of Toronto, "Ravine Strategy 2026 Implementation Update," February 10, 2026. "In 2025, this resulted in approximately 960 hectares of land being managed for invasive species... a 300 per cent increase in land managed by staff and 1,000 per cent increase by contractors" since 2020. Source: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/ie/bgrd/backgroundfile-264848.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14]

Toronto Nature Stewards and community-led restoration

The Community-led Restoration Program, piloted with Toronto Nature Stewards (TNS) since 2021 (TNS is hosted by the University of Toronto's Institute of Forestry & Conservation, John H. Daniels Faculty of Architecture, Landscape, and Design), recruited and trained over 2,000 volunteers in 2025 to steward 47 sites (52 planned for 2026) covering over 120 hectares of natural-area parkland, including monitoring and managing more than 35 invasive plant species. This February 2026 report recommends permanent implementation of the Community-led Restoration Program, moving it beyond pilot status [City of Toronto, February 10, 2026].

[source quote: City of Toronto, "Ravine Strategy 2026 Implementation Update," February 10, 2026. "TNS recruited and trained over 2,000 volunteers in 2025 to steward 47 sites that they identified and applied for (52 planned for 2026) covering over 120 hectares of natural area parkland across the city... monitoring and managing more than 35 invasive plant species." Source: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/ie/bgrd/backgroundfile-264848.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14]

A related joint project, "Project Buckthorn" (Urban Forestry, the national charity Don't Mess with the Don, and Toronto Nature Stewards), targets common buckthorn removal in Sunnybrook Park (Ward 15) [same source].

The InTO the Ravines program (with Park People, launched 2020) has received $0.545 million in City investment over five years, matched and exceeded by Park People fundraising for a total of $1.09 million; in 2025 it supported over 50 events and engaged over 1,200 participants, about one quarter of whom were first-time ravine visitors [same source]. This report recommends renewing the City–Park People agreement to December 31, 2026, renewable up to two additional one-year terms to December 31, 2028.

Environmentally Significant Areas (ESAs)

Toronto has 89 Environmentally Significant Areas, identified through scientific study and mapped on the Official Plan's Map 12A. 67 are under City management; the remaining 22 are managed by TRCA, the Toronto Zoo, or are privately owned. Of City-managed ESAs, 63 are actively managed and 22 have documented monitoring/maintenance plans. The Official Plan prohibits development within ESAs and the broader Natural Heritage System, except for certain compatible uses. A new City-wide ESA Management Plan and Framework has been piloted for two ESAs — Taylor Massey Creek and Wilket Creek Forest [City of Toronto, February 10, 2026].

Climate pressure on the ravines, named directly by the City

The February 2026 report explicitly links climate change to ravine vulnerability, citing the same July 2024 storm event this page’s sibling pages also treat: "In July 2024, Toronto experienced an intense storm that exceeded a 100-year storm in parts of the city, causing extensive flooding and erosion damage across the city and resulted in hundreds of millions of dollars in damage, such as the failure of the embankment that separated Mud Creek from the wetlands in Don Valley Brick Works (DVBW) Park." A permanent, resilient design solution for the DVBW site is described as "currently underway" as of February 2026 [City of Toronto, February 10, 2026]. This is named here as the ravines' own account of the same storm event; the corrected dollar figures for City flood-subsidy programs belong to the sibling climate-adaptation-stormwater-flooding leaf and are not re-derived here.

The report also names the specific invasive/pest species of concern threatening ravines as climate warms: common buckthorn, dog-strangling vine, garlic mustard, Japanese knotweed, phragmites, emerald ash borer, and oak wilt — explaining that a lengthened growing season and warmer winters (reduced die-off mortality) let these species increasingly outcompete native plants [same source].

Emerald ash borer

Emerald Ash Borer (EAB) has been present in Toronto since 2007 and has killed more than 800,000 ash trees in the city. The City injects more than 5,300 ash trees with TreeAzin insecticide annually (June 1–August 31 treatment window in 2025) on a rotating three-year cycle that restarted in 2025 [search-result characterization, ⚠️ still being checked — this figure was not independently fetched from a primary City document this review; it is carried at search-confirmed tier only, distinct from the directly-fetched Ravine Strategy figures above].

Biodiversity Strategy and Pollinator Protection Strategy

Toronto City Council unanimously adopted its first Biodiversity Strategy, "Wild, Connected, And Diverse: A Biodiversity Strategy for Toronto," on October 2, 2019 — explicitly designed to align with the Ravine Strategy and the Pollinator Protection Strategy "by addressing shared issues" [search-result characterization drawing on City of Toronto source documents identified in search (backgroundfile-136774.pdf, 2019), ⚠️ still being checked — the Biodiversity Strategy's own PDF was not directly fetched this review; the adoption date and alignment framing are carried at search-confirmed tier].

The Pollinator Protection Strategy was unanimously adopted by City Council in April 2018, covering more than 360 species of bees and 100+ species of butterflies and other pollinators, with six priorities and 30 actions. The PollinateTO grants program funded 112 pollinator gardens through 44 community groups in 2023; a "How to PollinateTO" in-house training series (7 weeks) launched in 2024 [search-result characterization, ⚠️ still being checked — not independently fetched this review].

Ravine-edge development pressure: the Glen Stewart Ravine case

A concrete, named instance of the "development vs. nature" tension the master briefing raises abstractly: real estate developer Gabriele Homes Ltd. proposed an 11-storey, 99-unit mixed-use residential building at 847-855 Kingston Road, adjacent to the north end of Glen Stewart Ravine, an Environmentally Significant Area in the Beaches neighbourhood. The original application (a 7-storey building) was filed in August 2015; a revised 11-storey proposal was submitted in December 2022 after the developer acquired the adjacent property. The proposal included a zero-metre setback from the long-term stable top of slope, where the Official Plan and TRCA policy require a minimum 10-metre setback — and included removal of seven ravine trees, including an 83.5 cm-diameter red oak. On November 12, 2024, the developer appealed to the Ontario Land Tribunal (OLT), citing City Council's failure to decide on the application within the Planning Act's statutory timeframe [Beach Metro Community News, "Plan for 11-storey building by Glen Stewart Ravine heading to Ontario Land Tribunal," April 24, 2025].

[source quote: Beach Metro Community News, Matthew Stephens, "Plan for 11-storey building by Glen Stewart Ravine heading to Ontario Land Tribunal," April 24, 2025. "They proposed a zero-metre setback from the long term stable top of slope, where our Official Plan and Conservation Authority policy requires a minimum 10-metre setback," said Beaches-East York Councillor Brad Bradford, who supported City legal staff opposing the application at the OLT. Source: https://beachmetro.com/2025/04/24/plan-for-11-storey-building-by-glen-stewart-ravine-heading-to-ontario-land-tribunal/ · accessed 2026-07-14]

The local Beaches-East York councillor's office publicly supported City legal staff opposing the application at the OLT. Community opposition centred on the Friends of Glen Stewart Ravine (a 1,200+ member Facebook group), which raised the inadequate setback, tree removal, and habitat impacts on "Special Concern" species including the Eastern Wood-Pewee and bat maternity colonies.

Resolution (2026): the OLT hearing, originally scheduled for April 2026, did not proceed; the parties instead reached a settlement (offered October 2025) under which the building height increased to 13 storeys but its footprint was reduced, increasing the setback from the ravine to 10 metres on the south side and 3.8 metres on the west side [search-result characterization of NOW Toronto reporting on the settlement hearing, ⚠️ still being checked — the settlement outcome itself was not independently fetched from a primary OLT or City document this review; only the original April 2025 dispute reporting was directly fetched and quote-verified].

This case is offered as a single, well-documented instance of the ravine-edge development tension the master briefing names in the abstract ("development vs. nature is a real tension... though density and green can coexist") — not as a representative sample of all ravine-adjacent development applications citywide. No claim is made here about how typical this outcome is; that would require a systematic review of OLT ravine-adjacent cases, which is outside this review's scope.

Toronto: the case for and against

Section merged 2026-08-11 from a companion Toronto-specific brief (this library's internal records, now a tombstone). This entire backgrounder is already Toronto-specific, so nearly all of the brief's evidentiary substance already appears in "Current state" above, drawn from the same February 2026 City report; this section carries forward the brief's FOR/AGAINST framing, two figures not otherwise stated in this document's own body prose, and its bottom-line synthesis.

FOR: Toronto's ravine investment is producing measured, verifiable ecological outcomes, not just spending — 3,760 hectares restored, 521 tonnes of garbage/metal removed, and 960 hectares actively managed for invasive species in 2025 alone [City of Toronto, February 10, 2026]; the City's capital strategy is working as designed, with the Priority Investment Area gap cut nearly in half ($104.9M to $50.2M) through a genuinely blended funding model [same source]; community-powered stewardship has scaled substantially and is not merely symbolic, with Toronto Nature Stewards training over 2,000 volunteers in 2025 across 47 sites, and the City's own February 2026 report recommending the program be made permanent [same source]; Toronto has real international standing on this file, selected in 2023 as one of eleven global UN Decade on Ecosystem Restoration Role Model Cities [same source]; and — a figure not otherwise carried in this document's own "Current state" section above — the equity-targeting mechanism embedded in program design appears to be functioning: 56% of 2025 stewardship events were held in Neighbourhood Improvement Areas, Emerging Neighbourhoods, and low Tree Equity Score neighbourhoods, against the City's own 50% internal target [City of Toronto, February 10, 2026].

AGAINST: a $50.2 million Priority Investment Area capital gap remains unfunded and unscheduled — the 2026-2035 Capital Budget and Plan's own capital-specific allocation, backed out from the $83.3 million combined operating-and-capital total documented above, works out to roughly $79.7 million for Ravine Strategy capital citywide, but sets no specific closure date for the PIA-specific shortfall (a brief-derived figure, not independently stated as such by the primary source itself); the system's own protection standard was not held without a fight in the Glen Stewart Ravine case, resolved only via a multi-year Ontario Land Tribunal process after City Council did not decide within the statutory timeframe (see "Ravine-edge development pressure" above); the 2026 settlement reportedly increased the building's height (11 to 13 storeys) in exchange for the corrected setback, meaning the resolution mechanism produced a more favourable height outcome for the applicant even as the setback violation was cured (see "Key tensions/tradeoffs" below); and the claimed increase in land actively managed for invasive species (300% by staff, 1,000% by contractors since 2020) shows that most of the measured increase in ecological-management capacity came from paid contractors, not the more visible volunteer stewardship program (see "Key tensions/tradeoffs" below).

Toronto bottom line: Toronto's ravine and biodiversity programming shows genuine, measured ecological progress backed by a directly-verified primary source — restoration hectares, a shrinking capital gap, and a scaling stewardship program are all real and current as of February 2026 — but a $50.2 million capital gap remains open without a closure schedule, and a concrete, documented development-pressure case (Glen Stewart Ravine) shows the system's own protection standards being tested and only partially held at the ravine edge, resolved through a multi-year Tribunal process rather than a City-driven decision within the statutory timeframe.

Toronto-specific uncertainties: all of the brief's open items — the search-result-only sourcing behind the Biodiversity Strategy's adoption details, the Pollinator Protection Strategy's figures, the emerald ash borer mortality estimate, and the Glen Stewart Ravine settlement's final terms; whether the Glen Stewart Ravine case represents a citywide pattern or a single instance; and the absence of a current, primary-sourced tree-canopy percentage or target tied specifically to the Ravine Strategy — are already carried in "What the evidence does and doesn't support" and "Open questions/data gaps" below; no additional uncertainty distinct from that list was found in the merged brief.

Key tensions/tradeoffs

  • Development density vs. ravine-edge protection. The Glen Stewart Ravine case shows this is not merely theoretical: a developer secured a taller building (13 vs. 11 storeys) in exchange for a smaller footprint and a compliant setback — a trade that increased height in exchange for restoring the Official Plan's own 10-metre standard. Whether this is a genuine win for ravine protection or a precedent that normalizes going to the OLT rather than negotiating locally is a live, unresolved question this review does not adjudicate.
  • Community-powered stewardship vs. paid capacity. Toronto Nature Stewards' 2,000+ volunteers stewarding 120+ hectares is presented by the City itself as a core strategy, not a stopgap — the February 2026 report recommends permanently institutionalizing the Community-led Restoration Program. This raises an open question the report itself does not fully resolve: whether reliance on volunteer labour for invasive-species management (a technical, ongoing task) is durable at the scale the "critical state" framing implies, especially given the parallel finding that paid contractor-managed hectares grew 1,000 percent since 2020 — i.e., paid capacity, not volunteer capacity, drove most of the measured increase in land actively managed for invasives.
  • Capital progress vs. remaining need. The $104.9M-to-$50.2M reduction in Priority Investment Area capital needs is real progress, but $50.2 million in unmet capital need remains identified and unfunded in the City's own 10-year plan as of 2026 — this is not a resolved gap, only a smaller one.

International context

What other cities have tried. Toronto was selected in September 2023 as one of eleven global Role Model Cities for Generation Restoration, a United Nations Decade on Ecosystem Restoration (2021-2030) project — recognition for contributions to nature-based solutions for biodiversity and climate. With that project concluding at the end of 2025, the City's February 2026 report recommends joining Cities with Nature, a UN Environment Programme-identified platform for continued international city-to-city collaboration on urban nature [City of Toronto, February 10, 2026].

What actually transferred to Toronto, and how. The Tree Equity Score Analyzer (documented in the sibling climate-resilience-floods-heat leaf) was built in partnership with American Forests, a US non-profit — a concrete instance of an external methodology being imported and adapted rather than built from scratch, though that tool is specifically canopy/heat-equity focused rather than ravine-specific. This backgrounder's own live-discovery pass did not locate a comparably concrete case of an external ravine-management or urban-biodiversity practice being imported into Toronto's Ravine or Biodiversity Strategy specifically; the Generation Restoration/Cities with Nature relationship is presented by the City as peer recognition and knowledge-exchange, not as a case of Toronto adopting a specific practice from a named peer city.

Toronto's own position as exporter. The Generation Restoration selection and the City's own "international recognition" framing in the February 2026 report suggest Toronto positions itself as a demonstration/exporter city on ravine-based ecological restoration and nature-based solutions, alongside ten other Role Model Cities globally — though this review did not identify the other ten cities or verify comparative claims about Toronto's relative standing among them.

Indigenous context

The February 2026 Ravine Strategy Implementation Update states the City "is committed to incorporating both western and Indigenous knowledge in protecting and reimagining our ravines and will continue to identify opportunities to create authentic and ongoing relationships with Indigenous communities," and describes the ESA Management Plan and Framework as developed "through input from the community and across City divisions, as well as from Treaty Holder Nations and the Urban Indigenous community" [City of Toronto, February 10, 2026]. This backgrounder defers substantive treatment of the land relationship and Indigenous stewardship to the forthcoming Indigenous-context suite referenced in the master briefing document, consistent with this page’s own scope discipline — a one-line deferral rather than an attempt to independently characterize that relationship here.
