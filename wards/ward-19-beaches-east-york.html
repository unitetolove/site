Ward 19 — Beaches-East York

Reading this in real time: every registrant figure below — who’s filed, incumbency, candidate counts — is a snapshot as of 2026-08-14, from the City Clerk’s candidate feeds. Nominations close Friday, August 21, 2026, at 2:00 p.m. — this page re-pulls and re-stamps every registrant figure after that date. Geography, transit, development and census figures are structural facts that don’t move at nomination close and are dated separately, section by section.

Beaches-East York runs along the lake in Toronto's east end — the Beach(es) neighbourhood, Upper Beaches/East End-Danforth, Woodbine Corridor, East Danforth, and part of Old East York. It's a lower-rise, largely residential ward with a historic Carnegie library on Kew Gardens, a subway line along its northern edge, and a GO station midway through a multi-year relocation and rebuild.

A federal by-election is happening here first — Monday, August 31, 2026. This is separate from the Ward 19 council race below: different office (Member of Parliament, not city councillor), different boundary (the federal riding of Beaches–East York, not the city ward), different ballot, different day. Six candidates are on the final ballot; the list closed August 12, 2026 and was re-confirmed directly against Elections Canada on 2026-08-15.

The Aug 31, 2026 federal by-election here

Nate Erskine-Smith, the Liberal MP who held the seat since 2015, resigned effective July 7, 2026. Prime Minister Mark Carney called the by-election for Monday, August 31, 2026. This is a federal race — the office is Member of Parliament for Beaches–East York, not a city council seat, and it runs on Elections Canada’s rules, not the City of Toronto’s.

The riding is not the ward. The federal riding of Beaches–East York (electoral district 35007) and the City of Toronto’s Ward 19 “Beaches-East York” share the same cardinal boundary streets in their public descriptions — Coxwell Avenue (west), Sunrise Avenue/the Don River (north), Victoria Park Avenue (east), Lake Ontario (south) — but they are legally distinct boundaries, not the same map. One candidate’s own published release states plainly that riding-level figures “closely align with, though [are] not identical to,” the ward. If you want to confirm which federal riding your address is in before voting, use Elections Canada’s own lookup at elections.ca — Voter Information Service rather than assuming your ward and your riding match.

  • Advance polls: Friday, Aug 21 through Monday, Aug 24, 2026, 9 a.m.–9 p.m. each day, at your assigned advance station.

  • Vote by mail: apply online or at the local office by Tuesday, Aug 25, 6 p.m.; completed ballots must reach Elections Canada by election day (or be dropped at the local office until polls close).

How Beaches—East York actually participates

Turnout, and only turnout. Everything in this half of the page is the share of registered electors who cast a ballot — the one figure in an election-results file that carries no partisan information at all. It says who showed up; it never says who anyone voted for. Elections Canada publishes poll-level results by candidate; this project committed to publishing participation only, never a vote for anyone, and no party’s vote is mapped anywhere on this site. Nothing below names a candidate or a party.

At the 45th general election (April 28, 2025) the riding had 82,102 electors on the list and 59,245 ballots were cast — a turnout of 72.16%.

Half of it happened before election day. 29,083 ballots — 49.09% of everything cast — were advance, mail or special ballots, which have no home polling division. Only 30,162 ballots (36.74% of electors) were cast at a polling division on election day itself. That is why a straight sum of the per-poll file produces 36.74% and not 72.16%: the two are different questions, not different answers to the same one, and every figure on this page says which one it is.

And the riding is not one place. Across polling divisions with at least 50 electors, election-day turnout runs from 22.22% (polling division 49, 378 electors) to 69.78% (polling division 401, 139 electors) — a 47.6 percentage-point spread inside a single riding, on a number that has nothing to do with which party anyone supports.

Total turnout, by advance-poll zone

The table above is election day only. The table below is everything — advance ballots included — at the coarser grain Elections Canada’s advance zones make possible, and it is the only place in this profile where a true total-turnout figure exists below the riding level. Read at that grain the riding runs from 55.9% to 75.7%: a 19.8-point gap between two parts of the same riding. And the share cast in advance moves with it: across these 15 zones the two correlate at r = +0.75, running from 35.7% to 54.2% of a zone’s ballots. What changed since 2021 — and the correction we had to publish

Comparing 2021 to 2025 in this riding is harder than it looks: the 2023 redistribution redrew the boundary and renumbered every polling division, so the two years are only comparable at the neighbourhood grain, and only after the merged polls of 2021 are folded onto the rows that actually received their electors. Done properly, the riding moved 35.8% → 36.8% on election day — +1.0 points across 10 neighbourhoods present in both years.

  • Old East York +11.4 points
  • Danforth +5.4 points
  • East End-Danforth +3.2 points
  • Danforth East York +3.0 points
  • Victoria Village +2.7 points
  • Taylor-Massey +2.5 points
  • Woodbine Corridor +2.0 points
  • Woodbine-Lumsden -0.2 points
  • O'Connor-Parkview -1.5 points
  • The Beaches -2.6 points

The row worth pausing on is Taylor-Massey, the riding’s tower core and its lowest-participating neighbourhood: +2.5 points. It neither fell further behind nor caught up. Whatever produced the riding’s small recovery since 2021, it did not happen there.

And the gap between the riding’s neighbourhoods narrowed. Across the 10 neighbourhoods measurable in both years, the distance between the highest-participating and the lowest-participating went from 9.3 points in 2021 to 7.0 points in 2025. The riding’s participation gap is real, it is large, and it did not get wider.

We published a wrong version of this, and this is the correction. An earlier build of our own data stories folded 2021’s merged polling divisions onto the wrong denominator. Three things followed from that one error: a neighbourhood that had left the riding was published at nearly double its true election-day rate; one neighbourhood’s change was published at more than twice its true size; and the headline claim — that the riding’s participation gap had widened — was the reverse of what the corrected data says. All three were found and fixed on 2026-08-16, the affected story was retitled and re-published at a new address with a redirect from the old one, and this generator now refuses to build at all if the corrected direction is ever reversed again. The figures on this page are the corrected ones. Nothing on this site should still describe that gap as growing; if you find something that does, it is wrong and we want to hear about it.

Why this section says 36.8% and the section above says 36.74%. They are the same ballots over two different lists of electors, and the difference is the point of the section above. The riding-wide 36.74% divides election-day ballots by every elector on the list — including the 29,083 who voted in advance, by mail or by special ballot and therefore have no home polling division. The 36.8% here divides the same ballots by the 74,947 electors actually attached to an ordinary polling division inside a mapped neighbourhood, which is the only basis on which 2021 and 2025 can be compared at all — and it is computed identically in both years. The two are a tenth of a point apart; neither is wrong, and neither is the other.

Where nobody is — the riding’s negative space

Almost every civic map published about a place shows what is there. This one shows what is not. We put a 120-metre grid over the riding, clipped it to land (the riding polygon runs out into Lake Ontario; 116 sample points over water were dropped rather than counted, which would have inflated the headline by a quarter), and measured every one of the 1,161 remaining points to the nearest community institution with a real, walkable address.

  • About 11,918 residents — 10.8% of the riding — live more than a 600-metre walk (roughly seven minutes) from any community institution on our map.
  • 97% of that entire gap sits in one neighbourhood: O’Connor-Parkview. 18,405 residents, 62.7% of its residential land beyond the walk, about 11,531 people in the gap. It is 54% renter and 39% immigrant, and it is nowhere near the commercial corridor where community work in this riding naturally concentrates — so no amount of corridor work will touch it.
  • It is also the riding’s largest population of Tagalog and Greek speakers and its second-largest of Urdu speakers, and it carries zero organisations with any evidenced capability in any of those languages.
What this measures, and what it does not. It measures where our map has no door — not where people are underserved. A gap over a ravine or a rail corridor is geometry; a gap over apartment blocks is a finding, and the two are separated in the underlying data rather than averaged together. It is also worth quoting the denominator whenever this number is quoted: the walk measurement uses the 78 institutions with a real walkable location, while per-capita coverage rates use the 93 institutions inside the riding including those known only to a general area. Those are two different true answers to two different questions, and neither is the other.

The language supply gap

Of 99 community organisations checked at priority, 18 have evidence of operating in at least one of Bengali, Cantonese, Mandarin, Tagalog, Urdu or Greek — and every single one of them sits in one corridor, the stretch of the Danforth between Main Street and Victoria Park. Not one sits in The Beaches, O’Connor-Parkview, Woodbine Corridor, Woodbine-Lumsden, Danforth, Old East York or Victoria Village. A further 25 report some interpretation route without running a programme in the language themselves. The remainder are recorded as unknown, which is not the same as no.

How word travels here

This riding still runs on print, email and Facebook — in that order. There is no riding subreddit (re-confirmed against a 272-community census of Toronto subreddits), no neighbourhood Discord, no publicly joinable WhatsApp community, no local radio station, no riding-native video creator, and no independent local newsletter beyond the ones the institutions described below already publish themselves. What it has instead is a non-profit community newspaper that has published since 1972 and is still delivered riding-wide by volunteers, a dense archipelago of Facebook groups, and — running in parallel and barely touching either — a substantially separate Bengali-language media system anchored on the Danforth.

Three information ecosystems, which overlap far less than their geography suggests: the Beach and Upper Beaches system (English, older, homeowner-weighted, organised around that newspaper and the business-improvement-area accounts near it); the Danforth Village and East Danforth system (mixed, renter- and family-weighted, organised around an eighteen-year-old neighbourhood association with its own blog, newsletter, weekly farmers market and autumn arts fair); and the Bengali-language system in Crescent Town and Taylor-Massey (a Facebook-first digital weekly, two Bengali-language newspapers distributed through mosques and Danforth businesses, and Canada’s first 24-hour Bengali television channel). Cutting across all three is a parents’ network of some 3,700 members running fifty-plus playgroups a session across four neighbourhoods — the best-organised non-institutional network in the riding.

What neighbours actually report to the City

Read this before you read a number. 311 measures who calls, not what is wrong. Whether a broken thing becomes a 311 record depends on knowing the service exists, believing it will work, having the English or the patience for the interpretation queue, having the tenure to feel entitled to complain, and living in a housing form where the complaint is yours to make rather than your building’s. A neighbourhood that calls less is not a neighbourhood with fewer problems — it is at least as likely to be the newer, poorer, more-renting, less-English one, whose problems go to a superintendent, a landlord, or nowhere. Read naively, this dataset says the wealthiest part of the riding is the part with the most going wrong. That reading is almost certainly backwards.

In the 2025 calendar year the riding filed 27,012 service requests — 247 per 1,000 residents against a citywide 179, an index of 1.38. The honest comparisons are the narrow ones, because they strip out the housing-form effect: a house on curbside collection has three bins and three separate chances to report a missed pickup, while a 300-unit tower has one bin and one superintendent who calls once, or never. On the public realm the riding runs 105 per 1,000 against 80 citywide; on the strictest cut of all — roads, sidewalks, snow and ice, delivered to a bungalow and a twenty-storey slab on identical terms — 50 against 39.

Source: City of Toronto Open Data, 311 Service Requests – Customer Initiated, 1 January – 31 December 2025 (the most recent complete calendar year), OGL – Toronto, fetched 2026-08-15. Renting here, and what is proposed on this land

The riding holds 202 registered apartment buildings with 13,170 rental units between them on the City’s RentSafeTO register. Against that, the City’s development pipeline carries 14,266 proposed residential units: more housing is proposed in this riding than exists in its entire registered rental stock today.

A trap worth naming, because it is easy to fall into. The City publishes one row per street address inside a development application. The riding’s 514 rows collapse to 219 distinct applications. Counting rows instead of applications overstates the pipeline by roughly two and a third times, and that is exactly the mistake a quick read of the open data produces.

On building condition, 57 buildings show a declining trajectory on at least one signal — either a falling score within the pre-2023 inspection era, or a drop of fifteen or more percentile points in their standing among the riding’s own buildings across the 2023 rubric change. None flags on both. Raw scores are never compared across that rubric change anywhere in this work, because the two scales do not mean the same thing; only a building’s rank among its neighbours survives the change. Buildings and companies only — nothing here is about any tenant.

Two accessibility numbers that are routinely quoted as one. 68 of the 202 registered buildings (34%) report an entrance you can get through without steps. Inside them, 530 of the 13,170 registered homes (4.0%) are recorded as barrier-free. A ramp at the door is not an accessible home; the register is precise enough to show the difference, which also means the entrance figure is the more flattering of the two and the one most likely to be quoted alone.

What this project refuses to do

  • Turnout only, never partisan geography. Elections Canada publishes results by candidate at the poll level. We hold those files and have deliberately never mapped them. No party’s vote appears on any map or in any dataset this project publishes.
  • No individuals. Institutions and public roles only, counted rather than named. The finest geography anywhere in this work is the polling division — a few blocks — and no address-level record is ever published.
  • No voters list, ever. Election law gives it to candidates and parties. This is not a campaign, we are not entitled to it, and we would not take it.
  • No modelled persuasion scores and no targeting model of any kind.
  • Participation only, never a vote for anyone. A campaign wants your vote; this is a neighbours’ project, and it can only help you cast one, for whoever you choose.

Three counts of the same place, and why they differ

Three independent measures of how many people live here sit within 578 people of each other — half of one percent: Statistics Canada’s count for federal electoral district 35007 is 109,359, the City’s count for Ward 19 is 109,465, and this project’s own method — spreading census population over residential land only and clipping it to the riding boundary — gives 109,937. They differ because the federal riding and the city ward are not the same shape: they are drawn by different bodies under different rules and share their cardinal boundary streets without sharing a map. Where a figure on this page is a riding figure it says so; where it is a ward figure it says so. If you need to know which federal riding your own address is in, Elections Canada’s own lookup is the only answer that cannot go stale.

Our own defect record

This project keeps a register of what its own published work got wrong, and fixes it at the generator rather than in the rendered page. The 2026-08-16 pass found and corrected a 2021 merged-poll denominator error that had produced one badly inflated neighbourhood figure, one badly inflated change figure, a literal missing value served as part of a public download, and a headline claim pointing in the wrong direction. All four were fixed the same day and are described in full in the correction notice above. This generator now re-derives each of the affected figures from the source data every time it runs and refuses to write anything at all if one of them has moved.

What this page doesn’t cover yet

This ward’s research brief names these additional gaps, plainly, rather than guessing over them:

  • Main Street Station's precise inclusion within Ward 19's polygon is stated from general geography (it sits just north of Danforth near the ward's western edge), not a verified point-in-polygon check — flagged, not asserted as precise.
  • The October 2023 UrbanToronto growth report's full text was not independently re-fetched (search-result confirmation of the article's existence/title only) — its specific project list should be pulled directly before use in a public-facing profile.
  • No current-year (2026) comprehensive private-development-application count was pulled for this ward — a full pipeline needs the City's Application Information Centre, not attempted.
  • Trustee-ward crosswalk not attempted here — already a named citywide gap (22 of 25 wards, per `profiles/`), out of this brief's scope.
  • No riding-level poll of this by-election exists — none has been published by anyone. Seat projections circulating for this riding are models built from national and regional numbers, not measurements of opinion here, and this page does not reproduce them as if they were.
  • Every turnout figure here is one election out of date the moment the polls close. The ingestion path for the August 31 result is built and dry-run clean; the figures above will be refreshed once Elections Canada publishes.
  • The census layer is at neighbourhood grain, not dissemination-area grain. A finer join is possible and has not been built, so every demographic figure here is a share-weighted neighbourhood average rather than a block-level one.
  • Nothing on this page has been translated and verified in any language other than English, in a riding where the census shows a substantial population that a purely English public conversation does not reach. Drafts exist; none has been confirmed by a named reviewer, and none will be published until one is.
  • Nobody has asked a resident of this riding what they need. Everything above is the supply side — what is here, where it is, who it reaches. The demand side is not data we hold, and no amount of open data substitutes for it.
  • A full address-to-trustee-district crosswalk is not complete for every board (school board trustee races do not share the 25-ward boundary map): for this ward, TDSB carries a news-reported mapping, not yet verified against an official board document and TCDSB is resolved to one exact TCDSB ward; CSC MonAvenir is resolved only to a region (Toronto-Est or Toronto-Ouest), never a ward, for every ward citywide. Conseil scolaire Viamonde is the one board fully resolved, exact, for every ward. See the tiered breakdown and this ward’s precision note in “What else is on your ballot” above.
  • Libraries and community/recreation centres are now geospatially joined to this ward’s boundary (see “What’s live here, by the numbers” under Local commons above — complete citywide datasets for both layers). Parks joining is partial citywide; not shown rather than shown unevenly (400 of 1,511 City-classified park-type rows fetched and joined so far).
