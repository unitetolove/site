State of the Election — Ontario 2026

The province-wide companion to the Toronto edition, from the standing election record. First Ontario edition August 7, 2026; this is the weekend edition, field truth as of August 10–11, 2026 — built on a completed collection: all 444 municipal clerk candidate lists gathered, and 4,751 registered candidates on file, 4,743 of them individually researched to a dated disposition (the remaining 8 are list-only, closing at nomination day). The receipts are at the bottom. Companion analysis: the Ontario Platform Baseline.

Weekend edition — field truth as of August 10–11, 2026: every figure below is a snapshot computed from the official municipal clerk candidate lists and our own per-candidate verification record. Nominations close Friday, August 21, 2026, at 2:00 p.m. everywhere in Ontario — the field is still moving, and every number here will be recomputed and re-stamped after close.

The whole story in one paragraph

Ontario is about to hold roughly 2,300 municipal elections at once — 2,289 races across 414 municipalities and the school boards. On today's filings, 1,457 of 3,180 listed seats would be filled without a contest (a seat count — many races elect several members; at the race level, 41.8% of races have no more candidates than seats), and 316 races (13.8%) have no candidates at all. Around those quiet ballots sits a quieter public record: of 4,743 individually researched candidates — 26.7% have a campaign website, 19.2% have anything resembling a platform page (post-correction registry — 13 wrong-attribution website values removed on re-verification; receipt §11 reconciliation), and once you leave the big cities both numbers collapse, down to 3.4% website coverage in the under-5,000 municipalities where a quarter of races have nobody running at all. In 854 of the 948 fully-researched acclamation-track races — 90.1% — no registrant has published a platform anywhere we could find: people on track to take office without a contest and without a written plan. None of this is an accusation; it is a system running on defaults. The door is open for ten more days: papers cost as little as $100 ($200 for mayor) and an afternoon, and in hundreds of these races one filing changes everything.

2,289 races, and the two-week window

  • 2,289 races across every directly-electing municipality in Ontario (414 of 414, checked against the province's own lists): 1,071 councillor · 604 school trustee · 414 mayor · 126 deputy mayor · 67 regional councillor · 7 county councillor. 4,751 candidates on file.
  • 316 races have zero registered candidates — 176 of them school-trustee seats, 97 councillor, 34 mayoralties. 956 races are on acclamation track (candidates ≤ seats), and 181 of those were acclaimed in 2022 too — the chronic no-choice class.
  • The gradient is size, not virtue: in municipalities under 5,000 people, 24.4% of races have nobody registered; above 100,000 people, 1.4%. No party recruits here, no war room staffs these races — and the smaller the place, the emptier the ballot.

Why this matters to you: if you live outside a big city, the odds are real that some line on your October ballot has no candidates, or exactly one. The filing window closes August 21 at 2 p.m. In a zero-candidate race, one afternoon of paperwork makes you the contest — here is everything you need.

And one class of office is off the ballot entirely, by law: the regional chairs of Durham, Halton, Muskoka, Niagara, Peel, Waterloo and York, plus Simcoe County's warden, are provincially appointed under the Better Regional Governance Act, 2026 — verified against the regions' own election pages. Roughly 4.5 million Ontarians will not vote for the office that chairs the government spending their regional tax dollars. That is a fact about the system worth knowing on its own.

Why this matters to you: an acclamation is often a good person doing a thankless job unopposed. But 1,457 seats on autopilot is a pattern — and in 181 races there was no contest in 2022 either. Contested government is a habit a place keeps or loses; keeping it takes exactly one more candidate per race.

By municipality size the same line runs 3.4% (under 5k) → 11.2% (5–25k) → 34.9% (25–100k) → 52.6% (100k+) website coverage. The least-findable offices: deputy mayors (11.8% with a website) and school trustees (15.0%).

The darkest, cleanest number on the map — acclamation × silence: of 948 fully-researched acclamation-track races, 854 (90.1%) have no registrant with a published platform; 809 have no registrant with even a website. 1,309 seats. And in contested races, 484 of 904 fully-researched (53.5%) offer a real choice with nothing to read on any side.

Fairness, stated plainly: absence of a published platform is a fact about the public record, not about a candidate's effort, character, or worth. Many of these candidates are door-knocking daily in places where that works. The claim is only that a voter who goes looking can't read anything — and that this is fixable in an afternoon.

Why this matters to you: in most of Ontario, "look up the candidates" is not currently something a voter can do. Candidates: the free kit takes you from nothing to a published, costed, dated platform in a weekend — and instantly puts you in the province's top few percent on published substance.

The platform layer, measured

We didn't stop at "has a page." The Ontario Platform Baseline read every published platform in the province — 2.36 million words of assessable candidate text (2,359,387 words across 484 candidates) — and scored each one under a public, viewpoint-neutral instrument (eight anchored dimensions, a commitment gate, office-scoped breadth, a quote behind every score, blind double-rating with published agreement statistics). The short version:

  • 85.9% of Ontario's 4,743 researched candidates offer the public nothing readable about what they would do in office — no platform found (search-verified) or a site with no platform on it. Toronto was re-scored fresh alongside the rest of the province this edition under the identical v2.1 instrument — and as of August 11, every candidate with any captured content has been individually read (416 scored platforms province-wide, up from a provisional 245 before the completion pass finished the word-count-gated sites) — see the full baseline for the class-by-class breakdown.
  • Where platforms exist, candidates campaign inside their office's real powers (the strongest measured dimension, everywhere) — but still rarely say when things happen or with whose money; the full per-dimension figures are on the baseline page.
  • The school-board blind spot holds everywhere: trustees are among the least likely to publish anything, and published trustee platforms tend to score lowest — for the offices spending the most per unit of public attention.
  • Incumbency: of 307 candidates on file flagged as incumbents, only 55 (17.9%) have a platform/policy URL on record and 87 (28.3%) have any campaign website at all (post-correction registry, receipt §11). Challengers as a class continue to out-publish incumbents. (An incumbent's record is a public document — but a record is what you did, not what you propose to do.)

And the good news, praise-only and linked: the exceptional platforms — the 74 candidates across Ontario who wrote down the province's most concrete published plans, each named with a verbatim policy commitment from their own platform. The bar is passable; they prove it.

The issue map — what the platforms that exist talk about

Among the 484 candidates with real platform text: housing appears in 82.8%, transit 76.8%, governance and transparency 75.7%, health and social services 75.1%, taxes and spending 74.9%. The bottom of the table is the story: climate and environment appear in barely half (51.5%), schools and education in 44.0% — and equity/inclusion (24.9%) and rural and agricultural issues (22.8%) are the things almost nobody names, the last one remarkable in a province where most municipalities are rural.

Copy-paste democracy? We checked.

We compared all 484 assessable platform texts against each other for borrowed language (8-word shingles). After separating out website-builder boilerplate (privacy policies and cookie banners — which, yes, a good share of the province's campaign sites share), the finding is: zero cases of platform-plank plagiarism copied between unrelated campaigns. Eleven mechanical clusters surfaced, spanning 34 candidates and 22 municipalities — every one of them turned out to be site-builder legal text, CMS widget artifacts, shared municipal procedural language, or a single vendor's GOTV/lawn-sign template, never a shared policy plank. Ontario's problem is not plagiarized platforms; it is that there is almost nothing to copy. The same sweep caught something better than a scandal: four candidates who had been credited with someone else's website, plus two poisoned captures (parked/login pages mistaken for platform text) — all found and corrected in our registry before they could contaminate a single published number. That is what verification is for.

One service finding from our passive site-hygiene scan, published in aggregate only: 47% of 998 checked campaign sites have no DMARC email protection — meaning anyone can send email that appears to come from those campaigns. Individual findings go privately to each campaign, free, no strings; we will never publish a target list.

What we still don't know — on purpose, out loud

16 races carry registrant counts we could not confirm (flagged, not guessed). Competitiveness ratings wait for nomination close. 153 candidate websites appeared after our capture window and are not yet analyzed; 143 more were assessed dead or parked on dated browser-check notes. Per-race 2022 turnout exists for 1,099 races and is not yet compiled into a provincial layer. Toronto and the rest of the province were both re-scored fresh this edition under the identical v2.1 instrument, on the same day — the prior cross-cohort caveat is resolved; the cross-edition calibration against the older instrument is published on the baseline page. And the whole field moves until August 21 — which is why every number on this page carries its date.

The leverage math

Everything on this page shrinks the crowd it takes to matter. 316 races where one filing creates the contest. 956 races where a second candidate creates a choice. 854 races where the first published platform would be the only thing to read. A candidate who writes down a costed, dated plan instantly joins the top ~1% of the province on published substance. Municipal turnout math means a festival-sized crowd decides most of these races — and in a village of 900, a living-room-sized one. The door fee is $100 and an afternoon, until August 21 at 2:00 p.m. Thinking of running? Start here — or join the conversation where your town's future gets written down.

Receipts — where every number comes from

Every figure on this page traces to one of these sources, each checkable without asking our permission:

  • The official candidate lists of all 444 Ontario municipal clerk's offices, gathered and re-gathered through August 10, 2026 (this weekend edition), against the Province of Ontario's own municipal and school-board datasets (414/414 directly-electing municipalities covered). Registrant counts, filing status, and candidate-submitted channels come from these lists; counts re-stamp at nomination close, August 21, 2:00 p.m.
  • Our per-candidate research record — every one of 4,743 researched candidates carries either a verified website or a dated search record; every claimed site was captured and archived; misattributions found by any pass are corrected on the record with dated notes. Method: how the research is made and how we verify.
  • The Ontario Platform Baseline — the versioned scoring instrument, the aggregate results, and the blind double-rating agreement statistics are published with the baseline page. Per-candidate assessments are never published; aggregates only, by standing rule.
  • The Better Regional Governance Act, 2026 (Bill 100) and the affected regions' own 2026 election pages (appointed-chairs finding, verified at source August 4).
  • 2022 official election results held per-race where municipalities publish them (turnout and margin baselines).

No aggregate on this page is attached to a group so small it would describe one or two identifiable people. Ontario's 2026 field will keep moving until August 21 — this page says so wherever it matters.
