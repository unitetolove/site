State of the Election — Ontario 2026

The province-wide companion to the Toronto edition, from the standing election record. First Ontario edition August 7, 2026, followed by the August 10 weekend edition; this is the August 17 edition, field truth as of August 17, 2026 — built on a completed collection: all 444 municipal clerk candidate lists gathered, and 5,371 registered candidates on file, 5,320 of them individually researched to a dated disposition (the remaining 51 are list-only, closing at nomination day). The receipts are at the bottom, and past editions are listed just above them. Companion analysis: the Ontario Platform Baseline.

Field truth as of August 17, 2026: every figure below is a snapshot computed from the official municipal clerk candidate lists and our own per-candidate verification record. Four days remain: nominations close Friday, August 21, 2026, at 2:00 p.m. everywhere in Ontario; certified lists post Monday, August 24, at 4:00 p.m. The field is still moving, and every number here will be recomputed and re-stamped after close.

The whole story in one paragraph

Ontario is about to hold roughly 2,300 municipal elections at once — 2,305 races across 415 municipalities and school-board seats, covering all 414 directly-electing municipalities. With four days left to file, 1,759 of 3,396 seats (51.8%) face no contest of any kind — 1,398 seats where the only people who filed will win by acclamation, plus 361 seats in the 260 races where nobody filed at all (those go vacant and are filled by council appointment, not by any vote). The field grew again this week — real nomination-week movement — but the quieter problem did not move with it: of the 5,371 people asking for public office, only 27.1% have a campaign website and 19.3% have anything resembling a platform page, and once you leave the big cities both numbers collapse, down to 3.1% website coverage in the under-5,000 municipalities and 7.2% across Northern Ontario. In 818 of the 900 fully-researched acclamation-track races — 90.9% — no registrant has published a platform anywhere we could find: 1,233 seats, including 153 mayoralties, going to people taking office without a contest and without a written plan. It is not only the quiet races: in 589 of 1,062 contested races (55.5%), not one candidate has a platform on record. None of this is an accusation; it is a system running on defaults. The door is open for four more days: papers cost as little as $100 ($200 for head of council) and an afternoon, and in hundreds of these races one filing changes everything.

2,305 races, four days to close

  • 2,305 races across every directly-electing municipality in Ontario (414 of 414, checked against the province's own lists): 1,071 councillor · 619 school trustee · 414 mayor · 127 deputy mayor · 67 regional councillor · 7 county councillor. 5,371 candidates on file.
  • 260 races have zero registered candidates — 158 of them school-trustee seats, 67 councillor, 29 mayoralties, 6 other. 921 races are on acclamation track (candidates ≤ seats), and 176 of those were acclaimed in 2022 too — the chronic no-choice class, unchanged in four years.
  • The gradient is size, not virtue: in municipalities under 5,000 people, 20.9% of races have nobody registered and 49.7% are on the acclamation track; above 100,000 people, it's 0.2% and 26.6%. No party recruits here, no war room staffs these races — and the smaller the place, the emptier the ballot.

Why this matters to you: if you live outside a big city, the odds are real that some line on your October ballot has no candidates, or exactly one. The filing window closes August 21 at 2 p.m. — four days away. In a zero-candidate race, one afternoon of paperwork makes you the contest — here is everything you need.

And one class of office is off the ballot entirely, by law: the regional chairs of Durham, Halton, Niagara, Peel, Waterloo and York, the District Chair of Muskoka, and the warden of Simcoe County, are provincially appointed under the Better Regional Governance Act, 2026 — the Act itself deems any nomination already filed for those offices withdrawn, verified against the enacted text and the regions' own election pages. Roughly 5.4 million Ontarians live in those eight jurisdictions (2021 census populations, summed for the areas each body governs) and will not vote for the office that chairs the government spending their regional tax dollars. That is a fact about the system worth knowing on its own.

Why this matters to you: an acclamation is often a good person doing a thankless job unopposed. But 1,398 seats on autopilot — 41.2% of every seat up for election — is a pattern, and in 176 races there was no contest in 2022 either. Contested government is a habit a place keeps or loses; keeping it takes exactly one more candidate per race.

By municipality size the same line runs 3.1% (under 5k) → 11.9% (5–25k) → 34.7% (25–100k) → 51.9% (100k+) website coverage. The least-findable offices: deputy mayors (14.7% with a website) and school trustees (16.1%).

One correction to a comfortable assumption: incumbents are not the ones publishing. Of 309 candidates on file flagged as incumbents, 31.1% have a website and 19.1% have a platform page; among the 1,440 flagged as non-incumbents (challengers), both rates are higher — 39.6% website, 32.1% platform. The people who already hold the seat are less likely to make a written case for keeping it.

The darkest, cleanest number on the map — acclamation × silence: of 900 fully-researched acclamation-track races, 818 (90.9%) have no registrant with a published platform; 769 have no registrant with even a website. 1,233 seats — including 153 mayoralties. And in contested races, 589 of 1,062 fully-researched (55.5%) offer a real choice with nothing to read on any side.

Fairness, stated plainly: absence of a published platform is a fact about the public record, not about a candidate's effort, character, or worth. Many of these candidates are door-knocking daily in places where that works. The claim is only that a voter who goes looking can't read anything — and that this is fixable in an afternoon.

Why this matters to you: in most of Ontario, "look up the candidates" is not currently something a voter can do. Candidates: the free kit takes you from nothing to a published, costed, dated platform in a weekend — and instantly puts you in the province's top few percent on published substance.

The platform layer, measured

We didn't stop at "has a page." The Ontario Platform Baseline reads every published platform in the province under a public, viewpoint-neutral instrument. Of Ontario's 5,371 candidates, only 546 have an assessable platform text — at least 50 words of real policy content, after excluding navigation, legal boilerplate, poisoned captures and misattributed sites. That's roughly one candidate in ten. The assessable corpus runs 2,974,657 words, median 1,412 words per candidate, p90 7,080. A further 720 candidates have a website with zero platform words on it — the brochure class: a photo, a biography, a donate button, and no stated plan. See the full baseline for the class-by-class breakdown.

  • The school-board blind spot holds: trustees are among the least likely to publish anything — 16.1% have a campaign website and 10.5% a platform page, both the lowest of any office — for the seats spending the most per unit of public attention.

And the good news, praise-only and linked: the exceptional platforms — the candidates across Ontario who wrote down the province's most concrete published plans, each named with a verbatim policy commitment from their own platform. The bar is passable; they prove it.

The issue map — what the platforms that exist talk about

Among the 546 candidates with real platform text: housing appears in 83.5%, transit 76.4%, health and social services 76.0%, arts and recreation 75.5%, taxes and spending 75.1%, governance and transparency 74.9%, economy and jobs 74.7%, roads and infrastructure 74.0%, safety and policing 70.2%, growth and planning 67.1%. The bottom of the table is the story: climate and environment appear in just over half (51.3%), schools and education in 43.6% — and equity/inclusion (24.4%) and rural and agricultural issues (23.3%) are the things almost nobody names, the last one remarkable in a province where 78% of municipalities have fewer than 25,000 people and 42% have fewer than 5,000.

Copy-paste democracy? We checked.

We compared all 546 assessable platform texts against each other for borrowed language (8-word shingles, Jaccard ≥0.30 or ≥40 shared shingles). After separating out website-builder boilerplate (privacy policies and cookie banners — which, yes, a good share of the province's campaign sites share), the finding is: zero cases of platform-plank plagiarism copied between unrelated campaigns. Twelve mechanical clusters surfaced, spanning 36 candidates and 22 municipalities — every one of them turned out to be site-builder legal text, CMS widget artifacts, shared municipal procedural language, or a single vendor's GOTV/lawn-sign template, never a shared policy plank. Ontario's problem is not plagiarized platforms; it is that there is almost nothing to copy. The same sweep caught something better than a scandal: four misattributed-website registry errors plus two poisoned captures (parked/login pages mistaken for platform text) — all found and corrected in our registry before they could contaminate a single published number. That is what verification is for.

One service finding from our passive site-hygiene scan, published in aggregate only: 47% of 998 checked campaign sites have no DMARC email protection — meaning anyone can send email that appears to come from those campaigns. Individual findings go privately to each campaign, free, no strings; we will never publish a target list.

What we still don't know — on purpose, out loud

Every count on this page is provisional until Friday, August 21 at 2:00 p.m. — four days out. Certified lists post Monday, August 24 at 4:00 p.m.; voting day is Monday, October 26, 2026. 12 races cannot be classified as contested or acclamation-track because we could not establish how many seats they elect — held UNKNOWN, never folded into either bucket. 51 candidates are list-only — a name from a clerk's roster and nothing else yet, closing at registrar-final. 128 candidate websites arrived after this edition's capture window and are not yet analyzed; 70 more were assessed dead or parked, on dated browser-check notes. A number of candidate websites are built on JavaScript frameworks a plain fetcher cannot read; recorded as unread, never as "no platform" — which means our published coverage rate is a floor, not a ceiling. A handful of municipalities publish their candidate list as a scanned image with no text layer, including a few new postings this week we could not yet read. Toronto's own count reconciles cleanly against its dedicated receipt: 256 candidate rows researched, 157 with a website (up from 141), 123 with a platform (up from 103) — the movement is filings and administrative changes since, not a re-score. We have not scored platform quality in this edition; everything above measures whether a readable record exists, not whether it is any good. A small number of races carry a board-geography mismatch in our source data — flagged, not silently corrected. And the whole field moves until August 21 — which is why every number on this page carries its date.

The leverage math

Everything on this page shrinks the crowd it takes to matter. 260 races where one filing creates the contest. 921 races where a second candidate creates a choice. 1,233 seats where the first published platform would be the only thing to read. A candidate who writes down a costed, dated plan instantly joins the top ~1% of the province on published substance. Municipal turnout math means a festival-sized crowd decides most of these races — and in a village of 900, a living-room-sized one. Nomination papers cost $100, or $200 for head of council, and an afternoon. Council candidates also need 25 endorsement signatures — but only in municipalities with 4,000 or more electors; smaller municipalities and every school-trustee candidacy are exempt. The door is a form and a fee, until August 21 at 2:00 p.m. Thinking of running? Start here — or join the conversation where your town's future gets written down.

Receipts — where every number comes from

Every figure on this page traces to one of these sources, each checkable without asking our permission:

  • The official candidate lists of all 444 Ontario municipal clerk's offices, gathered and re-gathered through August 17, 2026 (this edition), against the Province of Ontario's own municipal and school-board datasets (414/414 directly-electing municipalities covered). Registrant counts, filing status, and candidate-submitted channels come from these lists; counts re-stamp at nomination close, August 21, 2:00 p.m.
  • Our per-candidate research record — every one of 5,320 researched candidates carries either a verified website or a dated search record; every claimed site was captured and archived; misattributions found by any pass are corrected on the record with dated notes. Method: how the research is made and how we verify.
  • The Ontario Platform Baseline — the versioned scoring instrument, the aggregate results, and the blind double-rating agreement statistics are published with the baseline page. Per-candidate assessments are never published; aggregates only, by standing rule.
  • The Better Regional Governance Act, 2026 (Bill 100) and the affected regions' own 2026 election pages (appointed-heads-of-council finding, verified at source August 4; the population figure was independently checked and corrected for this edition — see the fact-check record, named in Past editions below).
  • 2022 official election results held per-race where municipalities publish them (turnout and margin baselines).

No aggregate on this page is attached to a group so small it would describe one or two identifiable people. Ontario's 2026 field will keep moving until August 21 — this page says so wherever it matters.
