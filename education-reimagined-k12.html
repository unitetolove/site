Scope

This leaf's neutral scope question: what is the current state of K-12 education governance and policy in Toronto/Ontario, and — separately — what does the inherited briefing's visionary case for AI-powered mastery learning, holistic/experiential development, gamified skill-trees, and teachers-as-coaches actually rest on, evidentially? This document covers: the current (2025-2026) governance status of the Toronto District School Board (TDSB), including provincial supervision; Ontario Ministry of Education guidance on AI use in classrooms; the evidentiary status of Alpha School and AI-tutoring claims generally, including new 2025-2026 independent reporting; and the specific, narrow municipal levers Toronto actually holds (community use of schools, City-funded after-school/out-of-school-time programming, advocacy). It explicitly does not cover, and hands off: adult/lifelong learning and the "learning city" agenda to most-educated-population-learning-city; general AI-driven labour displacement and the future of work to the dark-talent/youth-opportunity leaves; and general municipal-education funding-formula mechanics beyond what is needed to explain the TDSB's current governance status, which belongs more fully to a dedicated provincial-education-finance leaf if and when one exists (not confirmed to exist in this pass; not asserted here).

Current state

TDSB governance status, 2025-2026: under active provincial supervision — the single most important fact this pass verified

As of this pass's direct, live fetch of the Ontario government's own page (updated December 1, 2025; published June 27, 2025), the Toronto District School Board is currently under provincial supervision, not merely subject to a threatened or proposed intervention [NEW-2026-1]. This is a documented current fact, not a hypothetical. The sequence, as stated directly on the Ministry's own page: the Auditor General of Ontario issued an extensive report on the TDSB's school safety, financial management, and capital planning in December 2024, echoing concerns the Ministry says it had already raised with the board over several years, including a letter the year before "outlining the board's inability to meet its financial responsibilities under the Education Act" [NEW-2026-1]. In May 2025, the Ministry appointed PricewaterhouseCoopers LLP (PwC) as financial investigator, with a mandate to validate the board's financial position, assess compliance with the Broader Public Sector Executive Compensation Act, 2014, and recommend whether "control and charge of the board should be vested in the ministry" [NEW-2026-1]. The investigator's findings, per the Ministry's own summary: the TDSB relied on Proceeds of Disposition exemptions (i.e., property-sale proceeds) to balance its budget and would probably not have achieved a balanced budget without them; between 2021-2022 and 2024-2025, board management presented $24.8 million in cost-savings measures that trustees did not approve, and even full approval would not have been sufficient to eliminate the in-year deficits [NEW-2026-1]. Based on the investigator's recommendation, the Ministry appointed a supervisor "to take over the governance and decision-making functions of the TDSB, including financial management, policy implementation and operational oversight" [NEW-2026-1].

The supervisor's mandate, per the same Ministry page, is to review operational efficiencies, develop and implement a plan to return the board to balanced finances, "enhance financial oversight and governance," and report progress to the Ministry [NEW-2026-1]. Independent reporting corroborates and adds operational detail this pass's WebSearch discovery could not verify by direct fetch (both attempted direct fetches of Toronto-focused outlets returned no readable content, likely JS-rendered — flagged as WebSearch-discovered, not independently fetched, below): the supervisor was announced June 27, 2025, by the Minister of Education; while supervision is in effect, TDSB board meetings do not take place and committee participation is suspended; trustees are "sidelined — no pay, no board meetings and no input on policies that impact students"; a fall 2026 trustee election is still expected to proceed despite the suspension; and — a detail with direct bearing on this leaf's own evidentiary-honesty discipline — the financial investigator's report itself "found no evidence of financial mismanagement" even as it recommended supervision on deficit-trajectory grounds [NEW-2026-2]. As of April 2026, reporting places TDSB among a total of eight Ontario school boards under provincial supervision [NEW-2026-3]. Independently re-verified 2026-07-16: the "eight boards" count is now independently confirmed via direct fetch of CP24's April 2026 reporting ("Eight school boards, including the TDSB, were previously placed under provincial supervision and their elected trustees were suspended") — CBC's companion article remains JS-gated and UNREACHABLE, consistent with the wave-1 finding that CBC pages are systematically JS-gated. The separate "~1,640 schools / one-third of all schools in the province" figure, however, is not supported by the reachable CP24 source — that specific number does not appear in the fetched article. This document downgrades that one figure to ⚠️ VERIFY (unconfirmed) while leaving the "eight boards" count as confirmed; see the quote-context block and "What the evidence does and doesn't support" below. This pass's own financial-investigation findings above corroborate, with current documented detail, the inherited briefing's own framing of TDSB's position in its "Costs & financing" section: that the K-12 system generally, and TDSB specifically, faces "structural deficits and a funding squeeze," such that "the system is cutting music and pools, not investing" [inherited from the master briefing] — a framing this pass's independently-verified $24.8 million in unapproved cost-savings measures and reliance on property-sale-proceeds exemptions [NEW-2026-1] gives concrete, current-dated substance to, rather than merely repeating from the inherited source.

This is a decisive framing correction relative to how a reader might otherwise assume TDSB governance currently operates. Any current-state discussion of "what the City could ask the TDSB to do" or "what Toronto's school trustees have decided" must account for the fact that, as of this writing, TDSB's elected trustees have no governing authority — decision-making sits with a Ministry-appointed supervisor. This document uses institutional framing throughout per its own Addressee Discipline guardrail; where a WebSearch-discovered source names the supervisor individually, this document does not repeat that name, describing the role institutionally as "the Ministry-appointed supervisor" instead, consistent with this project's standing rule against naming government officials by name in its own prose.

A further, broader governance change: proposed legislation reshaping school board structure province-wide (April 2026)

Separately from the TDSB-specific supervision above, Ontario introduced Bill 101, described in coverage as the "Putting Student Achievement First Act," at Queen's Park on April 13, 2026 [NEW-2026-4]. As reported, the bill would restructure the current Director of Education role into two positions — a Chief Executive Officer (CEO), required to have a business background, who would lead budget development and refer unresolved spending disputes to the Minister for final decision, and a separate Chief Education Officer focused on pedagogical responsibilities, required to hold Ontario College of Teachers membership or equivalent [NEW-2026-4]. The bill would also set a province-wide range of five to twelve elected trustees per board (down from TDSB's current, larger complement — reported elsewhere as cutting TDSB's trustee count "nearly in half" [NEW-2026-3]), cap trustee honorariums at $10,000, restrict trustee expense and conference spending, and — per one legal-industry summary of the bill's Schedule 5 — replace the Ontario Public School Boards' Association and the Ontario Catholic School Trustees' Association with the Council of Ontario Directors of Education as the central employer bargaining agent, shifting the employer side of provincial teacher bargaining from trustee-led associations to an agency led by professional board staff [NEW-2026-4]. As of this pass, Bill 101 is reported as introduced/tabled, not confirmed passed into force — this document states it as proposed legislation, not enacted law, and flags its final form and passage status as an open question below.

Ministry of Education guidance on AI in the classroom

Ontario's curriculum has been updated to include mandatory content on digital literacy, critical thinking, "AI awareness," and responsible technology use across multiple subjects, differentiated by grade band: in Grades 1 through 8, guidance centres on using digital tools "safely and responsibly"; in Grades 9 through 12, AI-specific content appears in subjects such as Business Studies (using AI tools for business-related tasks) and Geography (assessing AI-produced content) [NEW-2026-5]. The Ministry states it is "working with partners across the system to ensure students benefit from these tools in responsible, age appropriate ways" [NEW-2026-5]. This is Ministry-level curricular guidance, not a province-wide operational AI-tutoring program — this document does not conflate the two. Individual school boards have separately developed their own AI-use guidance documents (several updated as recently as August 2025), and the Ontario Teachers' Federation and its affiliates published a discussion paper on generative AI in K-12 Ontario education in March 2026, indicating the issue remains under active, unresolved discussion across the sector rather than settled by uniform province-wide policy [NEW-2026-5]. Existing Ministry assessment guidance (the "Growing Success" framework) continues to govern fairness, transparency, and evidence-triangulation in student assessment generally, and was not reported as having been specifically rewritten for AI in this pass's search [NEW-2026-5]. This pass did not locate a single, binding, province-wide Ministry directive specifically governing AI-tutoring products (as distinct from curricular AI-literacy content or individual board guidance) — stated as a gap rather than assumed not to exist.

Toronto's actual municipal levers: community use of school space and City-funded after-school programming

Consistent with the jurisdictional discipline this leaf is scoped around, Toronto's City government does not set K-12 curriculum, does not govern teacher staffing or certification, and — as of this pass — does not even sit across the table from an elected TDSB board, since TDSB trustees currently hold no governing authority [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2]. What the City does hold, confirmed in this pass: a long-standing, provincially-funded Community Use of Schools program providing reduced or free access to school space for community-strengthening programs, run through TDSB's own community-use administration [NEW-2026-6]; decades-long shared-use agreements between the City's Parks, Forestry and Recreation division and both the TDSB and the Toronto Catholic District School Board, covering shared community centres and school facilities [NEW-2026-6]; and City-run before/after-school programming — the After-School Recreation Care (ARC) program for ages 6-12 and the Community Leadership After-School Program (CLASP) for ages 10-15, operated in schools and community locations on school days and some professional-activity days [NEW-2026-7]. This pass also found a specific, recent example of the City-TDSB shared-space model in practice: at Davisville Public School, the (arm's-length) Toronto Lands Corporation negotiated a long-term lease and shared-use agreement allowing the City to build a community aquatic centre on board land, with students using the new pools during four school-day hours and the City accessing the school's gymnasium for evening/weekend recreational programming [NEW-2026-6]. Separately, City of Toronto 2026 budget documents are reported to have expanded City-supported school food programs to an additional 155 schools and roughly 62,000 students, alongside a $1.4-million investment in bulk-purchasing infrastructure for school food programs [NEW-2026-7]. This pass did not verify the school-food-program figures by direct primary-source fetch of the City's own budget documents; flagged ⚠️ VERIFY below. None of these levers — community use of space, after-school programming, or school food support — reach curriculum, staffing, or the AI-tutoring/mastery-learning model this leaf's inherited briefing envisions; this document does not present them as such. The inherited briefing's own "Toronto-specific factors" section names four civic assets it argues the City brings to any champion/pilot/convener role it can play within these narrow levers: a "world-leading AI ecosystem" it suggests could build ethical educational AI; the City's "extraordinary diversity," which it argues makes skill-tree/passion-path models suited to honouring every culture and talent; a strong library and community-learning infrastructure; and a "reform-minded, ambitious civic culture" [inherited from the master briefing]. The same section states the educator-partnership imperative is "just as acute" in Toronto specifically because the city's teachers and their unions are heavily unionized, and must be "partners in, not targets of," any reimagining [inherited from the master briefing]. This pass's live discovery did not independently re-verify any of these four asset claims or the unionization framing; they are carried forward as INHERITED.

The inherited briefing's visionary proposal, clearly labeled as such

This leaf's inherited spine document [inherited from the master briefing] is explicitly self-described as "a deliberately visionary briefing — a reimagining of K-12 for the AI and post-jobs age," synthesizing AI-powered mastery learning (tested without AI), Waldorf-style holistic development, Green School-style experiential/project-based learning, gamified "skill-tree" progression, and a "teacher-as-coach" reframing, alongside a "village" model of community co-raising [inherited from the master briefing]. It is not, and does not claim to be, a report of enacted Toronto or Ontario policy — it is a synthesis proposal for the Regenerative Toronto Assembly's own deliberation, built on Bloom's 1984 "2 Sigma" finding (1:1 tutoring plus mastery learning associated with roughly a 2-standard-deviation achievement gain) [inherited from the master briefing] and on Alpha School's self-reported results as an early proof-of-concept [inherited from the master briefing], a school the inherited briefing itself notes is a private, expensive (~$40-75K/year) [inherited from the master briefing] ⚠️ VERIFY, selective setting. The inherited briefing's own "Sources to verify" section already flags Alpha School's claims as needing to be "presented as self-reported, private-school, unverified, and contested by independent experts... not as established results" [inherited from the master briefing] — this backgrounder treats that hedge as binding and does not soften it anywhere below; see "Key tensions / tradeoffs" and "What the evidence does and doesn't support."

Each of the inherited synthesis's component pillars, named in the paragraph above, rests on its own distinct evidentiary basis per the source document, restated here rather than left as bare labels [inherited from the master briefing]. Waldorf (Steiner) education is holistic, developmentally-paced education emphasizing imagination, arts, hands-on crafts, nature, story, and — notably — delayed/limited screens, offered by the inherited briefing as the "human, whole-child counterweight to techno-solutionism" [inherited from the master briefing]. Green School (Bali) is named as the model for experiential, real-world, sustainability-centred, project- and place-based learning — the "liberated time" component once AI-tutoring compresses core-academic time [inherited from the master briefing]. The gamified "skill-tree" model proposes structuring learning like a role-playing game — vast, branching trees of academic and practical skills, talents, crafts, and hobbies (coding and carpentry, music and mathematics, named explicitly) that a child follows by passion and mastery, harnessing intrinsic motivation and autonomy; the inherited briefing traces this reframe's lineage partly to Montessori's own self-directed, mixed-age, mastery-based roots [inherited from the master briefing]. None of these three components were independently re-verified or refreshed by this pass's live discovery — they are carried forward as INHERITED, per this leaf's binding rule against re-researching inherited material, not as independently confirmed current fact.

The "teacher-as-coach" reframing — this document's own title concept — is the inherited briefing's proposal to redefine the teacher from content-deliverer/lecturer to coach, mentor, facilitator, connector, and motivator: the relational, human work the briefing argues AI cannot fill, freeing teachers for what it calls "higher-value, more rewarding, and more human" work than lecturing a class through a fixed script [inherited from the master briefing]. Bundled with this is a practitioner-mentor model: broadening who teaches and when, by drawing teachers from people who have "gone out, lived, and mastered things in the world" — the carpenter, the entrepreneur, the scientist, the artist, the elder — who return to teach and mentor later in life, bringing real-world experience the briefing argues a straight-from-school entrant, however skilled, often cannot [inherited from the master briefing]. The briefing itself cautions this is additive, not substitutive: broadening who teaches should mean "valuing life experience," not "devaluing teaching expertise," since pedagogical skill and child-development craft are real and learned [inherited from the master briefing]. Finally, the inherited vision pairs both of the above with a return to "it takes a village" — families, elders, and community co-raising and co-educating children rather than outsourcing that work wholly to an institution — while explicitly warning that this "can't mean offloading onto unpaid (often women's) labour or abandoning professional support": community involvement is meant to complement, not replace, properly-resourced, professional schooling [inherited from the master briefing]. None of these three components (teacher-as-coach, practitioner-mentor, village) were independently re-verified by this pass's live discovery; they are carried forward as INHERITED.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

The inherited vision assumes a governance and reform-partnership context that the current, documented TDSB situation complicates rather than confirms. The inherited briefing's own "What determines success vs failure" section states that "genuine partnership with educators" — co-designed, not imposed — is "the make-or-break issue" [inherited from the master briefing]. Toronto's largest school board is, as a matter of current documented fact, governed by a Ministry-appointed supervisor rather than elected trustees, with trustees currently holding no board-meeting or policy-input role at all [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2]. This document does not assert that provincial supervision itself constitutes reform "imposed on" educators in the sense the inherited briefing warns against — supervision was triggered by a financial-deficit trajectory, not by an AI-education mandate, and the financial investigator explicitly found no evidence of mismanagement even while recommending supervision [NEW-2026-2]. But it is a documented tension worth naming plainly: any near-term proposal to reshape TDSB's pedagogical model would currently be negotiated with, or decided by, a Ministry-appointed supervisor and Ministry officials rather than an elected board answerable to trustees and, through them, to families — a materially different governance context than the inherited briefing's "co-design with educators and their elected/union representatives" framing presumes. A directly related caution from the inherited briefing's own "Costs & financing" section belongs alongside this governance tension rather than left silent: the briefing argues AI-scaled mastery learning could be more cost-effective than universal 1:1 human tutoring, but only paired with real investment — ethical, privacy-protecting educational AI, universal devices and connectivity, and retraining teachers into coaching roles — and states plainly that the vision "must never be sold as a cost-cutting exercise" (AI replacing teachers to save money), since doing so "would gut the model and betray the profession" [inherited from the master briefing]. [Restored 2026-07-16 (R3 G4 adversary pass) — this sentence was claimed as restored in reviews/coverage/education-reimagined-k12.md's "Costs & financing" block but was not actually present in this section; added here to close the gap.]

The inherited briefing's central evidentiary anchor (Alpha School) has grown more, not less, contested since the spine document was written. The inherited document already flagged Alpha as unverified and contested [inherited from the master briefing]; this pass's live discovery found that contestation has deepened through 2025-2026, not resolved. Independent investigative reporting (WIRED, October 2025, and 404 Media, February 2026, per WebSearch-discovered secondary summaries) reportedly documented that Alpha's "academic coaches" were largely remote contract workers based in the Philippines and Colombia rather than credentialed teachers, that home webcam monitoring was enabled by default, and that internally-flagged AI-generated lesson plans were assessed as sometimes doing "more harm than good" [NEW-2026-8]. A July 2026 academic commentary piece states plainly that research "doesn't show that AI tutors are better than human teachers," and that a 2026 Stanford review of more than 800 academic papers found AI tutoring's benefits weaken specifically when students are later asked to perform without AI support — the opposite of durable, internalized mastery [NEW-2026-9]. The same source reports that hybrid models (AI assisting human tutors, rather than AI replacing them) show more encouraging preliminary evidence, citing a 2025 Stanford study finding AI-assisted human tutors improved topic mastery by four percentage points over human tutors working alone [NEW-2026-9] — a materially different and more modest claim than Alpha's own self-reported ~2.3x-growth figures [inherited from the master briefing]. This tension is not manufactured for balance: it is the same "learn with AI, test without AI" caution the inherited briefing itself insists on [inherited from the master briefing], now independently corroborated by research specifically finding that AI-assisted gains erode under AI-free testing conditions. The inherited briefing's own catalogue of AI-in-education risks — beyond the learn/test-without-AI mitigation specifically — names over-reliance on technology, screen-time and developmental harms (especially for young children), loss of human relationship, surveillance and data exploitation (children's data as especially sensitive), algorithmic narrowing, and de-skilling of both students and teachers [inherited from the master briefing]; this pass's live discovery did not independently re-verify or refresh this risk catalogue, which is carried forward as INHERITED rather than re-argued here.

The practitioner-mentor and "village" proposals carry their own named cautions, worth stating alongside the governance tension above rather than only as unqualified additions. The inherited briefing pairs its practitioner-mentor idea with an explicit caveat that broadening who teaches must mean valuing life experience alongside, not instead of, real pedagogical and child-development expertise [inherited from the master briefing], and pairs its "village" model with an explicit warning against it becoming a vehicle for offloading care work onto unpaid — often women's — labour, or a substitute for properly-resourced, professional schooling [inherited from the master briefing]. This pass did not independently test either caution against current Toronto/Ontario conditions; both are carried forward as INHERITED framing.

A related open tension the inherited briefing names but does not resolve: how a personalized, skill-tree-structured model connects back to recognized credentials and pathways. The briefing itself poses this as a live, unresolved design question — a radically personalized model "must still connect to recognized credentials and pathways... or it disadvantages kids in a credential-gated world" [inherited from the master briefing] — and this pass's own live-discovery findings (TDSB supervision, Bill 101) do not touch credentialing or pathways mechanics at all, so this remains a genuine, unresolved gap rather than one this pass can close; see "Open questions / data gaps" below.

Municipal-authority tension: an AI-and-classroom-reform vision meets a City with only narrow, adjacent levers. The inherited briefing already names this tension and does not overstate City authority [inherited from the master briefing]. This pass's confirmation reinforces rather than revises that limit: even the City's own established relationship with school boards operates through shared-use agreements and community-use permitting administered by the boards themselves [NEW-2026-6], not through any City role in curriculum or pedagogical model choice. Where the City does invest directly and substantially — after-school programming, school food support [NEW-2026-7] — those investments sit adjacent to, not inside, the classroom-model question the inherited briefing's vision is actually about.

What the evidence does and doesn't support

Well-supported:

  • TDSB is currently under provincial supervision, with a Ministry-appointed supervisor holding governance, financial-management, and operational-oversight authority in place of elected trustees, per direct fetch of the Ontario government's own published page [NEW-2026-1].
  • The financial investigation preceding supervision found reliance on property-sale-proceeds exemptions to balance the budget and unapproved/insufficient cost-savings measures over multiple years, while explicitly not finding evidence of financial mismanagement [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2].
  • Ontario's curriculum includes AI-literacy and digital-literacy content differentiated by grade band, and the Ministry states an intent to work with system partners on responsible AI use — this is Ministry curricular guidance, distinct from any binding province-wide AI-tutoring policy [NEW-2026-5].
  • Toronto's actual municipal levers touching K-12 education are community/shared use of school space (administered through board-City agreements) and City-funded before/after-school and school-food programming — confirmed through multiple independent sources describing the same program structures [NEW-2026-6, NEW-2026-7].
  • Bloom's 1984 "2 Sigma" finding on 1:1 tutoring and mastery learning is a real, long-standing, and frequently cited education-research result, as the inherited briefing states [inherited from the master briefing] — this document does not re-verify Bloom's original study directly but notes it is widely treated as foundational in the education-research literature this pass's discovery touched (e.g., the AI-tutoring-efficacy discussion cited at [NEW-2026-9]).
  • Independent 2025-2026 reporting and a 2026 academic literature review both converge on the same directional finding: AI tutoring shows real but bounded promise, particularly in hybrid (AI-assisting-humans) models, and does not show clear superiority over skilled human tutoring generally [NEW-2026-9]. NEEDS-JUDGMENT flag, independently re-verified 2026-07-16: the directional finding itself (AI tutors not clearly superior to skilled human tutors) is corroborated by this pass's own successful fetch of the cited UPI article, which does make that general point.   • The inherited briefing's equity/Matthew-effect caution is a load-bearing, section-level argument, not a footnote, and is carried forward here in full [inherited from the master briefing]: education could be "the great equalizer — or the great entrencher." A well-built AI-mastery model could be "profoundly equalizing," giving every child the personalized 1:1 tutoring only the wealthy could previously buy, with personalized/mastery learning generally associated with reduced achievement gaps [inherited from the master briefing]. Done wrong, it "widens gaps," since the radical models exist mostly in elite private settings, and advantaged children (devices, connectivity, home support) could race ahead while disadvantaged children are left with under-resourced versions or excluded by the digital divide [inherited from the master briefing]. The inherited briefing states this must be designed in from the start — universal access, the most human coaching directed at those who need it most, and culturally-responsive skill-trees — and separately names personalized, paced, adaptive learning as potentially "transformative for neurodivergent and disabled learners... if designed inclusively" [inherited from the master briefing]. This pass's own live discovery did not independently test whether Toronto's current municipal levers (Community Use of Schools, ARC/CLASP, school food programs [NEW-2026-6, NEW-2026-7]) are equity-designed in the sense this equity argument calls for — stated as a gap, not assumed either way.

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. The right to education is anchored in the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), Articles 28 and 29 — Article 28 recognizing the child's right to education (with progressive realization of free, accessible schooling), and Article 29 setting out education's purposes, including "the development of the child's personality, talents and mental and physical abilities to their fullest potential" and preparation "for responsible life in a free society" [NEW-2026-10]. This is a genuine, checkable anchor for this leaf specifically: the inherited briefing's own emphasis on holistic development, individualized pacing, and "every child, not just the rich" [inherited from the master briefing] tracks Article 29's language on developing each child's talents and abilities to their fullest potential more closely than a narrower curriculum-delivery framing would. Canada ratified the CRC in 1991; this document states that as a general, well-established fact rather than one requiring fresh verification in this pass, consistent with the treaty's widely-documented status — flagged for a future Lane V pass to confirm the ratification date against a primary UN source if this document is promoted toward publication.

2. Global comparators. Beyond Alpha School, Waldorf/Steiner education, and Green School Bali — all already covered in the inherited briefing [inherited from the master briefing] — this pass's live discovery surfaced one additional, more recent comparator worth naming distinctly: hybrid AI-assisted human tutoring, as distinct from AI-replacement models, per the 2025 Stanford study finding a measured (not dramatic) four-percentage-point topic-mastery improvement when AI assists human tutors rather than replacing them [NEW-2026-9]. This is a meaningfully different model than Alpha's approach and is evidenced by a named, more conventional academic study rather than a school's own self-reporting — the evidence is modest but independently modelled, which is a materially different evidentiary status than Alpha's figures. This pass did not identify, within its search budget, a named public (non-private-school) North American school district that has adopted a comparable hybrid AI-tutoring model at scale with independently published outcome data — stated as a gap rather than invented.

3. What Toronto/Ontario can concretely and municipally steal. Given the jurisdiction limits confirmed above, the only genuinely municipal-scale "steal" available to Toronto from these comparators is infrastructure and access design, not pedagogy: the City's own Community Use of Schools and shared-use-agreement model [NEW-2026-6] is itself a transferable mechanism other municipalities could study, and — in the other direction — Toronto's after-school programming (ARC, CLASP) [NEW-2026-7] is a plausible, already-municipal venue in which a hybrid, non-replacement AI-tutoring pilot (of the kind the Stanford study evaluated) could someday be tested in an out-of-school-time setting without requiring provincial curriculum authority — a possibility this document notes as a description of what the mechanism could support, not a recommendation (recommendations are quarantined to the accompanying cards, per this template's own discipline).

NEW-2026-2 — Supervision operational details; no finding of mismanagement.

On June 27, the Education Minister announced the appointment of a supervisor to oversee the TDSB for the 2025-2026 school year. While the supervisor is installed, TDSB board meetings will not take place and committee participation has been suspended; trustees are sidelined — no pay, no board meetings and no input on policies that impact students. The move came after the provincial government appointed an investigator in spring 2025 to assess the TDSB's finances; "the audit found no evidence of financial mismanagement," but the Minister said a supervisor was nonetheless necessary.

NEW-2026-4 — Bill 101, "Putting Student Achievement First Act," April 2026.

The changes are being instituted as part of what the province is calling the "Putting Student Achievement First Act," introduced at Queen's Park on April 13, 2026. The current Director of Education role will be restructured into two roles: a Chief Executive Officer, required to have a business background, who would lead budget development and refer unresolved spending measures to the Minister for final decision; and a Chief Education Officer focused on student achievement and pedagogical responsibilities, required to be a member of the Ontario College of Teachers or hold equivalent qualifications. The number of elected trustees on a school board will be determined by regulation and restricted to a minimum of five and a maximum of twelve. Schedule 5 of Bill 101 proposes designating the Council of Ontario Directors of Education as the central employer bargaining agency, replacing both the Ontario Public School Boards' Association and the Ontario Catholic School Trustees' Association.

NEW-2026-5 — Ministry AI-in-curriculum guidance.

Ontario has updated curriculum to include new mandatory learning on digital literacy, critical thinking, AI awareness and responsible use of technology throughout many subjects, differentiated by grade level: in Grades 1 through 8, students may learn how to use digital tools safely and responsibly; in Grades 9 through 12, AI could be included in subjects such as Business Studies or Geography. The province is "working with partners across the system to ensure students benefit from these tools in responsible, age appropriate ways." Individual district school boards have developed their own responsible-AI-use guidance, several updated as recently as August 2025; the Ontario Teachers' Federation and affiliates published a discussion paper on generative AI in K-12 Ontario education in March 2026.

NEW-2026-6 — Community Use of Schools / shared-use agreements.

Parks, Forestry and Recreation, the Toronto District School Board and the Toronto Catholic District School Board have long-standing relationships spanning decades, with various arrangements established for the sharing of school board and City property to maximize the benefits of their respective assets for use by both the school population and the broader community. The Community Use of Schools project, funded by the provincial government, provides reduced or free equitable access to school space and facilities for programs that strengthen community bonds. At Davisville Public School, the Toronto Lands Corporation negotiated a long-term lease and shared-use agreement allowing the City to build a community aquatic centre on board land, with students using the new pools four hours each school day and the City accessing the school's gymnasium for evening and weekend recreational programming.

Source: City of Toronto, "Shared Use Agreements with the Toronto District School Boards," https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2021/cc/bgrd/backgroundfile-166326.pdf; TDSB, "Community Use of Schools," https://www.tdsb.on.ca/Community/Community-Use-of-Schools; Toronto Lands Corporation, "Using TDSB Space," https://torontolandscorp.com/tdsb-properties/using-tdsb-space/. NEW-2026-7 — City after-school programs and school food program expansion.

The After-School Recreation Care (ARC) Program, for ages six to 12, offers sports, outdoor play, special events, creative activities, and homework help. The Community Leadership After-School Program (CLASP), for ages 10 to 15, promotes physical literacy, health and wellness, and community involvement through leadership workshops. Programs are located in schools and community locations and generally operate five days a week for a few hours before and/or after school. The City's 2026 budget expanded school food programs to an additional 155 schools and 62,000 students, and the City will receive a $1.4-million investment to expand bulk purchasing for school food programs.

NEW-2026-8 — WIRED/404 Media Alpha School investigative allegations.

Two major investigations — WIRED in October 2025 and 404 Media in February 2026 — documented that Alpha's academic coaches were largely remote workers in the Philippines and Colombia rather than credentialed teachers, that home webcam monitoring was enabled by default, and that AI-generated lesson plans were internally flagged as sometimes doing "more harm than good." NEW-2026-9 — Academic commentary on AI tutoring efficacy (UPI/Conversation article), with a separately-sourced Stanford RCT figure.

Research shows AI and computer tutors can improve achievement and language-based interaction but are not superior to skilled human tutors; there is no clear evidence that AI or other computer-based tutors are superior to human tutors, though they might be cheaper — this directional finding is what the cited UPI/Conversation article itself supports, per a direct fetch of its text. Separately, and not carried by this same article: a Stanford SCALE Initiative/National Student Support Accelerator randomized controlled trial of "Tutor CoPilot" (more than 700 tutors and 1,000 students from underserved communities) found that students whose tutors used the AI-assisted coaching tool were 4 percentage points more likely to master math topics than students of tutors without it, with the gain rising to 9 percentage points for students of lower-rated tutors.
