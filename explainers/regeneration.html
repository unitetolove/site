The Living City — Explained Simply

v0.1, 2026-07-19 Date: 2026-07-19 ·

Status: v0.1, 2026-07-19 · RENDER — derived from this library's internal records, this library's internal records, and this library's internal records (all 2026-07-18), pilot per this library's internal records DRAFT, pre-ratification — the charter this page explains is still marked PROPOSED, not yet ratified. No ravine has been adopted, no steward recruited, and no partner organization or Indigenous nation has been contacted. This page changes none of that; it only explains it.

This is written for anyone who wants to know what Toronto's REGENERATION program actually proposes for the city's ravines, water, heat, and climate risk. It skips the full charter and the technical evidence review most people would otherwise have to read. It explains the plan, why it matters, how anyone would know if it's working, and where it could go wrong, based only on research already done for this program. Nothing below is a promise.

What it is

Some homeowners in west-end Toronto can no longer buy insurance for a flooded basement. Not because nothing was done. Their basement has already flooded too many times, and insurers won't cover it anymore. That is what a warming, wetter city can feel like in a real kitchen, not just in a report.

REGENERATION is a proposed research program built around one question. What would it take for Toronto to make its ravines, water, and climate defenses measurably healthier — not just less harmful? The plan rejects a common climate goal, net-zero, as too narrow a test. Net-zero counts carbon only. It says nothing about carbon already in the air, and nothing about whether a river, a forest, or a species is actually coming back to life. REGENERATION wants a broader kind of progress instead: one that leaves the city's water, plants, and animals better off because it exists, not merely less harmed.

The plan has two connected parts.

The first is called All-Ravines-One-Year. Toronto's ravine network is enormous: about 110 square kilometres, at least 150 separate ravines, roughly a sixth of the entire city. It is one of the largest urban ravine systems in the world. But no one has actually done the global comparison needed to call it the largest, so the plan is careful not to claim that. The idea: in year one, every ravine gets adopted by a named local volunteer group. Each one is then measured against a public baseline and listed in a public register. Visible work comes next — planting, clearing invasive species, reopening buried streams. That work happens over several years, in phases. It starts in the neighbourhoods with the least tree cover and the most heat risk.

The second part is the Living City Index, an honest public scoreboard for whether any of it is real. It tracks things a resident can check for themselves, like whether Toronto's beaches are safe to swim in. The outer Blue Flag beaches usually are. The inner harbour itself still isn't, because of old combined sewers that overflow into it.

One design choice shows how seriously the Index takes honesty. An early idea was to celebrate a rising deer population as proof the ravines were healthier. The evidence says the opposite: too many deer in a city ravine, with no natural predators, strip out young trees and undergrowth. That signals a forest in trouble, not one recovering. So deer were dropped as a celebrated number. In their place, the Index tracks real keystone species. Salmon are one: they returned to the Don River in 2025, the first sighting since 2012, after being locally extinct for over a century. Redside dace is another — an endangered fish used as an indicator of clean water. Beaver is a third, though the plan admits it hasn't actually measured Toronto's beaver population yet. That piece is a metric still to build, not a claim to make today.

Why it matters

Toronto's ravines and green spaces are not just nice to have. Some residents are already better protected from climate harm than others. REGENERATION exists because that gap is real and widening.

Heat shows the pattern clearly. Toronto's own modeling looked at one moderate warming scenario. Under it, the number of residents facing serious heat risk during a heat wave could grow from about 180,000 today to about 1.3 million by the 2050s. That's roughly seven times as many people. It's a model's projection tied to one warming path, not a guarantee. But it is concentrated in aging apartment towers and lower-income neighbourhoods with little shade. The direction is not in doubt.

Tree cover already follows the same pattern today. Wealthier neighbourhoods, many built around the ravines, often have more than 40% tree canopy. Low-income, industrial, and high-density neighbourhoods often have less than 15%. Toronto was the first Canadian city to measure this gap formally, under a program it calls tree equity.

Money is already reacting to the risk, even where city planning hasn't caught up. 2024 was the costliest year in Canadian history for severe-weather insurance losses — about $8.5 billion nationally. Some west-end Toronto homeowners, as mentioned above, can no longer buy flood insurance at all. Insurers are repricing climate risk faster than the city is adapting to it.

And net-zero genuinely isn't the whole answer. A large share of a building's carbon footprint comes from its materials. It's not just about running the building day to day. Today that "embodied" share is roughly a quarter of a typical building's total footprint. It's projected to grow to roughly half by 2050. As existing buildings get more efficient to operate, that harder-to-fix slice becomes a bigger part of the problem. A net-zero target that only counts day-to-day operating emissions misses that growing slice. It also says nothing about the carbon that's already been released into the air. That gap is the evidence-based reason the program treats net-zero as the wrong test.

How would we know it's working

The plan doesn't launch all at once. It moves through a series of checkpoints, and each one has to clear before the next starts:

  • Gate zero — ratification. The charter itself needs formal sign-off before anything below

happens. As of this writing, that hasn't happened.

  • Gate one — clean up the evidence. Every unresolved or disputed figure (see "What could go

wrong" below) gets checked or clearly labeled before the plan moves forward.

  • Gate two — write the details. Each part of the program — ravines, water, heat, energy,

materials — goes through the normal fact-checking process.

  • Gate three — outside review. The big ideas, the honest scoreboard and the anti-displacement

rule among them, get argued for and against before either one is approved to run.

  • Gate four — paper plans only. Still nothing on the ground: a detailed, year-by-year design for

adopting ravines, and for the volunteer steward program.

  • Gate five — the real-world go-ahead. Before any real resident, volunteer, or Indigenous

partner is contacted, three things have to happen. A safeguarding and consent review covers anything touching residents or volunteers. A legal review covers anything touching money. And named partners have to actually be on board. The plan calls this a two-key gate: no single person can wave a real-world action through alone.

One big design choice is still undecided. Should year one adopt and measure every ravine in the city — the plan's own recommendation? Or start with a smaller first group and expand later, the more cautious fallback? The people who wrote the plan recommend the bigger, all-at-once version. But they're honest that being adopted and measured in year one is not the same as being restored in year one. Actually restoring a forest or a river takes years no matter which option gets chosen, because living systems recover on biological time, not on a project deadline.

The plan also names, in advance, what would make the whole program a mistake. Picture a fair length of time passing. The honest scoreboard shows that none of the real signals moved. Salmon and rare fish didn't return. The tree-cover gap between rich and poor neighbourhoods didn't close. And displacement went up. Now picture that the city's existing partners were already achieving as much without this program's help. In that case, the plan says the honest conclusion is that the program added no real value. It should then be folded into other efforts, not kept running on its own.

What could go wrong

The program could displace the very people it's trying to help. This isn't hypothetical. When other cities restored parks and greenways, nearby rents jumped and the original lower-income residents got pushed out. In New York, rents near the High Line park rose about 68% in five years. That kind of displacement typically peaks about a year after a project opens. That's exactly when a fast, photogenic project would be declaring victory. This pattern is called green gentrification. The plan's answer is an anti-displacement covenant. Every project must include real protections from day one. And the scoreboard has to track who still lives in a neighbourhood, not just what's growing there. If rents or displacement rise sharply within about 18 months of a project, and the protections didn't hold, that project stops. It has to be fixed before any new one starts nearby. If it keeps happening across projects, the whole approach gets shut down and redesigned.

The honest scoreboard could quietly become dishonest. A future version could soften a bad number, drop an embarrassing measurement, or let a corporate sponsor's marketing attach itself to the program's name. That kind of thing has happened elsewhere. Roughly 90–94% of one major carbon-offset registry's forest credits turned out to be nearly worthless, according to a widely reported 2023 investigation. That registry's CEO resigned over it. This is exactly why the plan builds in a capture wall. Measurements can be added but never quietly removed. And no project may depend on carbon offsets or a sponsor's green marketing to count as a success. If that honesty rule is ever broken, the plan says the scoreboard has to be declared publicly compromised until it's fixed.

"150 ravines adopted" could become a press release with nobody actually doing the work. The plan's control: an adoption doesn't count in the public list unless a named local group is actually assigned. It also requires a real baseline measurement to be completed.

Wild-looking nature could get mistaken for neglect and mowed down. A native meadow or an unmanicured ravine edge can look, to someone used to a trimmed park, like the city stopped caring for it. That draws complaints and political pressure to tidy it up. The plan's answer is deliberate design choices — mown edges, signage — so wildness reads as intentional, not neglected.

A worker whose job disappears in this transition is owed a real next job first, not after. Take a gas-utility worker, whose job a shift away from fossil fuels would eliminate. The plan treats a funded next role as a requirement before that transition happens, not a nice-to-have that comes later.

Some pieces are honestly not built yet. The plan admits it doesn't yet have Toronto-specific baseline numbers for two of its own proposed success measures: beaver and pollinators. No funded project currently exists to reopen either of Toronto's two historic buried creeks, so any promise about that is still just a proposal. And the exact legal weight of the public ravine-adoption list — a simple public promise versus a formal City agreement — hasn't been settled either.

Anything involving Indigenous partners, land, or knowledge is not this program's to design. The plan is explicit about this. Co-governing the ravines, any naming, and any cultural practice like ceremonial burning are for Indigenous nations to lead. This research program does not propose or decide those things for them.

And underneath all of this: the charter is still a proposal. It hasn't been formally approved. No ravine has been adopted, no steward recruited, and no city department, conservation authority, or Indigenous nation has been contacted about any of it.

  • The flagship design brief — this library's internal records — the full case for and

Every number and claim in this explainer traces back to one of those three files. If a figure above doesn't have a citable source in those files, it isn't in this explainer either.

There's no petition to sign and no ravine to adopt yet — this program hasn't been approved, and it hasn't contacted anyone. The one honest action available right now is to watch for whether the charter gets ratified, and if it does, watch for the Living City Index's first real numbers — including whether it keeps the deer caveat and the beaches-versus-harbour split, not just the flattering ones. That is the actual test of whether this program keeps its own honesty rule once real numbers start coming in.
