The Enough Line — Explained Simply

v0.1, 2026-07-19 Date: 2026-07-19 ·

Status: v0.1, 2026-07-19 · RENDER — derived from this library's internal records and this library's internal records (both 2026-07-18), pilot per this library's internal records DRAFT, pre-ratification — the concept this page explains has not been approved yet, sits behind a two-person sign-off gate, and no real household has been contacted. This page changes none of that; it only explains it.

This is written for anyone who wants to know what "enough" actually costs to live on in Toronto, without reading four separate expert reports to find out. It explains the plan, why it matters, how anyone would know if it's working, and where it could go wrong — based only on research already done for this program. Nothing below is a promise.

What it is

A full-time worker earning Ontario's minimum wage in Toronto makes $17.60 an hour. Researchers calculate it actually takes $27.20 an hour to cover the basics here — rent, food, transit — a $9.60 gap for every hour worked, the widest gap of any region in the province. Right now, if you ask what "enough" costs in this city, you'll get four different answers from four different, credible sources — a living-wage calculation, an official poverty line, a food-bank usage report, and a "thriving" cost study — and none of them are built to be checked side by side, year after year, the way a weather forecast is. The Enough Line is a plan to fix that, in three parts.

First, a needs-floor basket: a real-dollar number, published separately for different kinds of households (a single adult, a single parent with one child, a family of four, a senior living alone), stating what it actually costs to cover food, shelter, transit, medicine, a phone/internet connection, and care for a child or elder — for real, right now, in this city. Second, a household budget observatory: real Toronto households volunteer, anonymously and never individually identifiable, to report how their own budgets actually compare to that basket, so the project can say how many households fall short, and by how much — something none of the four existing measurements currently do. Third, a four-domain scoreboard: the Enough Line published alongside three other everyday measures of how Toronto is doing — safety, getting around, and public spaces — so residents, reporters, and Council argue policy from one shared page instead of four competing ones.

Why it matters

Toronto's food banks were visited 4.1 million times in the past year — more than one visit for every ten people who live here — a number that has grown 340% since 2019. Most of those visits, 59%, are now people returning again and again, not people in a single one-time emergency. Rent is a central driver: the average asking rent in Toronto was $2,537 a month as of June 2026, and 88% of people visiting a food bank already live in housing they can't really afford, with more than one in five spending their entire income on housing and nothing else.

It's worth being honest about why a program like this cares about this question at all. One idea behind it is a hunch: that when people don't have enough, they get pushed toward desperate choices, and that people who hoard far more wealth than they need might be reacting to old fears about scarcity, not simple greed. The real research on this is genuinely mixed, not a clean yes. Losing unemployment benefits does reliably push up property crime — that part holds up well across decades of study. But a lot of theft that looks need-driven actually isn't: organized retail-theft rings and white-collar fraud are driven by profit and status, not poverty, and people who use drugs recreationally offend at nowhere near the rate of people caught in serious dependency. The idea that hoarding wealth comes from old scarcity fears is the least-tested part of the whole picture — plausible, but not something any rigorous study has actually confirmed for wealthy people as a group. The Enough Line doesn't try to settle any of that. It just builds the one number Toronto doesn't have yet: what "enough" really costs, and how many people are falling short of it.

How would we know it's working

The plan doesn't launch all at once. It's built in stages, and each stage only starts once the one before it is solid:

  • Stage one — the basket publishes. The real-dollar cost of "enough," by household type, using

only numbers that are already public today. No real household needs to be contacted for this stage to happen.

  • Stage two — the scoreboard publishes alongside it. Using only data the City, the transit

agency, and Statistics Canada already publish.

  • Stage three — the volunteer panel starts. Only after a privacy expert, a separate named

reviewer, and the project's own two-person sign-off process all approve exactly how people would be recruited and protected.

  • Stage four — the panel's findings publish. In combined form only, on whatever real schedule

the data can actually support — not a promised calendar date.

The plan also states honestly, in advance, what "not working" would look like, instead of deciding that after the fact. If the volunteer panel can't find enough willing households, twice, through trusted community partners, the panel gets shut down — while the basket and scoreboard, which don't depend on it, keep publishing. And if, after a full cycle, the Enough Line changes nothing — no council debate, no news story, no shift in how people talk about the cost of living — the project says so honestly, instead of quietly continuing to publish a number nobody uses.

What could go wrong

The basket could quietly get redefined to look better. A future version of this project could drop an item from the basket, or soften how much a household supposedly needs, to make "enough" look cheaper than it really is. The design tries to block this: any change to what's in the basket has to be announced publicly and independently reviewed before it takes effect, and old and new numbers are always shown side by side — never swapped out quietly.

The scoreboard could get cherry-picked. A good year in one measure (say, safety) could get used to paper over a bad year in another (say, cost of living). The plan's own answer is to publish all four measures together, every time, on purpose — including in years the numbers are embarrassing.

Real people's private budget data could be exposed or misused. The design promises that no individual household's information is ever published, or shared with a landlord, an employer, a benefits office, or police, and that even combined numbers are never published in groups small enough to identify someone — a floor of at least 10 households per published group, though the exact number still needs to be signed off by a privacy expert before the panel launches. If that protection ever failed, even once, even by accident, the plan calls for pausing the panel immediately for a full review.

One piece isn't designed yet. The basket is meant to include a cost for medicine — prescriptions, dental, vision — for households without workplace insurance, and this is the one component the project admits it hasn't actually costed out yet. It's flagged as needing real, original work before the basket can be considered complete.

The whole thing could be used as a political weapon. The design states, as a hard rule, that no campaign or candidate may use the Enough Line's numbers for "the city is failing" or "the city is succeeding" messaging — and if that rule is ever broken, the project commits to publicly saying so.

And underneath all of this: nothing here has been signed off yet. The Enough Line is still a proposal. No real household has been contacted, no money has been spent recruiting one, and every part of it needs a named second reviewer and the operator to both approve before anything above moves from paper to practice.

Receipts

Everything above comes from two documents, both still marked DRAFT and not yet formally approved:

  • The full concept brief — this library's internal records — the complete design, including

the models considered and rejected, and three open questions the plan itself says only a human reviewer can decide.

  • The evidence review — this library's internal records — 48 separate, individually graded

findings on poverty, addiction, and wealth, each rated by how solid the actual evidence is (well-supported, mixed, actively disputed, not supported at all, or simply unconfirmed).

Every number and claim in this explainer traces back to one of those two files. If a figure above doesn't have a citable source in those files, it isn't in this explainer either.

There's no petition to sign or donation to make — the Enough Line hasn't launched, and it touches no real household yet. The one honest action available right now is to watch for Stage One: the day the reconciled basket number actually publishes. Once it does, the real test of this page is whether anyone — a councillor, a reporter, a resident — actually points to it.
