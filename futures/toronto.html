Toronto Futures & Peer Cities

— planning-as-foresight, at home and abroad

PUBLISHED · 2026-08-05 Domain: Futures Library — Toronto & Peer Cities · Source file: this library's internal records · Sources verified: 2026-07-23

This page is Toronto’s own long-range planning record — the real wins and the real failures — plus honest examples of what a handful of other cities have actually done with long-range thinking, so a Toronto conversation about the future has real local precedent to point to, not only imported names.

What’s actually happening (Toronto’s own record)

Toronto’s first attempt at a citywide master plan — the 1943 Master Plan, designed by planner Eugene Faludi — was ambitious but had no legal authority or budget behind it; City Council largely ignored its recommendations and, in the words of a planning history published by the Neptis Foundation, it “quietly disappeared.” The real turning point came three years later: Ontario’s 1946 Planning Act, which for the first time let municipalities adopt legally binding official plans. The Corporation of Metropolitan Toronto (1954–1998), by contrast, succeeded because it was built with real authority from the start — a two-tier regional government credited with enabling the subway system and coordinated regional infrastructure, before being folded into the single amalgamated city in 1998 after 44 years.

The clearest case of citizens overturning an official long-range plan already under construction is the 1971 cancellation of the Spadina Expressway. Jane Jacobs and local activists mobilized residents against a highway that had already consumed most of its $79 million budget by 1969; Premier William Davis cancelled it in June 1971, telling the legislature: “If we are building a transportation system to serve people, the Spadina Expressway is a good place to stop.” The cancellation triggered a cascade — several other planned expressways were subsequently shelved.

Toronto’s own climate-foresight document, TransformTO, has a real accountability trail: City Council adopted a 2040 net-zero target in 2021 (moved up a decade from an original 2050 goal, after declaring a climate emergency in 2019). Toronto’s 2023 community-wide emissions were 16.1 megatonnes CO2e, 43% below 1990 levels — beating the city’s own 2020 target — but also a 1% increase over 2022, both logged honestly rather than only the good news.

Where credible people think it’s heading

Toronto’s current long-range land-use plan, “Our Plan Toronto,” extends the city’s planning horizon to 2051, aligned with provincial forecasts of roughly 700,000 additional residents and 450,000 additional jobs in Toronto by that year. The Ravine Strategy, covering more than 300 kilometres of ravine system, is a real example of why every figure needs its date attached: the City’s own 2026 documents give three different lengths for its flagship Loop Trail — 65 kilometres, 72 kilometres, and roughly 80 kilometres — depending on which report and which date, reflecting a newly proposed extension rather than any single settled number.

Sidewalk Labs’ Quayside project (2017–2020) is Toronto’s most-documented foresight failure, useful precisely because it’s well recorded. Alphabet’s Sidewalk Labs won a waterfront-development competition in 2017 promising a sensor-laden “smart neighbourhood,” but the project collapsed under its own governance problems — city councillors pushed for basic contract transparency, and privacy experts could get no firm guarantee that resident data wouldn’t be monetized — before formally withdrawing in May 2020. What replaced it is the honest second half of the story: a new plan, selected in 2022, drops the “smart city” framing entirely for 4,634 residential units (1,069 of them affordable), with construction beginning in 2025 and completion targeted for 2034.

Other cities offer real, evaluated precedent. Barcelona’s superblocks — reorganizing street grids to exclude through-traffic — produced a measured 25% reduction in one pollutant and 17% fewer fine particles in the built Sant Antoni superblock. A separate, larger figure — roughly 667 fewer premature deaths per year — is a modelled projection for the full, not-yet-fully-built citywide proposal, not a measurement of what’s already happened, and the two should never be confused. Singapore’s Centre for Strategic Futures, moved into the Prime Minister’s Office in 2015, represents three decades of continuous government foresight practice rather than a one-off consultation exercise.

What’s honestly uncertain

Whether Barcelona’s superblock expansion continues is genuinely unsettled: under the mayor in office since 2023, the program has shifted priority from new superblocks toward interior-block maintenance, even as the built Eixample superblocks won a 2025 European Union design award. Dubai’s Museum of the Future and Future Foundation are real and popular, but every visitor and reach figure attached to them traces back to the same government media sources, with no independent measurement found — included here, but labelled plainly as promotional material rather than neutral foresight infrastructure.

Why it matters for Toronto

Toronto already has real, if currently under-used, capacity to run its own foresight and citizen-participation processes. MASS LBP, founded in Toronto in 2007, pioneered the “Civic Lottery” model now used across Canada, completing more than 200 policy projects and 50 citizen panels over 19 years. The Toronto Youth Cabinet, founded in 1998, is the city’s own standing youth-advocacy body, open to anyone aged 14–24 living, working, or studying in Toronto, running ten working groups feeding into municipal decisions. Toronto’s own Planning Review Panel ran two full randomly selected citizen cohorts (2015–2017 and 2017–2019) before being put on hold in 2019 — “a real four-year institution,” as the source material puts it, “not a one-cohort pilot, and equally not a continuing one.” Wales’s Well-being of Future Generations Act 2015 — described by researchers as the world’s first statutory duty of its kind, enforced by an independent Future Generations Commissioner — is a national, not municipal, example, but real, checkable legal precedent for writing a duty toward future generations directly into law.
