Food & Agriculture Futures — feeding people, honestly measured

PUBLISHED · 2026-08-05 Domain: Futures Library — Food & Agriculture · Source file: research/futures/FOOD_AND_AGRICULTURE_FUTURES.md · Sources verified: 2026-08-05

This page separates verified food-system trends from venture-funded hype that already peaked and cracked — and grounds both in what Toronto’s own food-bank data actually shows.

What’s actually happening

One of the most repeated statistics in food policy — that the world needs “70% more food by 2050” — traces to a real 2009 FAO paper, but the number has outlived its caveats. A 2021 meta-analysis pooling 57 separate global food-demand studies (published in Nature Food) found the real range of projected demand growth from 2010 to 2050 clusters at 35–56%, substantially lower than the figure still repeated today.

Global hunger has fallen for three straight years: the newest joint United Nations report (released July 21, 2026) found undernourishment fell to 7.8% of the world’s population, or roughly 645 million people, in 2025 — down from 8.1% in 2024. But the improvement isn’t shared evenly: Africa alone accounts for 309 million of the world’s hungry people, a 20.0% prevalence rate, the highest of any region.

Alternative proteins have not followed the mass-market trajectory once pitched to investors. U.S. retail plant-based meat sales reached $1.0 billion in 2025 but fell 10% in dollar sales that year — figures from the Good Food Institute, an industry-advocacy group, labelled in the research file (and here) as advocacy data rather than neutral market research, while conventional meat’s retail sales grew 5% over the same period. Beyond Meat’s 2025 revenue fell 15.6% from 2024, and its stock traded around $0.62 as of an April 2026 report — down more than 99% from its 2019 peak. Cultivated (lab-grown) meat tells a similar story: the U.S. approved it for sale in 2023, but by February 2024, it had disappeared from every restaurant that had served it, and a 2026 review found the situation essentially unchanged three years after approval — regulatory approval was not the same thing as commercial adoption. Vertical farming saw a real boom and a real, well-documented bust: five named companies raised over $2 billion combined in venture funding, and one industry account counts 14 indoor-farming companies going bankrupt in 2025 alone.

Where credible people think it’s heading

Precision fermentation — engineering microbes to produce specific proteins like dairy whey, rather than growing whole animal cells — has cleared more regulatory ground and reached more real shelves than cultivated meat, but industry’s own reporting (March 2026) says the current bottleneck is capital, not science: fermentation factories cost hundreds of millions of dollars to build, and lenders want proof of demand before financing them, while buyers want proof of supply before committing — a genuine chicken-and-egg problem.

Gene-edited crop regulation is shifting in Europe toward an approach North America has arguably had in some form for years. The UK’s Precision Breeding Regulations took effect November 13, 2025; the European Union’s equivalent regulation entered into force July 16, 2026, exempting gene edits that could occur through conventional breeding from the EU’s stricter GMO rules.

What’s honestly uncertain

Whether cultivated meat can ever reach a competitive price is a genuinely disputed, unresolved argument between named parties: the most-cited independent cost analysis puts a floor price well above what’s needed to compete with conventional meat, even under its most optimistic assumptions, while some companies in the field argue that analysis was “spectacularly wrong.” Which vertical-farming survivors actually stay in business can also flip fast — one major company reported near-shutdown in late 2025 before a rescue acquisition, finalized June 11, 2026, kept it operating.

Why it matters for Toronto

Ontario is losing farmland, and the most commonly cited figure — 319 acres a day — traces cleanly to Statistics Canada’s 2021 Census of Agriculture, but it’s already several years stale, and the underlying rate is accelerating: the equivalent figure for the previous five-year census period was about 175 acres a day, meaning the loss rate nearly doubled between the two most recent counts. A new figure is expected from the 2026 Census of Agriculture sometime in 2027.

Toronto’s own food-bank use hit an all-time high in the most recent full report: 4,124,313 visits in the year to October 2025, up more than half a million from the year before and a 340% increase since 2019. More than 1 in 10 Toronto residents now rely on food banks. The driver is affordability, not joblessness — 46% of client households have at least one employed member, and the median amount left over per day after paying for housing is $8.83. Children make up one in four Toronto food-bank clients, and 18% of households with children reported their kids went hungry at least once a week. Urban farming is real in Toronto but small in scale: Black Creek Community Farm, an 8-acre working farm in the Jane-Finch neighbourhood, harvested 31,046 pounds of produce in its most recent reported year — valuable as community infrastructure, but, in the source’s own words, “a rounding error” against what a city of 3 million people actually eats.

Ontario’s protected Greenbelt farmland — 2,000,000 acres across the Greater Golden Horseshoe — was nearly opened to development in 2022 before a fully documented reversal. The province’s Auditor General found the 2022 site-selection process “biased in favour of certain developers,” and the Premier publicly apologized and reversed the removals on September 21, 2023. An RCMP investigation into the episode remained open as of an October 2025 report.
