Current state

The Percent for Public Art Program: mechanism and governance

Toronto's Percent for Public Art Program is a development-linked public-art funding mechanism administered by City Planning: "The Toronto Official Plan encourages the inclusion of public art in all significant private sector developments across the City. City Planning works with the private sector to secure public art contributions through development review, Official Plan amendments and rezonings" (City of Toronto, "Percent for Public Art Program" webpage, fetched live 2026-07-14). The program's stated governing principle: "that art is a public benefit to be enjoyed and experienced by residents and visitors throughout the city... The Program requires that the artwork must be clearly visible at all times from publicly accessible areas" (same source). City Council approved the current Percent for Public Art Program Guidelines in August 2010; the program is overseen by the Toronto Public Art Commission (TPAC), described as "a panel of citizen volunteers that serves as an informed peer review group to advise City Planning on public art projects and policies" (same source). The "1% of gross construction costs" figure commonly associated with the program's name is directly confirmed in the City's own primary materials: the program's brochure states "The name of the program is derived from the principal that a percent of the project's gross construction costs is dedicated to Public Art," and the City's own program page states "The recommended minimum public art contribution for a development is based on one percent of the gross construction cost (GCC) of that development" (City of Toronto, "Percent for Public Art Program" brochure and webpage; fetched live 2026-07-14). A commonly cited "1986" origin date for the program, found in secondary sources (a real-estate industry blog and a City-produced 2019 jurisdictional-scan appendix), does not match the City's own primary account: the City's own brochure states "The first policies to promote and implement public art were adopted by the former City of Toronto in 1985" — one year earlier than the commonly repeated "1986" figure (City of Toronto, Percent for Public Art Program brochure, PDF; fetched live 2026-07-14). The corrected origin year is 1985, not 1986.

When a developer makes a contribution, three options exist: "'Off-site' – the contribution is collected by the City in a Public Art Reserve Fund and used with other similar contributions to commission public art for a City-initiative in the local ward... 'On-site' – the contribution is used by the developer to commission a public artwork located on or immediately adjacent to the development property... 'On-site' and 'Off-site' – any combination of the above two options" (same source). The on-site option requires "a City Council approved Public Art Plan," developed "with the assistance of City Planning staff and the Toronto Public Art Commission... [to ensure] the developer identifies appropriate public art opportunities on the site; a feasible budget and project schedule; and a fair and professional method for art/artist selection" (same source). This structure — like the parkland cash-in-lieu mechanism documented in the sibling parks-recreation-access leaf — is inherently development-activity-linked: the scale of art funded through this program in a given ward tracks where and how much private development occurs, not a jurisdiction-wide equalized allocation, a structural feature named here as a fact about the mechanism's design.

StreetARToronto (StART): program family and current funding tiers

StreetARToronto is described by the City as "a suite of innovative programs designed specifically for streets and public spaces. Initiated in 2012 as an integral part of the City's Graffiti Management Plan, StART has been successful in reducing graffiti vandalism and replacing it with vibrant, colourful, community-engaged street art" (City of Toronto, "StreetARToronto" webpage, page last modified August 7, 2025; fetched live 2026-07-14). The City states StART programs "make our streets more beautiful and safe, encourage active transportation (walking and cycling), showcase local artists, mentor emerging talent, and reduce overall infrastructure maintenance costs" (same source) — several of these stated goals (safety, reduced maintenance costs) presented by the City as the program's aims, not independently verified as achieved outcomes by this review's sources.

Current StART funding tiers, per this review's search discovery (not independently fetched and quoted from each individual program's own webpage in full, beyond the summary figures below — flagged as a partial-confirmation gap): the Partnership Program offers up to $70,000 for combined community-engagement and mural-production work, run as a two-stage process (community engagement beginning fall 2025, mural production in 2026); the "Just Us" 2026 program will select "up to 6 projects to receive up to $7,000 based on the scale and complexity of their project, with projects distributed equitably across the city in Scarborough, Etobicoke, North York and Downtown" — an explicit geographic-equity distribution criterion named in the program's own call; the Support Mural Program offers "up to $3,000 of direct material purchases for art murals or graffiti art" to eligible City property owners; and the Outside Mural & Street Art Program offered "one-time funding... up to $7,500 for murals in commercial or employment districts" (applications closed as of this review's search). These are modest, project-level funding amounts relative to the Percent for Public Art Program's development-linked reserve fund (cited in the sibling parks-recreation-access page’s backgrounder at $123.9 million in Section 37 community-benefit funding over the current ten-year capital plan, a figure that includes but is not exclusively public-art spending — not independently disaggregated for public-art-specific spending this review).

Graffiti bylaw and enforcement

Toronto's graffiti removal framework places the compliance obligation on property owners regardless of who created the graffiti: "graffiti must be removed within 72 hours, and hate or gang-related graffiti must be removed within 24 hours... it is the responsibility of the property owner to remove it (or have it removed by a contractor)," per this review's search-discovery synthesis of the City's own 311/bylaw enforcement materials (not independently fetched and quoted from the primary bylaw page itself this review — flagged as a gap). Enforcement proceeds through a notice-and-comply process, and "if the owner does not comply within the specified timeframe, the City may remove the graffiti at the owner's expense," with costs added to the property owner's tax bill (same synthesis). A "Graffiti Art Exemption" process exists, referenced in the City's own webpage navigation for approved graffiti art distinct from vandalism (same source), consistent with StART's role in commissioning sanctioned street art as an alternative to unauthorized graffiti.

The actual University of Cincinnati mural study, checked directly against the master briefing's characterization

This review independently located and fetched the University of Cincinnati's own news release describing the peer-reviewed study the master briefing cites (published in the journal Cities, led by Hyesun Jeong, assistant professor of urban design at UC's DAAP; study began 2023, funded by a UC Faculty Scholars grant and extended by a National Endowment for the Arts grant) (University of Cincinnati, "Murals boost Cincinnati's vitality, community development," July 9, 2025; fetched live 2026-07-14. Source: https://www.uc.edu/news/articles/2025/07/how-street-murals-impact-communities-a-study.html · re-confirmed and URL added, independently re-verified 2026-07-16 — this document previously cited the source by outlet/title/date only, with no literal URL). The study's foot-traffic finding is consistent with the master briefing's characterization: "Murals are associated with higher foot traffic, underscoring their ability to attract people and energize public spaces," per lead researcher Hyesun Jeong, and the release states "even after accounting for demographic and density factors, murals are significantly linked to increased pedestrian activity" (same source).

The study's crime finding directly contradicts the master briefing's characterization. The master briefing asserts public art "draws people, and people deter crime" via a Jane Jacobs "eyes on the street" mechanism, framing this as part of the evidentiary case for beautification. The actual UC study states plainly: "No link between murals and crime: The study found no statistically significant relationship between murals and either violent or nonviolent crime rates" (same source). This is a direct, sourced correction to the master briefing's own characterization of the evidence it cites, not a new independent finding — the master briefing appears to have adopted the "eyes on the street" theoretical mechanism as if the cited study confirmed it empirically, when the study it draws its foot-traffic figure from explicitly found no such crime effect.

The same study also documents a gentrification-relevant finding the master briefing's own text separately acknowledges as a risk in general terms, but which this independently fetched source quantifies more specifically: "Areas with murals tend to have denser housing, more college-educated residents and higher rates of walking to work. These areas have seen faster increases in income, rent and home values from 2010 to 2020, though they still show higher vacancy rates and slightly lower median incomes than non-mural areas. Artist populations also remain more concentrated in mural zones" (same source). The study's own author response to this finding, per the release: "the study also cautions that as neighborhoods revitalize, issues like rising property values and displacement may follow. To address this, Jeong suggests strategies for balancing cultural development and affordable housing for residents and artists" (same source) — the study's own author treating the gentrification-correlation finding as a real design consideration, not dismissing it.

Toronto: the case for and against

This backgrounder is itself scoped to Toronto's public-art funding mechanisms — as with several companion backgrounders in this corpus, there is no separate national-versus-Toronto split for this issue. This section carries over the companion Toronto brief's FOR/AGAINST synthesis framing, plus one Philadelphia Mural Arts Program funding figure the brief's Precedents section carried that fills a gap this document's own "International context" section had flagged as unconfirmed. The underlying facts are otherwise already documented in "Current state" and "Key tensions / tradeoffs" above and are cross-referenced rather than restated in full.

FOR:

  • The Percent for Public Art Program has a real, functioning governance structure: City Planning "works with the private sector to secure public art contributions through development review, Official Plan amendments and rezonings," overseen by the Toronto Public Art Commission, "a panel of citizen volunteers that serves as an informed peer review group" — see "The Percent for Public Art Program: mechanism and governance" above.
  • The actual peer-reviewed research behind the most-cited mural benefit claim confirms a real, statistically significant effect: "even after accounting for demographic and density factors, murals are significantly linked to increased pedestrian activity" — see "The actual University of Cincinnati mural study" above.
  • StART's smaller grant programs include an explicit geographic-equity design feature: the "Just Us" 2026 program will select "up to 6 projects to receive up to $7,000... with projects distributed equitably across the city in Scarborough, Etobicoke, North York and Downtown" — see "StreetARToronto (StART)" above.
  • Sanctioned street art functions as a documented alternative to unauthorized graffiti, with a formal "Graffiti Art Exemption" process referenced in the City's own bylaw-enforcement navigation, consistent with StART's role in commissioning approved murals — see "Graffiti bylaw and enforcement" above.

AGAINST:

  • The single most consequential finding in this document's research is a direct correction, not a new independent claim: the master briefing's own cited crime-reduction evidence does not exist in the form claimed — the actual study found "no statistically significant relationship between murals and either violent or nonviolent crime rates," the opposite of a crime-reduction finding, from the very research being cited to support one — see "The actual University of Cincinnati mural study" and "Key tensions / tradeoffs" above.
  • The same study documents a real, quantified gentrification-relevant correlation: mural areas "have seen faster increases in income, rent and home values from 2010 to 2020" than non-mural areas, though the study's own design is correlational, not causal — see "Key tensions / tradeoffs" above.
  • The Percent for Public Art Program's funding scale is structurally tied to private development activity by design, meaning the amount of public art funded in a given ward tracks where development happens, not an equalized citywide allocation — a documented structural feature of the mechanism itself, distinct from StART's smaller programs which include at least one explicit equity criterion — see "The Percent for Public Art Program" above.
  • Several of the City's own stated goals for StART — that it "reduce[s] overall infrastructure maintenance costs" and improves "safety" — are presented by the City as the program's aims in its own promotional material, not independently verified as achieved outcomes by any source fetched — see "StreetARToronto (StART)" above.

Precedents (one figure not previously carried in "International context" above): Philadelphia's Mural Arts Program, named in the master briefing's own "Real-world precedents" list, saw its city budget cut "from $5.1 million in fiscal year 2026 to $3.7 million in 2027," a reduction the Greater Philadelphia Cultural Alliance's president and CEO described as "cutting the budget of signature programs like Mural Arts by 26%" (Peter Dobrin, "Mural Arts and Philadelphia Cultural Fund face the brunt of City budget cuts," The Philadelphia Inquirer, published June 26, 2026). This fills the gap "International context" above flags ("this review did not independently fetch and quote a primary source specific to its current scale") with a directional comparator on program funding volatility and scale — Philadelphia's program operates at a materially larger annual budget than any single Toronto public-art funding tier documented in this page — not a claim that Philadelphia's program design, outcomes, or evaluation methodology transfer directly to Toronto, none of which this document independently verified.

Toronto bottom line: Toronto operates two real, differently structured public-art funding mechanisms — a larger, development-linked reserve fund and a smaller family of geographically-distributed grant programs — but the single most defensible, independently verified finding from this document's research is a correction: the specific "public art reduces crime" claim that has circulated in Toronto policy discussion, attributed to peer-reviewed research, is not supported by that research as characterized — the actual study found a real foot-traffic benefit and explicitly no crime-reduction effect, alongside a real, quantified correlation with faster income/rent/home-value increases in mural areas. This document does not resolve whether that gentrification correlation is causal; it states that the correlation itself, and the absence of the commonly claimed crime effect, are both facts a deliberating body should have in front of it.

Toronto-specific uncertainties: whether any current live StART public-facing material repeats the specific "murals reduce crime" claim found unsupported by its own cited research; whether the Percent for Public Art Reserve Fund's off-site contributions are spent in the ward where collected or pooled citywide; and whether a Toronto-specific study comparable to the Cincinnati research exists, evaluating StART's own actual effects on foot traffic, safety, or property values — all already tracked in "Open questions / data gaps" above.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

This section documents that a tension exists in the evidence, not which side of it is correct.

The master briefing's cited crime-reduction evidence does not exist in the form claimed. This is the single most consequential correction in this page’s own coverage: the actual study behind the master briefing's foot-traffic figure explicitly tested for and found no crime-reduction effect from murals, in either direction, for violent or nonviolent crime. This does not mean beautification categorically has no safety-relevant effect — no source checked this review tested a different specific mechanism or a different city — but it means the specific evidentiary claim the master briefing made, attributed to research that does exist, is not supported by that research as characterized. A card or brief built on this page’s evidence should not repeat the "eyes on the street... reduces crime" framing as an empirically supported finding.

Murals correlate with, and may accelerate, exactly the property-value dynamics the master briefing itself calls the central risk. The master briefing names "art-washing and gentrification" as "the central risk" of a beautification agenda, while also asserting "recent research finds displacement is driven more by the absence of housing policy than by art itself." The actual UC study this page independently fetched finds mural areas saw "faster increases in income, rent and home values from 2010 to 2020" than non-mural areas — a real, quantified correlation the master briefing's own softer "driven more by... than by art itself" framing does not fully anticipate. This backgrounder does not resolve whether this is causal (murals drive gentrification) or the reverse/confounded (murals get placed in already-revitalizing areas, per the same study's finding that "murals cluster near vibrant urban features" and "are rarely found near vacant lots") — the study's own correlational design does not establish causation either way, and neither does this backgrounder.

A development-linked funding mechanism (Percent for Public Art) sits alongside small, geographically-distributed grant programs (StART) with an explicit equity criterion. The Percent for Public Art Program's funding scale tracks private development activity by design (the same structural feature documented for parkland cash-in-lieu in the sibling parks-recreation-access leaf), while StART's smaller grant programs include at least one explicit "distributed equitably across the city" criterion (the "Just Us" 2026 program). This is a documented structural difference in how the City's two main public-art funding channels distribute geographically, not a finding that either channel is failing at its own stated goal. The master briefing's own normative argument on this point — that beautification should be directed to neglected, lower-income neighbourhoods first, not just downtown and affluent areas, because those neighbourhoods are usually both the least beautiful and the least able to attract development-linked art funding [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (beautiful city)] ⚠️ still being checked — is a prescriptive claim this document does not independently evaluate; it is named here as the master briefing's own position, distinguishable from the structural funding-distribution fact above.

Whose taste and who decides — a distinct tension from the gentrification-correlation finding above, not resolved by it. The master briefing separately argues that public art and beautification risk becoming "top-down, corporate, sanitized aesthetics" imposed on communities, and that the answer is art that is community-led, diverse, and grassroots-protective — reflecting each neighbourhood's own people, cultures, and history, made with and by local and emerging artists rather than a homogenized corporate veneer, and explicitly protecting grassroots and street art alongside commissioned murals [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (beautiful city), original sourcing: the document's own argument, cross-referencing its own "joyful-city" sibling briefing] ⚠️ still being checked. This document's own "Current state" section, above, documents that the City's actual public-art funding channels sit on a spectrum between top-down and grassroots: the Percent for Public Art Program requires City Planning and Toronto Public Art Commission sign-off on a Council-approved Public Art Plan for on-site works, while StART's smaller grant programs (Support Mural Program, Outside Mural & Street Art Program) fund property-owner- and community-initiated projects directly — a structural fact relevant to, but not a test of, the master briefing's own community-led/grassroots-protective argument, which this review did not independently evaluate.

Abandoned and derelict buildings — an argument the master briefing explicitly points elsewhere for, but the pointer does not hold up. The master briefing argues that painting over an abandoned building's surface is not enough — that owners of derelict property must be held accountable, and buildings ideally brought back into use — and names this page’s own sibling, the vacant-home-tax briefing, as where that argument belongs [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (beautiful city)]. This review checked our research file for that page directly: it contains no discussion anywhere in its body text of derelict-building accountability, abandoned-building bylaws, or bringing vacant/derelict structures back into use — its scope is the Vacant Home Tax as a housing-supply lever, a related but distinct instrument from a general derelict-property bylaw. The claimed handoff is not real, so this is treated as a genuine gap in this page’s own coverage rather than silently marked dropped. This document's own "Graffiti bylaw and enforcement" section, above, documents a related but narrower mechanism — the property-owner obligation to remove graffiti within 72 hours (24 for hate/gang-related graffiti) — which concerns graffiti removal specifically, not derelict or vacant buildings generally, and does not fill this gap. Toronto is understood to maintain separate vacant-building and property-standards bylaw mechanisms distinct from both the graffiti bylaw and the Vacant Home Tax, but this review did not independently fetch and confirm the specifics of any such bylaw, so none is asserted here beyond naming the gap ⚠️ still being checked.

Pairing beautification with anti-displacement, rather than resolving art-washing risk by keeping neighbourhoods ugly. Beyond the gentrification-correlation finding documented above (from this review's own independently fetched University of Cincinnati source), the master briefing makes a further, distinct normative argument: that the correct response to art-washing risk is not to leave neighbourhoods blighted in order to keep them affordable, but to pair beautification with the affordable-housing, tenant-protection, and anti-displacement agenda, citing a general claim that "displacement is driven more by the absence of housing policy than by art itself" [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (beautiful city), original sourcing: the master briefing cites the Wikipedia "Artwashing" article for this claim] ⚠️ still being checked. This review did not independently confirm this housing-policy-vs-art causal claim; the University of Cincinnati study's own correlational finding above neither confirms nor rebuts it (a correlational finding cannot establish which factor, if either, is the primary driver). This review also checked our research file for that page for any independent treatment of the art-washing/displacement question; it contains no mention of public art, murals, or beautification, so no additional corroboration or rebuttal was found there either.

The "beauty is a luxury amid a housing and affordability crisis" objection. The master briefing also anticipates and responds to an objection that beautification is frivolous given more urgent needs, arguing beauty is cheap, high-return, available to residents regardless of income, and not a trade-off against "the basics" [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (beautiful city), original sourcing: the document's own argument, cross-referencing its "free/gift-city" sibling briefing] ⚠️ still being checked. This review checked our research file for that page directly for a "free public beauty" framing that might carry this argument instead; that document does not mention public art or beautification anywhere in its body text, so the claimed cross-reference does not resolve this argument elsewhere in the corpus either — it is recorded here as the master briefing's own unadopted framing, not independently evaluated by this backgrounder.

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. Public art and cultural placemaking engage UNESCO's 2005 Convention on the Protection and Promotion of the Diversity of Cultural Expressions, which Canada ratified and which frames cultural expression (including public art) as meriting policy support distinct from purely commercial cultural goods. No claim in this page’s coverage cites a Canadian or Ontario government document directly invoking this Convention in relation to Toronto's own public-art policy; named here as the applicable framework rather than an established domestic-policy linkage.

2. 2–3 best global comparators. Philadelphia's Mural Arts Program (named in the master briefing's own "Real-world precedents" list, absorbed here as a coverage-checklist prompt) is widely described in general urban-policy literature as the largest and longest-running public mural program in the United States, though this review did not independently fetch and quote a primary source specific to its current scale or evaluated outcomes — flagged as a gap for a future pass rather than filled with an unconfirmed figure. Cincinnati, Ohio — already detailed above as the site of the actual peer-reviewed comparator study this page independently verified — is itself a directly relevant comparator city: "more than 300 murals across the city, including 55 in one historic district alone," recently ranked "No. 1 spot on USA Today's 2024 list of top U.S. cities for street art" (University of Cincinnati, July 2025 release). A third, non-U.S. comparator was not independently identified and confirmed this review; flagged as a gap. Two further real-world precedents the master briefing names are also restored here rather than silently dropped: creative placemaking as a general practice — the master briefing names Project for Public Spaces and "the Social Life Project" as examples of using art, music, and design to transform and enliven public space [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (beautiful city)] ⚠️ still being checked, neither independently fetched this review, so both are named only as the master briefing's own reference points; and anti-art-washing "place-guarding" practice — community-controlled beautification paired with anti-displacement measures, which the master briefing calls "the cautionary model to follow (beauty without eviction)" without naming a specific city or program [From this library’s earlier research from this page’s carried-forward master briefing (beautiful city)] ⚠️ still being checked, distinct from, though related to, the gentrification-correlation finding this page’s own live discovery independently confirmed above.

3. What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. The most concretely transferable element from the Cincinnati study is methodological, not programmatic: Cincinnati's own research partnership (a local university, funded partly by the National Endowment for the Arts, studying the city's own existing mural program using foot-traffic, crime, and demographic data it already had access to) is a replicable model for evaluating StART's own effects rigorously, rather than relying on the program's own stated goals as evidence of achievement. Toronto has multiple universities with relevant urban-planning departments; whether any local academic partnership already evaluates StART's actual effects on foot traffic, safety, or property values was not established one way or the other this review — named as an open question, not an existing gap or an existing program.

Indigenous context

A an overlay check (2026-07-14) checked this page against the Indigenous lane's seed atlas (this library's Indigenous-sources seed atlas) and made a live Indigenous-authored or co-produced discovery attempt. Unlike most Domain B/D leaves checked in this same pass, a substantive, Toronto-specific Indigenous placemaking angle was found — this page’s own subject (public art, murals, and beautification of public space) is one where Indigenous-led work exists directly on point, not merely adjacent.

The Spirit Garden at Nathan Phillips Square. Toronto Council Fire Native Cultural Centre — an Indigenous-led Toronto service and cultural organization (seed atlas row: councilfire.ca, Indigenous-authored) — states on its own website, fetched directly 2026-07-14: "The Spirit Garden Project is a 20,650 square foot Indigenous cultural space that will provide teaching, learning, sharing and healing experiences for the Indigenous community of Toronto, the City's diverse citizens, newcomers and visitors. The project responds in part to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada Call to Action 82, for government to commission and install a Residential Schools Monument in each capital city to honour residential school survivors, and all the children lost to their families and communities." (Toronto Council Fire Native Cultural Centre, "Spirit Garden" — https://www.councilfire.ca/spirit-garden/ · accessed 2026-07-14.) The same page names each contributing artist and their nation directly: the central turtle sculpture by Solomon King (Anishinaabe, Neyaashiinigmiing First Nation); the Inuksuk by Henry Kudluk (Inuit); the Teaching Lodge by John Keeshig Maya-waasige (Anishinaabe Knowledge Keeper, Neyaashiinigmiing First Nation); the Spirit Canoe by Tannis Nielsen (Métis — Sohto, Anishnawbe and Danish); and the Kaswentha/Two-Row Wampum walkway by Brian Porter (On^yota'a:ka, Two Row Architects). Per Council Fire's own account, the project originated from the organization itself: it "began in 2017, when the Toronto Council Fire Native Cultural Centre told the city that there was an absence of 'Indigenous placemaking' in the downtown core" (per this review's WebSearch synthesis of CBC News coverage of the same project, not independently fetched from CBC directly this review — ⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people)-adjacent secondary corroboration only, cited here for the origin narrative specifically, not as the primary source for anything Council Fire states in its own words above). The garden opened September 2024 in a City-partnered public space (Nathan Phillips Square, in front of Toronto City Hall) — a genuine City/Indigenous-organization collaboration, but the source cited here for what the project is and means is Council Fire's own Indigenous-authored page, not the City's framing of it.

Street-level Indigenous placemaking: the Ogimaa Mikana project. Separately, and predating the Spirit Garden, Anishinaabe artist Susan Blight and Anishinaabe scholar Hayden King co-founded Ogimaa Mikana in 2013, a language-arts project restoring Anishinaabemowin place names to Toronto streets via billboards and street-sign stickers, beginning with a stretch of Queen Street West. Both Blight and King are Indigenous artists/scholars publishing under their own names (Indigenous-authored per this lane's own criteria). This review located this project via WebSearch synthesis of CBC News and the Institute for Public Art's case-study page (direct fetch of the Institute for Public Art's own page was attempted but blocked by a tool-level rate limit in this review, not a source-availability problem) — flagged ⚠️ still being checked as secondary-sourced rather than independently fetched from the artists' own words directly, and named here as a lead for a future pass to confirm against Ogimaa Mikana's own primary materials rather than dropped for lack of a completed fetch.

Neither finding is offered as "what Indigenous people want" from beautification policy generally — each is a specific, named project with specific, named Indigenous authors/organizations, cited in their own or their own organization's words. This page’s broader Cui Bono and evidentiary sections (the University of Cincinnati mural study, the Percent for Public Art Program mechanics) are non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people)/City-institutional material and are not treated as speaking to Indigenous perspectives; they are kept separate from this block per this library's Indigenous-sources provenance standard's voice rule.
