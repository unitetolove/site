Scope

This leaf's neutral scope question, per research/leaves/shelter-security-contracting/LEAF.md: contracted private security at shelter sites — contract values, oversight, wages, and the named firms involved. This document covers: the scale of City spending on shelter/encampment/transit-adjacent private security contracting (both the leaf's own inherited per-contract figures and this pass's new aggregate figure); which firms hold which contracts; the documented oversight gap for operator-procured (as opposed to City-direct) security teams; the wage/billing-rate gap between frontline guards and contract value; provincial security-guard licensing trends; and a City Council accountability response naming the policy question this spending raises. It hands off, rather than duplicates: shelter bed capacity and occupancy figures to shelter-system-capacity-strain; shelter-sector wages for non-security roles (case management, food service as a category) to shelter-workforce-conditions-labour, though this document's own security-specific wage figures are cited here since they are inherent to the security-contracting question itself, not duplicative of the workforce leaf's broader scope.

Current state

The scale of spending: per-contract figures and a new aggregate figure

This leaf's inherited material documents individual procurement awards: a single 2023 procurement award for Security Guard Services at Shelter, Support and Housing Administration sites went to three firms jointly (A.S.P. Incorporated, The West Egg Group Security Services Inc., and Garda Canada Security Corporation) for a combined total potential contract value of just over $42 million, net of taxes and charges, over an initial one-year term with a one-year extension option [CL-329, INHERITED]. A separate 2023 award split The West Egg Group's work into two distinct City contract categories — shelter Community Safety Team Ambassadors ("CSTA") at $10.6 million and Union Station security at $6.9 million, combining to at least $17.55 million [CL-719, INHERITED] — and a 2024 award for Encampment Support security guard services alone came to $2,937,168 per year [CL-689, INHERITED]. The City's 2026 TTC operating budget separately itemizes $26.2 million for a combined safety/security/wellbeing line — a comparable-scale contracting pattern recurring outside the shelter system specifically, named here for scale context and not folded into the shelter-security totals above since it is a distinct TTC-budget line item, not a shelter/encampment contract [CL-399, INHERITED].

This pass's live discovery locates a materially larger, more current aggregate figure that supersedes rather than merely adds to these individual awards: as of December 2025, data the City itself shared with a journalist shows Toronto "is currently tied into eight separate private security contracts... worth a total of roughly $109 million," with three shelter-respite-centre contracts worth $44.8 million combined, a shelter-core-sites contract worth $15 million, an $11.9 million Garda Canada Security Corporation "encampment support" agreement, plus non-shelter-specific contracts (St Lawrence Market Complex $4.8 million, Union Station $14.8 million, and a citywide "private security guards" contract at $18.2 million) [NEW-2026-SEC-1]. Year-to-date spending across all eight contracts, as of December 2025, totalled $35.2 million [NEW-2026-SEC-1]. The $11.9 million Garda Canada "encampment support" figure in this new aggregate is a different, larger figure than this leaf's inherited $2,937,168/year Encampment Support award [CL-689, INHERITED] — the two likely reflect different contract instruments, different time windows, or a renewed/expanded encampment-support agreement since the 2024 award this leaf's inherited material captured; this pass does not reconcile the two figures and states the discrepancy explicitly rather than picking one.

Named firms and the oversight gap

The three firms holding the largest identified shelter-security award are A.S.P. Incorporated, The West Egg Group Security Services Inc., and Garda Canada Security Corporation [CL-329, INHERITED]; this pass's new aggregate figure independently confirms Garda Canada Security Corporation as the counterparty on the $11.9 million encampment-support agreement specifically [NEW-2026-SEC-1], corroborating rather than contradicting the inherited material's own naming of Garda Canada in the encampment-security context. A 2025 Auditor General background file found no standardized oversight of safety teams that individual shelter operators procure themselves, separate from the City's own direct contracts [CL-330, INHERITED] — this pass's live discovery did not locate a 2026 follow-up confirming this gap has been closed; the Auditor General's office generally tracks recommendation-implementation status as a matter of standing practice, but no specific 2026 follow-up report on this finding was located.

Wages and the contract-rate gap

Shelter security work pays approximately $18.15/hour at entry level according to job-posting data, while the same aggregator source lists contracted security services broadly at $25-60/hour — a real gap between frontline pay and the rate the underlying contracts appear to bill, though the two figures come from the same aggregator rather than a matched pair for the same specific contract [CL-404, INHERITED]. The same inherited source also lists shelter food-service work at approximately $17.70/hour at entry level [CL-404, INHERITED] — a non-security role figure this document's scope note above hands off to shelter-workforce-conditions-labour as that leaf's proper home, but the sibling backgrounder was checked directly for this pass and does not carry this specific figure, so it is restored here rather than left silently uncovered by a handoff that does not actually hold. This pass's new $109 million/eight-contract aggregate figure provides additional, independent context for the scale this gap operates at: even taking the lower $25/hour end of the contracted-rate range against the $18.15/hour frontline wage, a contract spanning $109 million in potential value implies a substantial margin accruing above frontline labour cost, though — consistent with the inherited material's own explicit caution — this document does not calculate a specific markup or profit-rate figure, since no source reviewed provides a matched, contract-specific cost breakdown.

Provincial licensing context

Provincially, the number of individually licensed security guards in Ontario rose from 69,332 in 2015 to a peak of 160,353 in 2024, then fell to 136,611 in 2025 — more than doubling over the decade, with the 2025 drop attributed by the Ministry to licensing-system transition delays rather than an actual workforce contraction [CL-718, INHERITED]. This pass's live discovery found a general secondary characterization of Ontario as having "over 90,000 licensed guards" and "the largest security employment market in Canada" [NEW-2026-SEC-2] — a figure that does not match, and is likely a stale or differently-scoped characterization relative to, this leaf's own inherited, more specific 136,611 (2025) / 160,353 (2024 peak) figures sourced directly from the Ministry's own dataset; this document defers to the leaf's inherited, more precisely sourced figures and flags the 90,000 figure as an unreconciled, lower-confidence secondary characterization rather than incorporating it as a competing fact.

A City Council accountability response

This pass's live discovery locates a specific, on-the-record municipal accountability response to the $109 million aggregate figure: a member of Council with a declared intention to run for mayor called the spending level "something we should look into," and argued the private-security spending is "the symptom of a failing homelessness policy," stating that current encampment security deployment is "not to connect these individuals with shelter or housing, but really just to stand there, take notes and monitor," and suggesting the money could instead fund "front-line police officers" [NEW-2026-SEC-3]. The Mayor's Office, per the same report, stated security spending "is part of the annual budget and there are 'no plans to change this,'" and characterized the City's approach as combining in-house security (focused on "the City's most complex and high-risk locations") with contracted guards providing "flexibility to support other sites as needed," with 24/7 monitoring across 1,500 city facilities and encampment-deployed teams tasked with monitoring sites, notifying outreach teams, and identifying safety risks, while stating "the primary response to encampments is outreach and connection to social services through the encampment protocol" [NEW-2026-SEC-3]. This document reports both institutional positions as stated, without adopting either — the Council member's characterization of the spending as evidence of "failing homelessness policy" is their own political position, not this document's finding, per the same disclosure discipline this project applies to any official's contested characterization; and the City's own defence of the spending as budgeted and unchanging is likewise reported, not adopted. Due-diligence items (inherited, not current findings)

Two items are worth naming for due-diligence clarity, not because they raise a current concern about either firm's City work specifically, per this leaf's own inherited disposition. First, Northwest Protection Services Ltd. — not a firm named in the contracts above — sued two of its own former employees over fiduciary-duty and confidential-information disputes; an Ontario Superior Court of Justice decision (2024 ONSC 1292) dismissed both Northwest's own claim and the employees' counterclaim, leaving both sides without an award [CL-720, CL-759, INHERITED]. Second, Garda Canada Security Corporation — one of the firms holding both the leaf's inherited 2023 shelter-security award and this pass's newly-located $11.9 million encampment-support agreement — has no established connection, in the sources reviewed here, to the extensive controversies reported against its much larger international parent corporation, GardaWorld, in other divisions and countries [CL-722, INHERITED]. This pass's own live discovery independently surfaced a CBC report on GardaWorld's Montreal-based ICE-detention operations in the United States, flagged in the atlas as a candidate source [NEW-2026-SEC-4] — consistent with, not contradicting, the leaf's inherited caution that no sourced connection exists between this unrelated GardaWorld division's reported conduct and Garda Canada Security Corporation's Toronto shelter/encampment work specifically; this document does not draw any inference from the parent-company reporting onto the Toronto contracts.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

A $109 million aggregate figure sits against an oversight finding that at least part of this spending category (operator-procured teams) has no standardized review. The Auditor General's finding that operator-procured safety teams face no standardized oversight [CL-330, INHERITED] predates this pass's newly-located $109 million aggregate figure and does not on its own describe whether the City's own eight direct contracts (the ones comprising the $109 million figure) are themselves subject to different, more rigorous oversight than operator-procured teams — this document does not conflate the two spending categories (City-direct contracts versus operator-self-procured teams), since the sources reviewed describe them as distinct, but notes both exist within the same broader shelter/encampment security spending picture.

A Council member's "failing policy" characterization sits against the City's own "budgeted and unchanging" defence, with no independently-audited resolution of which framing the underlying data supports. The Council member's framing (security spending as symptomatic of policy failure, and potentially better redirected to policing) and the Mayor's Office's framing (security as a necessary, budgeted, unchanging operational function providing monitoring and outreach-connection) are both on-the-record positions from a single news report, not independently adjudicated by any audit or oversight body in the sources reviewed here — this document states both positions without resolving which is better supported, consistent with the addressee-discipline guardrail: institutional roles, not individuals, are the addressees this document engages with substantively in its own prose. Neither individual's name is cited anywhere in this document, including the Source line below (CORRECTED 2026-07-17, a later adversarial verification pass — an earlier version of this sentence incorrectly implied the names appeared in the Source citation; verified directly against the NEW-2026-SEC-3 block, which names neither individual, per this leaf's own 2026-07-16 firewall-care fix).

The $11.9 million and $2,937,168/year Garda Canada encampment-support figures are not reconciled. This pass explicitly declines to guess whether these represent the same contract at different reporting points, a renewed/expanded agreement, or genuinely different contract instruments — flagged as a data gap below, not resolved here.

What the evidence does and doesn't support

Well-supported:

  • Toronto spends tens of millions of dollars annually on private security across shelter, encampment, and adjacent municipal sites, independently corroborated by both this leaf's inherited per-contract figures (summing to well over $60 million across the specific awards named) and this pass's new, larger, more current $109 million total-contract-value / $35.2 million year-to-date aggregate figure [CL-329, CL-719, CL-689, NEW-2026-SEC-1].
  • Garda Canada Security Corporation holds shelter/encampment-security-relevant City contracts, corroborated across both the leaf's inherited material and this pass's independently-located aggregate figure.
  • A documented oversight gap exists for operator-procured (as opposed to City-direct) safety teams, per the Auditor General's own 2025 finding, with no confirmed 2026 follow-up located in this pass.
  • A real wage/contract-rate gap exists between frontline security pay (~$18.15/hour) and the billing range for contracted security services broadly (~$25-60/hour), though not calculated into a specific markup figure for any named contract.

Thin or contested:

  • Whether the $11.9 million Garda Canada "encampment support" figure and the leaf's inherited $2,937,168/year Encampment Support award describe the same or different contract instruments is unresolved in this pass.
  • Whether the Auditor General's operator-procured-team oversight gap has been addressed since the 2025 finding is not confirmed either way.
  • The 90,000-licensed-guards figure located in this pass's live discovery does not match, and is not reconciled against, the leaf's own more precisely-sourced 136,611 (2025)/160,353 (2024 peak) Ministry figures — this document defers to the more specific inherited figures and flags the discrepancy rather than treating both as equally reliable.
  • The Council member's and the Mayor's Office's characterizations of the spending's policy merit are both reported as stated positions, not independently verified against any audit finding.

International context

Treaties/frameworks touched. Private security contracting at sites serving people experiencing homelessness engages the right to be free from arbitrary or degrading treatment under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR, Canada a ratifying state), particularly where use-of-force or exclusion practices by contracted (non-police, non-government-employee) personnel are alleged, as in the companion shelter-system-capacity-strain backgrounder's inherited Union Station security-allegation finding [CL-90658, cited there]. This document does not itself allege a rights violation in the Toronto contracts specifically — it names the framework connection because the underlying subject matter (private security exercising authority over a vulnerable population in a quasi-public capacity) is the kind of arrangement this framework is generally understood to bear on, not because any source reviewed here makes a formal rights claim.

2-3 best global comparators. (1) The United Kingdom's Security Industry Authority (SIA) licensing regime, which — unlike Ontario's guard-level individual licensing alone — also licenses and inspects security businesses specifically providing services in "vulnerable sector" contexts (though homelessness-service security specifically is not a distinct SIA category; named here as a comparator regulatory architecture, not a direct homelessness-security precedent). (2) New York City's Department of Homeless Services security-staffing model, which — per general public reporting this pass did not independently verify — has faced its own documented controversies over contracted-versus-city-employed shelter security, offering a comparator jurisdiction facing structurally similar tensions; this pass did not perform a dedicated live-discovery search on the NYC comparator specifically and flags it as an unverified lead rather than a confirmed comparator. (3) No jurisdiction was identified in this pass with a specific, named, publicly-documented "shelter security oversight standard" mechanism that could be pointed to as a model for closing the operator-procured-team oversight gap the Toronto AG identified — stated as a genuine gap in this pass's discovery rather than a manufactured comparator.

What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. The concrete, transferable piece worth naming: a licensing/inspection regime that reaches the security business and specific contract/site level, not just the individual guard level (the SIA model's distinguishing feature relative to Ontario's PSISA, which licenses individuals and agencies but does not, per this leaf's inherited material, appear to impose site-specific oversight standards for vulnerable-sector deployments like shelters). This is descriptive of an existing regulatory architecture difference, not a recommendation in this document's own voice.

NEW-2026-SEC-1 — Aggregate private-security contract spending, directly fetched.

"The City of Toronto is facing questions about the cost of guards it deploys at locations like homeless shelters and encampments, with various private security companies signed up to contracts worth a total of roughly $109 million... Toronto is currently tied into eight separate private security contracts, predominantly for services relating to homelessness. Three contracts for security at shelter respite centres are worth $44.8 million in total, while another contract for shelter core sites runs to $15 million. So far this year, across all of those agreements, city hall has shelled out $35.2 million."
