Part V — The fix · Chapter 20

What One Year Could Do

Working chapter of Why can’t Toronto move? — the report’s summary page uses only claims that passed our receipt check. Figures below marked ⚠️ are still in the re-verification queue, labelled honestly rather than hidden. How that works: check our work.

If Toronto started every fix it already controls on the same day, one year would not transform the system — it would prove the system can be transformed, and that proof is the thing Toronto has never had.

A note on method, up front: no document in the research behind this book models a combined "run everything at once for one year" scenario. This chapter builds that composite for the first time, from each item's own documented cost, timeline, and evidence. Where items interact, we say so; where the honest answer is "not within a year," we say that too. Nothing here is a projection dressed up as a promise.

The starting gun

Everything in this chapter is drawn from the Now tier of the program in Chapter 18 — the list that needs no one's permission but the City's and the TTC Board's own. Imagine Council and the Board pass the whole list in a single season. What does the city actually see twelve months later?

Month one: the free wins

Three items on the list cost almost nothing and deliver on day one.

The City starts archiving the real-time transit data. As far as this research could determine, no archive of the TTC's raw stop-level performance data longer than about sixty days exists anywhere — the two summer months behind this book's own analysis are, as best we can tell, the only such dataset in existence. A daily archiving job is a near-zero-cost action, and every month it doesn't run is a month of the system's real history gone for good. By month twelve, Toronto would have its first-ever year-round record — including the winter no one has ever measured this way.

The TTC publishes an honest scoreboard. The same measurement instrument this book used — already built, already validated — reports on-time performance under a rigorous rider-centric standard next to the TTC's own lenient one, and reports early-running separately from lateness. This costs approximately nothing. What it buys is the ability to see whether anything else on this list is working.

The TTC Board reforms itself. Board composition sits in a City by-law — Toronto Municipal Code Chapter 279 — that Council can amend on its own vote. Published skills-gap disclosure, staggered citizen terms. Done in a season. We make no claim about how fast better governance shows up in service (no such timeline exists in the evidence, and this book does not invent one); the point of doing it in year one is that every later decision on this list gets made by the reformed board.

Months one to twelve: money starts arriving

Two revenue items are already legal and already proven. The commercial parking levy has been available to the City under its own Act since 2006, with an estimated yield of $100–108 million a year — the most shovel-ready new transit revenue in this research. The fare-evasion enforcement scale-up extends a program that in 2025 already recovered roughly $13 million against 2023 losses of $140.9 million. Neither closes a $37.3-billion capital gap — nothing in the Now tier does — but by month twelve, Toronto would have a nine-figure annual revenue line it created itself, which is the strongest possible opening position for the funding negotiations in Part IV.

Months six to twelve: the street starts to move

This is where the one-year honesty matters most, so here it is item by item.

Camera enforcement of streetcar lanes is the fastest street-level lever. The pilot hardware is already running on five routes; the legal authority to ticket already exists; what's missing is the decision to activate it. On King Street, a University of Toronto study counted roughly 6,800 illegal car movements a day, of which more than 99.7% went unticketed. Activation plus scale-up carries a documented under-two-year time-to-benefit — meaning the first measurable deterrence effects, on the corridors where our own data shows dedicated lanes roughly double on-time performance on the TTC's own standard, plausibly land inside the year.

Transit signal priority carries a documented one-to-three-year band, with much of the funding already inside the City's existing congestion plan. In year one: the first treated corridors, not the network.

Off-peak frequency increases carry a one-to-two-year band and need no construction at all — they are a scheduling and budget decision, constrained mainly by operator availability. The best local precedent for a within-one-year measured gain is the TTC's own bunching pilot: 512 St Clair went from headway adherence in the low thirties to 66–68 percent in about a year. That is what "visible in twelve months" looks like when the TTC actually focuses on a route.

The network redesign — the single highest-leverage item in this book — does not deliver new ridership in year one, and this chapter will not pretend otherwise. Peer redesigns took two to three years from decision to rollout. What year one buys is the part Toronto has never done: the systematic duplication-and-trip-matrix audit, the public map of options, and a Council decision that the redesign is happening. All-door boarding on buses (two-to-four-year band) and RapidTO lane expansion (two-to-five years per corridor) similarly spend year one in procurement and paint — locked in, not yet felt.

What a year cannot do — and why it still matters

Say it plainly: one year of everything-at-once does not fix the $37.3-billion capital backlog, does not restore the operating funding model that broke in 2020, does not build a single metre of new rapid transit, and does not touch the two provincial statutes that ban road pricing or the fare powers that moved to the province in 2026. Those are Parts III and IV of this book, and they are multi-year fights involving governments Toronto does not control.

A caution on arithmetic: the gains above do not simply add. Several act on the same corridors — signal priority, lane enforcement, and frequency increases compound on a street like King, but that also means their measured effects overlap and cannot be triple-counted. Peer-city percentages come from different networks under different conditions. This chapter therefore refuses to publish a single composite number ("X% better in one year") — any such number would be an invention, and this book's rule is that invented numbers don't get published.

What one year demonstrably could do is this: give Toronto its first honest measurement baseline and its first permanent data archive; its first self-made nine-figure revenue line; a reformed board; ticketed lanes on the corridors the data says matter most; measurably steadier service on the routes the TTC chooses to focus on; and an irreversible public commitment to the redesign. None of that is transformation. All of it is proof — proof that the paralysis is a choice, built entirely from powers the city already holds. The transformation case in the rest of this book stands on that proof.
