Home / The full vision

pronounced “unite T–O love”

Unite Toronto behind becoming the world’s most abundant, regenerative city — a city of enough, for everyone, forever — leading Ontario and the world.

The way there is not a new movement. Toronto doesn’t need one. It needs the movements it already has — hundreds of them, strong and unhappy and ready — organized, coordinated, talking, and working together. What follows is one citizen’s synthesis of thousands of conversations, offered with the homework already done, to trigger exactly that.

One line underneath all of it: we overcome fear with love — through seeking understanding and doing together. Fear is what we feel toward what we do not yet understand. So the work is understanding — between neighbours, between the city and its own future — and the answer to almost every problem, followed far enough down, turns out to be love with receipts.

0. What this is (and isn’t)

This is not an organization. There is nothing to join, no staff, no membership, no dues. This begins as one person and a set of ideas, published behind a library of verified civic information that anyone can check. Everything else already belongs to the city: the organizations, the halls, the facilitators, the methods, the talent, and the unhappiness — the deep, city-wide sense that we were built for something more than this.

The bet is simple: everyone is unhappy, the infrastructure already exists, and the only missing ingredient is the trigger that gets the crowd and the infrastructure into the same conversation. This document, the library beneath it, and the election ahead of us are that trigger. Nothing more — and if it works, nothing less.

One link, everywhere: uniteTOlove.ca. Not ten different sign-up forms, not a dozen group chats that never meet each other — one door. Thirty seconds: your name and a way to reach you. That’s the whole first ask.

1. Look at the mirror first

Every number below is verified, sourced, and checkable — true of every number this library will ever publish.

Ride Line 1 for twenty minutes and you cross a twelve-year gap in life expectancy — 86 years near Yonge and Doris, 74 near Moss Park. 105,115 households wait for social housing; at the current exit rate, the queue is effectively a life sentence. Fifty-nine people died in our shelters last year; 884 since the city started counting. Drug poisoning killed 347 Torontonians in one year — the leading cause of death for ages 20 to 44. The buses are 14% slower than eleven years ago; the transit system carries a $37.3-billion unfunded gap. Nationally, healthy life expectancy has fallen three and a half years from its 2010-12 peak.

Our governments have said it themselves, formally. Toronto City Council declared a climate emergency, 25-0 (2019), and homelessness an emergency, 24-1 (2023). The Board of Health asked the province to declare an opioid emergency (2017); the province declined. Ottawa, Niagara, Hamilton, Peterborough, Fort Erie, and Belleville have declared homelessness, mental-health, or addictions emergencies — and Ontario’s 29 big-city mayors, representing about seven in ten Ontarians, are formally asking the province for a state of emergency right now. Nobody here chose the word emergency. Our governments did, on the record. Taking them at their word is not radicalism; it is literacy.

And it isn’t only local. The scientists who keep the world’s Doomsday Clock moved it, this January, to 85 seconds to midnight — the closest it has ever stood in its 79-year history, citing a failure of global leadership on nuclear risk, unsafe technology, and the climate. We didn’t set that clock either. It’s simply the sky above the same city.

And the strangest number of all: amid every one of these declared emergencies, seven in ten of us didn’t vote last city election — 29.7%. This time, 21 of the 25 sitting councillors have already re-registered — nearly every incumbent who can run again is running again — and the challengers who have stepped up are mostly running alone and unstaffed. Nominations close August 21. A city in declared emergency, with the same names on the ballot and the challenge running on empty.

Read those paragraphs together and the conclusion writes itself: nobody is coming. It’s us — and “us” already exists.

2. The city already has everything it needs

The infrastructure is there. The settlement world (TRIEC, COSTI). The shelter and housing frontline (Fred Victor, Homes First, Covenant House, Dixon Hall, YWCA). The researchers who already did the math (Wellesley Institute, Maytree, the Canadian Observatory on Homelessness). The facilitators who already know how to hold a thousand-person room (MASS LBP and a city full of trained hands). The builders and conveners (Center for Social Innovation, MaRS, Civic Tech Toronto). Eight universities and colleges with their students and faculty. The union locals. The congregations of every faith. The run crews, rec leagues, coaches, and choirs. A public library in every neighbourhood. Our own count found 108 real Toronto organizations, by name, across every one of these categories. None of these organizations has endorsed this — they are named as evidence, not affiliation: the city we need is already here. It has simply never been introduced to itself.

The solutions are there. Nearly every problem in the mirror has been solved somewhere, often by cities poorer than ours — cataloged in this library, graded, with the receipts. Vienna planned a fairer city street by street. Barcelona freed neighbourhoods from through-traffic and published what happened. Helsinki lets residents decide real budget money directly. Ireland handed its hardest question to 99 ordinary citizens and a chair, and the country voted their answer into law. The people who built those solutions will help; builders love to see their work take root somewhere new. One honest catch, carried on every page: solutions live inside the powers of the places that built them — so every issue here maps exactly who can act: what City Hall can do alone, what it must demand of Queen’s Park, what only Ottawa can move. When the lever sits above the city — it often does — a coordinated crowd's ask, addressed to the right door, is what tens of thousands of organized neighbours can do that seven in ten people staying home on election day never could.

The people are there — including those the city has never asked. Internationally trained doctors and engineers working far below their training. Students who were sold a path and deserve better than limbo. A whole generation of Canadian-born youth who did everything they were told — studied hard, played by the rules — and stepped into a world without the jobs they were promised, through no fault of their own. Elders with decades of mastery and nobody asking. Their barriers — the credential wall first — belong on the public agenda before anyone asks for their energy. And the most universal story in this city: go out young — learn, earn, explore — then come home to raise a family. Toronto is where half the world went out to. Nobody here is presumed to be leaving — many are already home; this is the family we’re raising. And for everyone whose heart holds two places, there’s a second way to help: carry what works here back to the community you came from, too.

And the unhappiness is there. Everyone wants something more than this. That wanting is not despair; it’s fuel, waiting for a credible place to go.

3. The trigger — what is actually being offered

Three things, and only three. None of them is an organization.

The homework. A citizen’s information commons for this election and beyond: the problems measured honestly, the world’s proven solutions, the ideas worth debating — every claim sourced, every source archived, every correction published. Not the best by anyone’s say-so; good enough that you can check it, which is the only “best” that matters. It exists so that no organization, candidate, or resident can ever again say “we’re not ready to talk about this.” Website as the front door; open wiki underneath; receipts all the way down.

The synthesis. Thousands of Torontonians’ hopes and complaints, gathered and clustered into a shared vision the city can recognize as its own — with every line traceable back to the voices that fed it. The tools are live and free. Say what's broken, and it becomes a public problem card whose status moves. Say what you want, and your sentence joins the vision, findably. Say which communities you're proud of, and the city's map of itself fills in — making every community on it easier to join. Everything returns a receipt: what your contribution became, and the next smallest thing you could do. No newsletter. No list to be on. Receipts.

The invitation. To the organizations: meet each other. Same rooms, existing rooms — the halls, libraries, campuses, and congregations that already stand — convened on your own terms, facilitated by the facilitators this city already has, deciding with methods already proven. What do we want? What do we do first? Who does what? The disaster-response playbook this city's own declarations call for — operations centres for logistics and information — is on the table too. It serves those decisions; it never commands them. To the residents: pick any door. The menu runs from thirty seconds to running for office; nearly every rung lands you inside an existing organization, not a new one. The crowd this creates doesn’t belong to anyone; it feeds the city’s own infrastructure — new members, new energy, new mandates for the groups that have carried this work for decades.

One shape this invitation is already taking, concretely: not ten thousand new events, but ten thousand of the gatherings this city already holds — a book club, a mosque youth night, a condo hallway, a work lunchroom, a Discord voice channel — each one adding twenty minutes and a simple kit to what it was already doing. The target — a target, never a count — is ten thousand of these conversations across three months. None of it counts as an audience: a conversation only counts once it sends something back — a vision, a complaint, a name worth encouraging toward candidacy — into the same synthesis everyone else is feeding.

And to every candidate, all of them, identically: the same library, the same published citizen questions, the same standing offers. This infrastructure serves whoever serves the city.

Anyone can sign up — not only voters. Residents who can’t yet vote, permanent residents, international students, the teenager next door, even someone abroad who loves this city — everyone is welcome into the conversation, because the conversation isn’t the ballot. Kids sign up through a parent, with the same privacy protections as everyone else. The running count of everyone who’s joined — voters and non-voters alike — is published openly, sorted so Toronto’s own number always leads. It’s how the city gets to watch itself show up.

Your information is handled by a real, named person, not a database. Whoever holds that job will be publicly named, reachable, and bound to Canadian privacy law, with a working way to delete your information whenever you ask. (As this page goes to print, that seat is not yet filled — it is held by a placeholder name, “Bob Villa,” until a real person takes it on. Nothing is collected until that person is real and named publicly.)

4. The proposal on the table

Once the rooms fill, one proposal will be on the table among many — this one, from the synthesis, offered not imposed. It’s built around six shared goals and a shared calendar, but the crowd may choose differently. That’s the point of a crowd.

Six quests, one city. What would it take for Toronto to become the Happiest, Healthiest, Safest, Cleanest, Friendliest, and Most Beautiful city in the world? Six public scoreboards, one for each — and any neighbourhood, block, or organization can pick the quest that speaks to them and get to work. Nobody has to do all six. Everybody can do one.

Quest One starts with something anyone can join: The Great Toronto Clean-Up — a city-wide, street-by-street clean-up, run in public, with a public scoreboard, open to every resident regardless of age, status, or how long they’ve lived here. No gatekeeping, no sign-up barrier beyond showing up. It’s the easiest door in this entire proposal, and it’s first on purpose.

Six values sit underneath the six quests: Peace, Love, Unity, Respect, and Gratitude — through Service. Not slogans to believe in, but ways of showing up: service to your neighbours, your community, your city, and the truth. However you help — an hour, a block, a skill — that’s the currency that counts most here, ahead of title, ahead of money, ahead of fame.

A calendar, not just an idea. The sprint doesn’t wait for the ballot and doesn’t end at it. It ignites the first week of school — the single biggest week Toronto already gathers for — when all six quests go live together, each with its own public scoreboard, and any classroom, campus, or block can plug into the live count from day one. Around the middle of October, everything those ten thousand conversations have gathered — every vision, every complaint, every nomination — comes together in One Big Conversation: a city-scale look at what Toronto actually said, independent voices heard first, real deliberation after, published back as the State of the Conversation for anyone to read. Voting day, October 26, lands six weeks into that sprint — a scoreboard check, not the finish line. Whatever the vote decides, the year keeps running, September to September: a full year of a city trying its six quests together, in public, at whatever scale it reaches. And when it proves what a coordinated city can do, give the method away to every city on Earth that wants it — carried, in part, by the diasporas who love two homes at once. Toronto learning to coordinate its own crowd is Toronto learning to help coordinate the world’s.

The sense-then-decide discipline holds throughout: independent voices gathered first (that’s where a crowd’s wisdom actually lives), deliberation after (that’s where legitimacy comes from), AI tools translating and clustering but never deciding anything.

5. Why now, and why running for council is harder than it should be

Nominations close August 21. Every extraordinary person in this city who might serve has only the days remaining on that clock to decide. So ask the honest question: why do so many of Toronto’s most capable people never even consider it? Not because they don’t care. Because the price is real. The scrutiny lands on their whole family, not just them. Running seriously means months of unpaid work and a public defeat to explain to any future employer if it doesn't work out. A first-time challenger faces incumbents and machine advantages alone. And when the race already looks decided, why bother filing at all?

Every one of those has an answer, and this library is built to be it. The research is already done, so no candidate has to build it from a kitchen table. A published, identical set of citizen questions means every candidate — first-timer or incumbent — faces the same fair questions in the open, not a gotcha designed to produce a stumble. And a crowd that shows up to listen, asks fair questions, and defends the dignity of every candidate changes what it feels like to stand for office in this city.

The single scariest question a candidate faces — what would you actually do in office? — now comes with a worked answer attached. We ask every candidate, before the vote, to publish exactly what they’d table in their first weeks: the motions, the budget lines, the plan. Right now, of the platforms this city’s candidates have published, not one commits to a single date. That's not a character flaw — a candidate running alone and unstaffed defaults to vague because detail is expensive. We just made detail free. If an unfunded volunteer library can draft a real first-weeks plan, any candidate for the office can publish one too.

6. What this stands on (the short form)

Dignity, always — workers and institutions critiqued with receipts; persons never scapegoated. Honest baselines — real numbers even when they shame us. Enough, for everyone — meet needs and most desperation dissolves; addiction is treated as health, not crime; harm to others still meets accountability. Practice over theory — actions, not manifestos (this document is the exception that proves it). Safety first — anything touching a vulnerable person moves slowest, behind two keys. Work yourself out of the job — this project included; the whole point is to become unnecessary. Borrow shamelessly, credit gratefully. Everyone invited — every language, faith, status, age; Indigenous wisdom on Indigenous terms, never spoken for. Service before status — however you help is the highest thing this city can say about you.

The full design doctrine and the framework behind it are public in the library, receipts included.

7. What this is honest about

Tensions, held on purpose: emergency-style logistics serving decisions that stay local — coordination centres organize information and logistics, communities decide for themselves. Bold vision beside hard evidence — convictions labeled as convictions, tested in public, both sides argued. Solving problems while honouring the people paid to manage them today. Local pride and borrowed genius. As fast as possible, as slow as necessary — and consent is never rushed: the election clock can bend. The rule that nothing starts in a community without an invitation from inside it does not.

Limits, admitted: the city doesn’t hold all its own levers — much sits with a province that has already declined one emergency request; the answer is organizing the ask, not pretending otherwise. Solutions don’t transplant automatically; every “solved elsewhere” claim carries its conditions. One person’s synthesis can be wrong; that is what the receipts, the open corrections channel, and the crowd are for. And an effort this young has everything to prove — which is why the homework ships first, and why the standing invitation is: check it, break it, improve it.

Even the people who lead this proposal answered honestly to the question “who elected you?” — nobody did, and nothing here pretends otherwise. Everything is offered, never imposed. Legitimacy belongs to what the crowd itself decides, not to whoever started the conversation.

8. The clock, the firewall, the finish line

The clock: the library launches with what’s already fact-checked and ready, then grows daily in public · nominations close August 21 · September 1: every school-facing door on the six quests is ready — ignition, the first week of school, all six scoreboards live at once · One Big Conversation, around the middle of October · voting day October 26 — six weeks into the sprint, a scoreboard check, not the finish line · the sprint year runs September to September, whatever the vote’s result · then the method belongs to any city that wants it.

The firewall: the information commons is neutral civic infrastructure — serving every voter and candidate identically, captured by no campaign, publishing nothing unverified. Unverifiable serious allegations go to journalists and authorities, never to these pages. Every piece we publish survives two tests at once: a hostile fact-checker, and the dignity of the person described. Anything built with AI says so, openly.

The money, honestly, to the dollar: the blueprints are free forever. Nobody pays to participate — not at the door, not at events, not by email — and there are no donation asks. Every gathering under this banner welcomes kids, shares food, and never charges admission, period. If you ever see merch for sale — a shirt, a pin, a poster — that's optional, sold at a disclosed margin. Every dollar of that margin funds the AI tools and the printed posters that make this possible, accounted publicly, to the dollar. Nobody involved is paid yet, including the people doing the asking. Money never buys your way in, and nobody’s participation is ever a revenue line for anyone.

The finish line isn’t the election. It's a city that can see itself clearly, decide together, and act. That's measured in libraries of fact actually used, conversations that got smarter, organizations that found each other, problem cards whose status finally moved, and whether the people this city serves worst today are served first tomorrow.

If not Toronto, then where? If not now, then when? Even if it fails, it succeeds — the learning is written down and handed to whoever tries next.

One link: uniteTOlove.ca. Love, organized. By you.
