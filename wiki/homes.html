Homes & Homelessness

Published 2026-07-30 · part of the research library.

Toronto is the most expensive city in Canada to buy a home, and more than 105,000 households are waiting for subsidized housing — a wait that can stretch past ten years. This page covers housing supply and affordability, homelessness and shelter capacity, the fees and rules that shape new construction, tenant protections, and short-term rentals like Airbnb. Some levers that could change this sit with the city; others sit with the province, and sometimes the federal government.

Where things stand

  1. Toronto is the most expensive city in Canada to buy a home.
  2. At the end of 2025, 105,115 households were on the city’s waiting list for subsidized (rent-geared-to-income) housing — up from 81,664 five years earlier. In 2025, 21,538 new households joined the list; only 2,838 got housed. At that pace, people can wait more than ten years.
  3. In Ontario, municipalities cover about 77% of the cost of social housing, the province about 14%, and the federal government about 9% — even though the province writes most of the rules cities must follow.
  4. In June 2025, City Council allowed small multi-unit buildings — sixplexes — as -of-right only in part of the city (Toronto and East York, plus Ward 23), not citywide.
  5. Municipal fees on a new low-rise home in Toronto — development charges, parkland dedication, and community benefit charges combined — totalled $195,832 per unit as of August 2024, the second-highest of 16 GTA municipalities studied.
  6. A 2025 Auditor General audit of Toronto’s winter shelter system found 625 beds at the winter peak, an average of 174 unmatched callers per day at Central Intake, and 4,331 bed-nights left vacant as Warming Centres were deactivating.
  7. Ontario caps most landlords’ annual rent increase at 1.9% for 2027 (2.1% for 2026) — but any unit first occupied after November 15, 2018 is exempt from that cap entirely. Toronto’s own Rental Renovation Licence Bylaw, in force since July 2025, requires landlords to hold a City licence before evicting a tenant for renovations.
  8. Toronto requires short-term rental hosts to register their principal residence and caps entire-home rentals at 180 nights a year. The city’s own 2024 Auditor General audit estimated about 10% of operators may have exceeded that cap.

What the city controls vs. what needs the province

The city can act on directly: allowing small apartment buildings (like sixplexes) on more residential streets — it has done this in only part of the city so far; waiving building fees on new rental homes; funding housing that comes with support workers; licensing and enforcing rules on short-term rentals; its own Rental Renovation Licence Bylaw; how it plans and staffs winter shelter capacity.

Needs the province: restoring the city’s power to require affordable units inside new buildings — a 2022 provincial law (Bill 23) cut that power back; changing how social housing costs are shared between city, province, and federal government; the province sets and enforces rent-increase guidelines and their exemptions, and runs the Landlord and Tenant Board, which resolved only about half of cases within its 90-day target in fiscal 2025-26 (51–53% against an 80% target).

One honest gap: this page doesn’t have a solid, citable figure for Toronto’s actual eviction rate — the City’s own housing staff have called the underlying Landlord and Tenant Board eviction data incomplete. This page names that as an open question rather than inventing a number.

How this connects to the election

Several of the levers described above sit with Toronto’s city council and mayor; the province controls others directly, as noted above. A resident who wants to ask a candidate for city council or mayor what they would do about any of these levers can use the neutral, non-partisan candidate question bank for this category — every question there works identically for any candidate, any party. This page does not endorse, score, or rank any candidate or party, and does not direct a reader to any organization or campaign.
