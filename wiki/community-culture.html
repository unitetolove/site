Community, Culture, Sport & Belonging

Published 2026-08-06 · part of the research library.

A lot of what makes Toronto feel like home isn’t run by the city at all — festivals, sports leagues, faith gatherings, and community groups mostly run on volunteers, in borrowed rooms, on thin budgets. This page covers what the city does fund and run directly: libraries, recreation, animal services, and its own reconciliation commitments to Indigenous communities — plus the harder question of who actually owns the stadiums and arenas public money helps build.

Where things stand

  1. Toronto’s Welcome Policy helps lower-income residents pay for recreation programs — sports leagues, swimming lessons, fitness classes. In 2024, it supported 13% of all recreation registrations, close to the 12.5% of Toronto’s population estimated to qualify.
  2. When BMO Field was built in 2007, three governments paid for it — $27 million from Ottawa, $8 million from Ontario, and $9.8 million cash plus $10 million in land from the City of Toronto. MLSE, the company that runs Toronto FC, put in $8 million. The City kept ownership of the stadium. That outcome isn’t guaranteed to repeat: Toronto’s own SkyDome (now Rogers Centre) was built mostly with public money in the 1980s and 90s, then sold off through a chain of private owners.
  3. BMO Field sits on land managed by a City agency, the Board of Governors of Exhibition Place — a 192-acre site run by a nine-member, Council-appointed board since 1983. A bill before the provincial legislature would shift authority over that land to the province instead, though the responsible minister’s office has said that isn’t the plan. The question remains live and unresolved.
  4. After Toronto Public Library expanded hours at some branches in 2024, those branches saw a 44% jump in visits. When it expanded Sunday hours in late 2025, Sunday visits rose 50% and Sunday borrowing rose 54% compared with the same months a year earlier. All 100 branches are set to be open seven days a week by summer 2026.
  5. Toronto Animal Services aims to answer 80% of its service requests within 5 days; it hit 77% in 2024 and projects 75% for 2025, while requests rose 13% year-over-year, driven mostly by injured or distressed animals, cadaver removals, and coyote-related calls. Provincially, Ontario’s own animal-welfare enforcement agency reports calls, inspections, and animals removed from distress all rose again in 2024.
  6. Toronto’s first Reconciliation Action Plan runs from 2022 to 2032, with 28 actions across five themes: restoring truth, righting relations and sharing power, justice, financial reparations, and the Indigenous Affairs Office. The city’s own November 2025 progress report breaks those 28 actions into 141 smaller steps: 103 are underway, 3 are finished, and 18 haven’t started. That same report says plainly that the plan “has not been Indigenous-led and important concerns remain.”
  7. The city already funds an engine for community life that doesn’t need a new organization — it needs more attention. The Community Partnership and Investment Program funds over 225 nonprofits and 238 resident-led projects: festivals, cultural groups, neighbourhood projects.

What the city controls vs. what needs the province

The city can act on directly: growing the Community Partnership and Investment Program and cutting red tape for small volunteer-run events; keeping expanding library and recreation hours the way it just did; setting the standard its own Animal Services holds itself to and deciding how to respond as demand rises faster than the service can keep pace; deciding the terms of any future public contribution to a sports venue — whether the city keeps ownership, what the public gets back, and whether the deal is public before council votes on it; setting the pace and public-reporting standard on its own Reconciliation Action Plan.

Needs the province: whichever government ends up holding the land under a facility like BMO Field decides what gets built on it next — and that decision is currently unresolved between the city’s own Exhibition Place agency and a provincial bill that would shift authority to Queen’s Park. Ontario’s own animal-welfare enforcement capacity and provincial policy set the wider context Toronto Animal Services operates inside.

One honest gap: this page doesn’t have a backgrounder specific to two of this category’s issue index keys — equity strategies implementation, and arts, culture & nightlife investment — beyond the community-grants and library material already covered above.

How this connects to the election

Several of the levers described above sit with Toronto’s city council and mayor; land-authority and provincial animal-welfare questions sit partly or wholly with the province, as noted above. A resident who wants to ask a candidate for city council or mayor what they would do about any of these levers can use the neutral, non-partisan candidate question bank for this category — every question there works identically for any candidate, any party. This page does not endorse, score, or rank any candidate or party, and does not direct a reader to any organization or campaign.
