Toronto's police and crisis-response call volume: a fourth emergency service and its diversion record

Toronto Police Service responds to tens of thousands of mental-health calls a year against a $1.70 billion budget, while a newer, non-police alternative — now recognized as the city's fourth emergency service — has a documented, growing record of diverting those calls away from police entirely.

The finding

Toronto Police Service confirmed responding to more than 33,000 mental health calls in 2022 CSRL2-116, against a 2026 gross operating budget of $1.70 billion CSRL2-117. Toronto Paramedic Services separately projects 387,065 emergency calls for 2026 CSRL2-153.

Since its inception in March 2022 through December 2024, the Toronto Community Crisis Service (TCCS) received 10,339 mental health crisis calls and wellness checks, diverting 7,800 of them away from 911; 78% of total calls were completed without police involvement, with the overall diversion rate projected to reach 84% by 2025 CSRL2-118. TCCS is now recognized as Toronto's fourth emergency service — and the City's own Auditor General has identified six further 911 event types (check address, check well-being, dispute, noisy parties, landlord and tenant dispute, and unwanted guest) that could potentially be handled by a non-police responder beyond TCCS's current mental-health-crisis mandate [CL-90662].

Why it matters

The scale contrast matters directly: TPS's 33,000+ mental health calls in a single year sit against a $1.70 billion budget, while TCCS — an alternative specifically built to divert exactly this call type — has already diverted 7,800 calls from 911 in under three years, with a diversion rate climbing toward 84%. The Auditor General's own identification of six additional non-mental-health call types as potentially divertible is a concrete, City-sourced roadmap for where a non-police response model could extend next, not a hypothetical policy proposal.

What this page does not cover

This page does not estimate potential cost savings from further TCCS expansion, since the sources reviewed do not provide that calculation. It does not verify a separately circulating figure for TCCS's own 2026 expansion budget or position count, since two direct attempts to confirm it via primary-source fetch (CBC, CanadaCommons) both returned access errors — that more specific figure is deliberately excluded rather than cited on secondhand confidence. It does not carry a "case against" either TPS's or TCCS's operations, since the sources reviewed are each service's own reporting and a policy-research organization's budget analysis, not a documented opposing position.
