TCHC and social housing vacancy management

Toronto's 2019 Auditor General audit Opening Doors to Stable Housing found that better management of vacant rent-geared-to-income (RGI) housing units — through operational and data-quality fixes alone, with no new construction and no new program — could house thousands more people and recover millions of dollars a year in otherwise-wasted subsidy and rental revenue.

The finding

The Auditor General's report was published June 21, 2019, and adopted by City Council on June 28, 2019 (agenda item AU3.14) CSRL2-036. It is sometimes cited as a 2020 report; the correct date is 2019.

Management estimated that in 2018 — the year the audit examined — the social housing system's vacancy loss totalled $7 million, combining the subsidy the City paid to other housing providers for vacant RGI units and TCHC's own estimated lost rental revenue on its vacant RGI units CSRL2-795. The report frames this as an ongoing structural inefficiency rather than a one-off event: on average, 1,400 RGI units sat vacant across the system throughout the year, and it took an average of six housing offers to fill a single vacant unit. The $7 million figure is the report's measured estimate for 2018 specifically; the report does not state it as a fixed standing annual amount, though the underlying pattern it documents is described as recurring.

A 50% improvement in how existing units are used could get at least 2,200 more people RGI housing assistance system-wide, with zero new construction or new program spending CSRL2-796. The estimate is built from TCHC's own units alone yielding at least 1,700 more people housed, then extended to the entire social housing system.

A related but separate finding in the same report: 1,375 RGI tenants in TCHC buildings were living in units larger than they needed: re-housing them in appropriately sized units could free space for roughly 1,550 more people. This is a distinct lever from the 2,200-person estimate above, not part of it — noted here for completeness; not yet independently ledgered as its own claim.

Why it matters

The report's central case is that these are efficiency fixes, not capital projects: better waiting-list data quality (so housing offers reach applicants who can actually be found and will respond), clearer prioritization rules for who gets offered housing first, and stronger oversight of how units are actually used, rather than new supply. That framing — real capacity already inside the existing social housing system, currently going unused — is what makes this a comparatively small, well-evidenced starting point for anyone assessing what Toronto could do about housing and homelessness before considering new construction.

What this page does not cover

This page does not verify current (2026) implementation status of the Auditor General's recommendations — whether the practices identified in 2018–2019 have since changed is a separate, larger claim that would need its own primary-source check. It also does not carry a "case against," since the source material reviewed here is a set of accepted audit recommendations, not a live policy debate with a documented opposing position.
