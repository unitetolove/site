Supportive-housing waitlist depth, income-support clawbacks, and UK comparative evidence

Three separate but related bodies of evidence bear on the same underlying question — how far current programs and income-support rules actually close the gap between people who need housing and the housing system's ability to provide it: how long Toronto's own supportive-housing waitlist actually runs, how Ontario's income-support clawback rules affect people while they wait, and what a country that has tried government-wide homelessness interventions at scale (the UK) has actually found about what works.

The finding

Toronto's supportive-housing waitlist, analyzed by the Wellesley Institute using The Access Point's own data, found that nearly 60% of applicants on the waitlist (4,431 people) had already waited two or more years, and the top decile of the waitlist had waited 4.5 years or longer. Separately, of the 12,733 applicants in the full 2009-2015 applicant cohort, more than half (52%, or 6,592 people) self-identified as homeless or in temporary housing at the time they applied — a larger, differently-scoped population than the "still waiting" figure above, not two measurements of the same group. This data describes waitlist status specifically as of October 31, 2015 — the analysis itself was published in March 2018, but the underlying figures are 2015-vintage and should be labelled as such for anything time-sensitive CSRL2-818. A fresh S1 discovery this pass (T-0065 batch 4) checked for a current re-measurement: none exists as a precise figure, but The Access Point's own current live FAQ page now gives a general wait-time guideline of 5 to 7 years as most common — somewhat worse than the 2015 cohort's top-decile figure of 4.5+ years, though the two are not a like-for-like comparison [CL-90677]. A separate, widely-repeated "14,000 people... as of 2025-2026" figure was checked directly against its own cited source and found to be the same 2015-vintage data republished in an April 2018 Wellesley Institute page — not a fresh count — and is deliberately not repeated here.

On income support, the federal Canada Disability Benefit (CDB) began paying up to $200 per month ($2,400 per year) starting in July 2025. In May 2025, Ontario announced it would not claw back the CDB from Ontario Disability Support Program (ODSP) or other provincial social assistance payments. A real access barrier remains: CDB eligibility requires Disability Tax Credit certification, and an estimated two million Ontarians lack a family doctor able to certify it. Ontario's own Ministry of Children, Community and Social Services has publicly asked the federal government to make ODSP recipients automatically CDB-eligible, rather than requiring separate certification CSRL2-819.

Ontario's existing social-assistance clawback architecture extends beyond the CDB specifically. Both Ontario Works (OW) and ODSP recipients have Employment Insurance, Canada Pension Plan, and WSIB payments clawed back at 100%. Earned income is treated very differently between the two programs: OW exempts only the first $200 per month before applying a 50% clawback above that, while ODSP exempts the first $1,000 per month with no penalty at all CSRL2-820.

For international comparison, the UK's Rough Sleeping Initiative (RSI) is one of the few homelessness interventions with a peer-reviewed, regression-adjusted impact evaluation. The Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government's 2019 Difference-in-Differences analysis found the RSI produced a 32% reduction in rough sleeping attributable specifically to the initiative — a net reduction of 1,321 people across 83 RSI areas, against a modelled counterfactual of 4,069 people absent the program. This 32% figure, not a separate and less reliable 43% figure that circulates from later, pandemic-affected calculations, is the one to cite CSRL2-821.

The UK government also began, in February 2025, publishing preliminary findings from what it describes as the first systems-wide government evaluation of homelessness and rough-sleeping policy impact anywhere, in the UK or internationally. Early findings from the five areas studied so far found that less than 1% of RSI funding was spent on prevention, that rising crisis-level demand was offsetting programme-level gains, and that cross-departmental collaboration remained limited. The evaluation is ongoing; these are first-phase, preliminary findings only CSRL2-822.

Why it matters

Read together, these five claims describe a system under sustained pressure from multiple directions at once: a supportive-housing waitlist where a majority of applicants already meet the threshold of being homeless or precariously housed at the point of applying, and where waiting several years is typical, not exceptional; an income-support system where clawback design (particularly Ontario Works' far stricter earnings exemption compared to ODSP) can make it harder for people to improve their financial position while housed or waitlisted; and — from the one jurisdiction with a genuinely rigorous, attributable impact evaluation of a large-scale homelessness intervention — evidence that even a program measured to produce a real 32% reduction in rough sleeping still left prevention badly underfunded (under 1% of spending) and was still being outpaced by rising crisis-level demand. The throughline is that measured, real progress at the program level does not by itself guarantee the underlying need is shrinking.

What this page does not cover

This page does not have a precise current re-measurement of Toronto's supportive-housing waitlist (see above for the current qualitative guideline that does exist). On Ontario Works' clawback architecture: a fresh S1 search this pass confirmed the $200/month earnings exemption remains unchanged for 2026, with no government announcement of a change despite advocacy pushes to raise it to $1,000 to match ODSP — the figure in CL-820 is current, not stale, as of this check. It also does not attempt to draw a direct numerical comparison between the UK's 32% RSI reduction and any Toronto-specific program, since the two are different interventions measured with different methodologies in different housing systems — the UK evidence is presented here as a comparative data point on program evaluation and the limits of program- level success, not as a benchmark Toronto's own results should be expected to match.
