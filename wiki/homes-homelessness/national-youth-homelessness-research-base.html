National youth homelessness research base: scale, criminalization, and what prevention looks like

Toronto's youth shelter capacity gap sits inside a much larger, well-documented national picture. Canada's first pan-national youth homelessness survey, a two-decade research record on youth and policing, and a national six-year prevention program together describe both the scale of the problem and evidence for what measurably reduces it.

The finding

The 2016 Without a Home survey — the first pan-Canadian youth homelessness survey, with 1,103 respondents across 47 communities — found that youth make up approximately 20% of Canada's homeless population, an estimated 35,000 to 40,000 people annually and 6,000 to 7,000 on any given night. Among respondents, 40.1% were younger than 16 at their first homelessness experience and 75.9% had experienced multiple episodes. The survey also found stark outcome gaps against the general population: 85.4% reported high mental health distress and 42% had attempted suicide at least once; 75.7% were unemployed against a 13.3% national rate, and 53.2% had dropped out of high school against a national rate below 9% CSRL2-615.

Youth's contact with policing has been measured twice, two decades apart, with converging results. A 2002 survey of 208 Toronto street youth found 81.6% had been a victim of a crime in the past year — three times the general population's rate CSRL2-624. A 2011 follow-up study of 240 Toronto street youth found 78% had had some kind of police encounter, breaking down to 59.8% stopped and searched, 36.8% told to move on, 33% ticketed, and 44% arrested; the report frames this pattern as "social profiling" driven by visibility rather than racial profiling specifically, and recommends the City of Toronto, with the Province, develop an amnesty program letting homeless people clear Safe Streets Act-related records CSRL2-618.

Nationally, youth who identify as LGBTQ make up 25% to 40% of the youth homeless population, a disproportion documented across multiple studies CSRL2-623.

On the prevention side, Making the Shift — a national youth homelessness prevention program launched in 2019 with a $17.9 million federal Networks of Centres of Excellence grant, later scaled nationwide with an additional $28.1 million secured by A Way Home Canada — supported more than 2,800 young people and 400 families before concluding in March 2025. Its own reported results include a Family and Natural Supports intervention model that reduced the share of youth sleeping away from home from 24.6% to 12.2% within six months, and its research directly shaped Canada's 2024 Reaching Home federal homelessness funding directives CSRL2-625.

Why it matters

Toronto's local capacity gap and criminalization patterns are not local anomalies — they sit inside a national research base that has measured the same dynamics (scale, repeat episodes, disproportionate policing contact, LGBTQ overrepresentation) consistently for two decades, with the earliest and most recent policing studies showing broadly similar patterns twenty years apart. At the same time, the existence of a national program with a measured, specific prevention effect (the Family and Natural Supports intervention's outcome shift) is evidence that youth homelessness responds to targeted intervention, not only to shelter-bed capacity — a relevant data point for anyone weighing prevention spending against capacity spending.

What this page does not cover

This page does not independently verify current program funding or replacement arrangements for Making the Shift now that its own funding has concluded. It does not cover a second, larger 2019 national youth homelessness survey that exists in this library's research trail — its own only available source could not be independently verified against real archived text at the time of writing, so it is deliberately left out here rather than cited on the ledger's description alone. It does not cover Toronto-specific youth shelter capacity and funding, which is covered in a companion page (see Related pages below).
