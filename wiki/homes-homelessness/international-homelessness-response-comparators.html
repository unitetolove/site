International homelessness response comparators: Denver, Houston, Rockford, and the UK's Everyone In

Four different international responses to homelessness — a large, aggressive US mayoral initiative with a real accountability problem, a steady-state US metro area, a small US city's genuine long-term success, and a UK emergency program — offer contrasting evidence for what large-scale intervention can and cannot guarantee.

The finding

Denver's All In Mile High (AIMHigh) initiative, launched by Mayor Mike Johnston in July 2023, reports 8,746 distinct people moved into shelter and 7,925 moved into housing as of its own dashboard, current through June 2026 CSRL2-852. An independent evaluation (city-funded but externally conducted by the Urban Institute) found unsheltered homelessness fell 45% between January 2023 and January 2025, alongside sharp encampment declines: unique encampments down 46% (3,002 to 1,633) and large encampments of 10 or more tents down 91% (359 to 31) CSRL2-853. But Denver's own City Auditor, reconstructing the program's actual spending independently, found AIMHigh cost $178.1 million through June 2025 — $20.1 million more than the $158 million the City had previously reported publicly — and concluded the City "does not own" the data behind its own reported move-in statistics, meaning it could not independently verify the accuracy of the very numbers it had been publicizing CSRL2-854.

Houston's official HUD Point-in-Time count for the Houston/Harris County region (Continuum of Care TX-700) has held relatively steady across five consecutive annual counts: 3,047 (2021), 3,124 (2022), 3,270 (2023), 3,280 (2024), and 3,325 (2025) CSRL2-855. Rockford, Illinois — a mid-size Rust Belt city of over 350,000 people — reached and has sustained "functional zero" for veteran homelessness since December 2015 and for chronic homelessness since January 2017, becoming the first US community to end both, and one of only three to have done so as of a 2020 case study CSRL2-856. In the UK, the COVID-era "Everyone In" emergency program moved 11,263 people into emergency accommodation and a further 26,167 into settled or supported accommodation by January 2021, backed by more than £700 million in 2020/21 funding and a further £750 million committed for 2021/22 CSRL2-857. A fresh S1 discovery this pass (T-0065 batch 6) partially resolves this page's own flagged question about whether these gains held past January 2021: England's autumn rough-sleeping snapshot count continued falling through autumn 2021 (a further 9% fall, on top of 2020's 37% COVID-era fall) — but even at that low point, the count of 2,440 was still 38% higher than 2010's pre-Everyone-In baseline of 1,770, showing the program's gains were real but partial even at their peak, not a full reversal of the underlying trend [CL-90682].

Why it matters

These four cases show that "does large-scale intervention work" does not have one answer: Rockford shows a genuine, sustained, multi-year success at ending veteran and chronic homelessness specifically, proving the underlying goal is achievable even for a mid-size city without Toronto's or Denver's scale or resources. Denver shows the opposite risk in the same category of intervention — real, independently verified declines in unsheltered homelessness and encampments, but also a real, documented failure of the City's own financial and data accountability over a program spending well over a hundred million dollars, serious enough that the City's own auditor said it could not verify its own headline numbers. Houston's steady count over five years is a useful reminder that not every large metro area is trending sharply in either direction. The UK's program shows what a genuinely emergency-scale, centrally-funded response can move in a matter of months, at a funding level several times larger than anything discussed in Toronto's own budget debates.

What this page does not cover

This page does not attempt to calculate a per-capita or per-dollar comparison between these cities and Toronto, since population, cost of living, program design, and measurement methodology all differ too much for a direct numerical comparison to be meaningful without much more work than is done here. It does not cover Denver's earlier, separate Supportive Housing Social Impact Bond program, which exists elsewhere in this library's research trail and should not be confused with AIMHigh, a different program under a different mayor. Everyone In's gains are now confirmed sustained specifically through autumn 2021 (see above); whether the decline continued or reversed from 2022 onward remains genuinely unconfirmed — a fresh S1 search this pass found secondary-source claims of a later reversal (a reported 26% year-over-year increase in autumn 2022) but could not verify this against a primary UK government source (both direct UK Parliament links returned HTTP 403, and GOV.UK's own technical-report page for the relevant snapshot doesn't itself state the year-by-year count table), so it is not repeated here.
