Toronto's shelter hotel program: costs, a documented overpayment, and procurement concentration

Toronto's shelter hotel program began as a COVID-19 emergency response and has persisted for years past that original justification, at a cost the City's own Auditor General found included a real, documented overpayment problem.

The finding

The Temporary Hotel Program was created explicitly as a COVID-19 response. By 2024, it required $278.052 million in funding — with only $50.469 million in projected savings from planned site closures — meaning most of its cost structure has persisted roughly six years past the original pandemic justification CSRL2-384. A fresh S1 discovery this pass (T-0065 batch 5) finds the wind-down continuing on a multi-year track: the City's 2026 Budget plans further hotel-program closures totalling $49.188 million in annualized 2027 savings and $52.047 million in 2028, on top of the 2024 figure above [CL-90678]. Toronto's Auditor General, in a 2022 audit of hotel-based emergency shelter operations, found the City paid over $13 million over two years for hotel charges not in accordance with the express terms of its own contracts — an amount that could have paid for approximately 52,000 room nights, including meals and wraparound support services, for an entire year CSRL2-565. The same Auditor General's broader audit of emergency shelters found that emergency shelter costs, including the hotel program, run more than three times the cost of supportive housing and roughly seven to ten times the cost of subsidized housing, rental subsidies, or housing allowances CSRL2-228.

On procurement specifically, City Council records show ten non-competitive blanket contracts against just two competitive blanket contracts for hotel-based shelter support services (infection prevention and control, custodial, and catering) — a five-to-one ratio CSRL2-327. Among these, Council authorized new non-competitive catering contracts with 2361173 Ontario Inc., operating as Sunray Group Hotels, for $20 million, and with 2656601 Ontario Inc., operating as Toronto Plaza Hotel, for $25 million, both over a potential five-year term — a factual record of the procurement decision, not an allegation against either named company CSRL2-328. A frontline shelter worker's published first-person account from December 2022 described shelter hotel residents facing strict curfews enforced "like they were serving a jail sentence," and staff who were not trained in social service work, including a lack of overdose-response training CSRL2-293.

Why it matters

The persistence of a program built for a temporary emergency, combined with a documented, audited overpayment finding and a heavily concentrated non-competitive procurement pattern, describes a program that has drifted from its original justification without a matching shift in cost discipline. The Auditor General's own cost-multiplier finding gives that drift a direct financial frame: money spent on the hotel program buys several times less housing stability, by the City's own accounting, than the same money would buy through supportive or subsidized housing. The frontline account adds a service-quality dimension the cost and procurement data alone don't capture.

What this page does not cover

This page still does not include the Auditor General's more precise "$13.2 million ($14.9 million with HST)" figure or its specific three-category breakdown (vacancy fees, facility surcharges, destination marketing fees) — a fresh S1 search this pass specifically re-checked this and found the opposite of a clean confirmation: two independently-fetched secondary sources gave inconsistent category-to-dollar mappings for the same audit (one attributed roughly $13 million to DMF charges specifically; another described $5.4 million-plus-tax as a vacancy-only figure from two hotels), and the already-archived primary Auditor General document still does not state the breakdown directly. This page's original caution about not repeating an unconfirmed breakdown is reinforced by this check, not resolved by it. It does not cover a documented death at a hotel shelter that exists in this library's research trail — that is a distinct, serious matter involving family allegations that needs its own careful, dedicated treatment, not inclusion as one data point in a general costs-and-procurement page. It does not evaluate whether the named catering contractors performed according to their contract terms, only that the contracts themselves were awarded non-competitively.
