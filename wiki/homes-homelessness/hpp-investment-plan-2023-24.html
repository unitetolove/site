Ottawa's Homelessness Prevention Program: funding structure and the 2023-24 investment plan

Ottawa's homelessness sector runs on three government funding streams layered on top of each other — provincial, municipal, and federal — administered through a program the province significantly restructured in 2022. The City's own 2023-24 Investment Plan report is one of the more detailed public accountings in this library of exactly how a mid-size Ontario city's homelessness dollars break down, and it documents a real, still-unresolved dispute between the City and the province over how much of a provincial funding increase Ottawa actually received.

The finding

Ottawa's homelessness-sector funding for 2023-24 totals over $83.5 million a year from all three levels of government: 58% provincial, about 23% municipal, and 19% federal [CL-90183] [CL-90182]. The provincial share flows through the Homelessness Prevention Program (HPP) — created in April 2022 when the Ministry of Municipal Affairs and Housing (MMAH) consolidated three older programs (the Community Homelessness Prevention Initiative, Home for Good, and the Strong Communities Rent Supplement Program) into one flexible envelope. Ottawa's combined funding under those three predecessor programs had been $46,053,419 in 2021-22; HPP's first-year allocation held that steady at $47,619,500 for 2022-23 [CL-90185] [CL-90180].

In March 2023, MMAH announced a further $202-million-a-year increase to HPP province-wide. Ottawa's share of that increase was $845,100 — a 1.8% bump to the City's own allocation, but only 0.4% of the province's new money, bringing Ottawa's total to $48,464,600 annually for 2023-2026 [CL-90181]. The City publicly pushed back on that split: Mayor Mark Sutcliffe's reported reaction — "We thought there maybe was a zero or two missing" — captures the gap between the province's stated commitment and what actually reached Ottawa CSRL2-269.

The 2023-24 plan allocates that $48.46 million across five categories: emergency shelter solutions get the largest share ($21,440,275, funding 10 shelter and transitional-housing programs run by eight agencies), followed by supportive housing ($13,798,275), housing assistance ($8,195,375), community outreach and support services ($2,607,444), and administration, capped at the HPP-mandated 5% ($2,423,230) [CL-90186] [CL-90187]. Within housing assistance, Ottawa's Housing First long-term rent allowance program (formerly Home for Good) served 360 households going into the year, with roughly 180 more expected to be added [CL-90188]. New in this plan is a pilot housing benefit for large, chronically-homeless families — households of 7 or more who have spent 180+ cumulative nights in shelter over the past year. It pays more than the Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit (COHB) would: a single parent with 6 children in a $1,882/month unit would receive $1,486/month under the pilot versus $1,026.80 under COHB, a $459.20 difference [CL-90189]. Supportive housing funding also covers about 950 beds across 25 Residential Services Homes — private and non-profit residences for adults needing help with daily living — at roughly $7.6 million, plus 224 additional supportive units under construction [CL-90190] [CL-90191].

Ottawa also carries two municipal-only funding streams that predate HPP and sit alongside it: the Housing and Homelessness Investment Plan (HHIP), a $14-million program approved in 2011 and split into five fixed, non-transferable funding "envelopes" (three of which — Employment Supports, ODSP Application Support, and Shelters/Supports to Housing — contribute $6.16 million, about 7%, to the homelessness budget specifically) [CL-90195]; and Supports in Social Housing (SSH), a smaller $1-million program dating to 2009 that now makes up about 1.5% of the homelessness budget [CL-90196]. Outside the dedicated funding envelopes entirely, the City also absorbs an unbudgeted cost for overflow hotel/motel placements when the family shelter system runs over capacity — an operating deficit of roughly $8 million in 2022 alone, about half offset by one-time provincial funding [CL-90184].

The report frames all of this against a system under sustained strain: Ottawa's shelter population rose roughly 5% a year for six straight years (2014-2019), leading City Council to declare a housing and homelessness emergency in January 2020 [CL-90192]. The pandemic temporarily reduced shelter use — a 48.9% drop in refugee claimants crossing from the United States who had been accessing Ottawa's shelters — but for those who stayed in shelter, the average length of stay rose about 23% [CL-90193]. The system that manages all of this now involves more than 60 agencies, 30 of them homelessness-focused [CL-90194].

Why it matters

This is a rare level of public detail on how a mid-size Ontario city's homelessness funding actually works underneath the provincial/federal/municipal split that shows up in higher-level summaries — useful as a direct comparator to Toronto's much larger, more heavily-studied system elsewhere in this library. The HHIP envelope structure is a concrete example of a specific, well-evidenced administrative problem: funds fixed to five separate categories that cannot be redistributed between them, even when community need shifts from year to year — the report calls this "a significant administrative barrier to the most effective use of these funds" in its own words. A 2025 audit by Ottawa's Auditor General reached a related, independent finding: supportive-housing demand is growing faster than the current system can accommodate, and coordination gaps between the three levels of government on capital, operating, and health funding are a real structural obstacle, not just a resourcing question — a finding that parallels this library's own Toronto-focused funding-silo arguments elsewhere CSRL2-465.

The dispute over the 0.4% allocation is also a concrete, documented instance of a pattern this library tracks across jurisdictions: a province announcing a large headline funding increase where the actual share reaching the service-delivery municipality is a small fraction of it. Ottawa's own City Council raising this discrepancy publicly, on the record, is a stronger evidentiary basis than a claim of this kind usually gets.

Since this 2023-24 plan was written, the pressure it describes has continued rather than eased. Ottawa's official October 2024 Point-in-Time Count found 2,952 people experiencing homelessness CSRL2-846, and monthly shelter-system client counts tracked in the City's own HIFIS data rose from 3,658 in June 2024 to 4,596 by May 2026 CSRL2-848. In response, City Council approved a new 10-Year Housing and Homelessness Plan (2026-2035) — the third such plan since 2014 — structured around 3 priorities, 7 goals, and 18 objectives, and deliberately built around directional rather than fixed numeric targets while more baseline data accumulates CSRL2-847. Read together, the funding architecture this page documents and the plan that has since replaced its strategic frame show the same system: still growing, still split across three governments, and still working through the coordination problems both this report and the 2025 AG audit independently identified.

What this page does not cover

This page covers the 2023-24 Investment Plan specifically; it does not independently archive or verify the equivalent 2024-25 or 2025-26 HPP investment plans, which likely exist as separate City reports. Live web searches conducted while researching this page turned up 2026 news reporting describing a sharp rise in family-shelter overflow and hotel/motel costs, and a "Shelter Diversion Pilot" program — but these could not be fetched and properly archived in this session (network access to the relevant sources was unavailable), so no figures from that reporting are cited here as claims. This is flagged as a real discovery-queue candidate for a future Ingest-lane pass with working fetch access, not silently dropped.
