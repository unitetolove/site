Encampment clearance: policy, votes, and costs in Toronto

Toronto's encampment policy has moved through Ombudsman findings of "significant unfairness," a Council vote establishing a 3-shelter-offer enforcement limit, and individually documented clearance costs running into the millions — alongside a real transparency problem in how the City itself counts encampments in the first place.

The finding

The City's own Ombudsman found "significant unfairness" in the City's 2021 encampment clearance practices and issued 23 recommendations CSRL2-099. In 2023, the City's own records show 880 people were referred from encampments to shelter, with 155 assisted directly into permanent housing — a 17.6% housing rate from all encampment contacts that year, replacing an earlier "under 10% acceptance" framing that had no primary-source support CSRL2-100 CSRL2-035.

Council has voted on encampment enforcement policy repeatedly and not always with consensus. A 2024 encampment strategy motion (EC13.8) passed 21-2 CSRL2-278. Most recently, on November 13, 2025, Council passed a motion (MM34.4) limiting encampment residents to three shelter offers before enforcement action, by a 16-3 vote CSRL2-572, with an enforcement buffer of 50 metres and a 24-hour notice period CSRL2-713.

Direct clearance costs have been itemized at least once in detail: a 2021 clearance operation cost roughly $2 million in total, including $357,000 for fencing and $792,668 for turf restoration CSRL2-354 — for scale, that single operation cost roughly a quarter of Toronto Shelter and Support Services' entire 2025 annual encampment-response budget of $8.807 million, cited below CSRL2-437. On the funding side, the City's Parks, Forestry and Recreation division received $3.698 million in one-time federal funding plus a dedicated 5-person cleaning crew for encampment-related parks work CSRL2-406.

On the count of encampments themselves, Toronto's own reporting uses at least three or four genuinely different, non-comparable encampment metrics across different contexts — a real transparency finding in itself, not a data inconsistency to be papered over CSRL2-470. Two of those metrics, cited here separately and not added together or reconciled into one figure: total encampment sites citywide fell from 156 in January 2025 to 91 by January 9, 2026 (with parks-with-tents falling from 107 to 49, and street sites from 49 to 42) CSRL2-204; separately, encampments specifically on City property fell from 283 in March 2025 to 84 by March 15, 2026 (65 in parks, 19 in rights-of-way) CSRL2-431. The City confirmed this second figure by official statement, but named advocates (Diana Chan McNally, Doug Ovens) have publicly disputed the interpretation, arguing the decline reflects displacement — people moving into stairwells, garages, transit, and ravines — rather than an actual reduction in the number of people without housing CSRL2-431. A fresh S1 discovery this pass (T-0065 batch 6, the final page of this task) adds a fourth, genuinely distinct metric to the same non-comparable-metrics finding above: the number of people (not sites or parks) living in tents in Toronto parks fell from 539 in November 2024 to 196 by December 2025, per the same outlet already cited for the site/park counts [CL-90684] — a different measurement entirely, not to be added to or reconciled with the site/park figures above.

Why it matters

This is a policy area where the City's own record includes both an Ombudsman finding of unfairness and a recent Council vote tightening enforcement further, alongside real, itemized costs and funding streams that show clearance is not a cost-free administrative action. The encampment-count precision issue is worth surfacing on its own terms: when a City's own reporting uses multiple non-comparable metrics for the same underlying phenomenon, distinguishing which metric is actually being cited becomes a precondition for any claim about whether encampments are "increasing" or "decreasing" being meaningful at all.

What this page does not cover

This page does not cover Toronto's own encampment outreach or shelter-referral program operations in depth beyond the specific 2023 referral/housing figures cited above. It does not attempt to reconcile the different encampment-count metrics into one single citywide number, since the sources reviewed explicitly establish that no single reconciled number currently exists in the City's own reporting. It does not cover the D6 Business Improvement Areas' own separate advocacy position on encampment policy, which was reviewed but judged not central enough to this page's scope to include without its own fuller treatment. It deliberately does not cover the 2026 Waterloo Ontario Superior Court Charter ruling on encampment clearance (and Ontario/Waterloo Region's own response to it) — that ruling is under active appeal, not final law, and is legally and politically sensitive enough to need its own dedicated page and legal review, not a passing mention inside a Toronto-focused cost/policy page.
