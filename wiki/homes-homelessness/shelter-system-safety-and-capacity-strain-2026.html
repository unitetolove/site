Toronto's shelter system: safety pressures and a real capacity reduction in 2026

Both sides of Toronto's shelter system show real strain in 2025-2026 — a documented multi-year rise in violence against shelter staff, allegations of security violence against unhoused people specifically tied to World Cup preparations, and a real, scheduled capacity reduction driven by a non-homelessness City commitment.

The finding

A CAMH-authored Shelter Safety Study found physical assault incidents against City of Toronto shelter staff increased 423.5% between 2011 and 2021 CSRL2-801. Resident deaths within the shelter system remain a steady, tracked burden: TSSS's own 2025 figures show 59 deaths that year, unchanged from 2024, bringing the cumulative total to 884 since 2007 (an average of 49 per year) — though 2025's figure is 55% lower than 2021's peak, or 68% lower once adjusted for system size CSRL2-793.

A different kind of safety concern surfaced in May 2026: the Toronto Underhoused and Homeless Union interviewed approximately 50 people at Union Station, with some reporting being beaten by security guards, dragged out of bathroom stalls, and subjected to verbal abuse, tied to security posture ahead of the FIFA World Cup. One respondent described being turned away from a city shelter, returning to Union Station, and being assaulted by a security officer there, leaving injuries that now affect his mobility. The City did not directly address the assault allegations, instead describing existing outreach protocols and a complaint-submission process [CL-90658].

Capacity itself took a real, scheduled hit tied to the same event: the 250-bed, 24-hour respite site at Exhibition Place's Better Living Centre stopped new admissions in mid-February 2026 and closed fully by March 15, 2026, due to a FIFA World Cup hosting-duties venue booking secured back in 2022 — a non-homelessness City commitment displacing shelter capacity, not a service-design decision CSRL2-825. Demand for the system beyond emergency shelter remains high: more than 14,000 people are currently waitlisted for supportive housing through Toronto's coordinated access system, The Access Point, per the organization's own current figures CSRL2-794.

Why it matters

A 423.5% rise in physical assaults against shelter staff over a decade is a serious, documented workplace- safety trend independent of any single incident — it should inform how staffing, training, and physical site design are assessed system-wide, not just at one location. The Union Station allegations and the Better Living Centre closure are two different manifestations of the same underlying pressure: a major international sporting event reshaping how and where the City manages people experiencing homelessness, in ways that are not primarily about homelessness policy at all. The 14,000+ waitlist figure is a reminder that emergency shelter capacity strain sits on top of an even larger unmet demand for the longer-term supportive housing that would reduce reliance on emergency shelter in the first place.

What this page does not cover

This page does not include the more specific "90% of people surveyed" figure attributed to a different outlet's reporting on the Union Station allegations — that source could not be independently verified via direct fetch, so only the confirmed CP24 reporting (approximately 50 people interviewed, specific allegations, one named injury case) is cited here. It does not verify the City's own account of its outreach protocols beyond what its spokespeople stated to media. It does not independently confirm The Access Point's 14,000+ waitlist figure beyond the organization's own published number. It does not carry a "case against" CAMH's shelter safety findings or TSSS's own death-reporting figures, since both are the respective organizations' own primary research and reporting.
