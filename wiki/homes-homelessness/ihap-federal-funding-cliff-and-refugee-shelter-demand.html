The Interim Housing Assistance Program's funding cliff, and refugee claimant shelter demand

The federal government's program funding Toronto's response to refugee claimant shelter demand is being wound down sharply just as national data shows refugee claimants remain a fast-growing share of shelter use — and new federal eligibility legislation is adding further uncertainty to the pipeline.

The finding

Toronto's federal funding through the Interim Housing Assistance Program (IHAP) is falling from $300 million in 2025 to an anticipated $97.078 million in 2026 — a $202.9 million reduction — as the City works toward establishing a dedicated 1,000-capacity refugee shelter system by the end of 2026 and winds down other refugee-specific programs. The federal-City agreement covers January 1, 2025 to March 31, 2027, at a 95% federal cost-sharing rate [CL-102, CL-108]. Even before that scheduled 2026 reduction, the City was already facing a shortfall: in a September 2025 letter to Council, Mayor Olivia Chow said the federal government had offered only 26% of Toronto's 2025 IHAP funding request, leaving a $107 million gap for that year alone — equivalent to roughly 1,800 shelter beds. As of August 8, 2025, 3,528 refugee claimants were living in Toronto's shelter system, about 40% of all shelter clients CSRL2-761.

Toronto's situation sits inside a national trend moving in the same direction, at a much smaller scale. The federal government's National Shelter Study found refugees and refugee claimants made up 5.1% of shelter users nationally in 2023, up from 2.0% in 2022 CSRL2-383. The following year's edition of the same study — which relabels the same category "resettled refugees and asylum seekers" — found the national share had continued rising to 6.6% in 2024 CSRL2-607.

Federal eligibility rules are also changing. Bill C-12 received Royal Assent on March 26, 2026, introducing new eligibility bars for refugee claimants; Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada has cited a 196,000-case national asylum backlog and, by April 2026, had sent procedural fairness letters to 30,000 asylum seekers warning their claims may not receive a full hearing. Immigration lawyers have publicly challenged the fairness of resolving complex asylum claims through written procedural-fairness responses rather than a full tribunal hearing CSRL2-357. A fresh S1 discovery this pass (T-0065 batch 3) sharpens what those "new eligibility bars" actually are: claims made more than one year after arrival in Canada, or 14 or more days after an irregular land-border crossing between official ports of entry, are barred from a full oral hearing before the Immigration and Refugee Board — with an exemption for unaccompanied minors under 18. This detail comes from the Canadian Council for Refugees' own submission opposing the bill, not a government source; the mechanics themselves are independently corroborated by several immigration-law news outlets, but no Toronto-specific shelter-demand data reflecting the new rules' actual effect has yet been found, so that downstream question remains open [CL-90673]. Separately, the City has directed a specific site transition: 2035 Kennedy Road in Scarborough, previously a temporary homeless shelter, is being converted to a dedicated shelter site for refugee and refugee-claimant households CSRL2-749.

Why it matters

This is a live, quantified funding gap layered on top of a genuine demand trend, not a one-time budget dispute: refugee claimants already made up about 40% of Toronto's shelter population as of August 2025, national data shows that share climbing every year measured so far, and the scheduled 2026 federal funding cut arrives on top of a 2025 shortfall the City says it already had to absorb. New federal eligibility legislation adds a further variable to how this demand may change going forward, in a direction that is not yet possible to quantify from the sources here.

What this page does not cover

This page does not include a dollar figure for the Kennedy Road site transition — a figure attached to this fact elsewhere in this library's research trail turned out, on direct inspection of the cited source, to be a $250,000 procurement work-assignment threshold from an unrelated section of the same council document, not a $250 million funding figure; that error is flagged here for correction rather than repeated. A fresh S1 search this pass (T-0065 batch 3) specifically re-checked for a corrected Kennedy Road cost figure and for any Toronto-specific data on Bill C-12's downstream shelter-demand effect — neither was found, so both remain genuinely open questions, stated plainly rather than speculated on. It does not cover the Ombudsman's anti-Black-racism finding on refugee shelter exclusion or the related Council vote, which are covered in a separate, existing page on that topic.
