Purpose, Flourishing, and a Post-Work Transition

What gives a life meaning if AI takes away paid work, and how ready Toronto's community infrastructure is for that.

Written per this library's standard page structure, a later review, 2026-07-14. This document does not adjudicate whether Toronto should pursue any particular "flourishing infrastructure" investment — it states what is documented about Toronto's current civic/social-connection baseline and recent basic-income wellbeing findings, as the substrate for any such judgment.

Scope

This page’s neutral scope question (per the carried-forward briefing's own framing): what does the evidence show about human flourishing and purpose when income is secured without paid employment, and what is Toronto's actual current baseline of the "flourishing infrastructure" (community connection, civic participation, library/recreation access) the carried-forward briefing argues must be built for that transition to succeed? This document covers: Toronto-specific social-capital, loneliness, and civic-engagement data; recent (2025-2026) basic-income wellbeing findings from named pilots; and the current fiscal risk to Toronto's own civic/recreation infrastructure. It hands off, rather than duplicates: municipal AI governance to ai-public-good-adoption; Toronto's AI-sector economic opportunity to ai-toronto-sovereignty-opportunity; and AI's labour-market and income-replacement mechanics to ai-work-income-replacement.

Current state

Toronto's own social-capital baseline: a real, measured decline in exactly the "latent functions" this page’s thesis depends on

The carried-forward briefing's central thesis is that freedom from paid work flourishes only if work's "latent functions" (structure, social contact, collective purpose, status, activity) are replaced by other institutions [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. This review locates the single most directly relevant Toronto-specific data source for assessing that baseline: the Toronto Foundation's Toronto Social Capital Study 2022 (a survey of 4,163 Toronto adults, conducted with the Environics Institute, comparable against a 2018 baseline survey of the same design) [NEW-2026-1]. Its findings are precisely on point and, read plainly, sobering: Toronto's volunteering rate fell from roughly 40% to 25% of adults between 2018 and 2022; the charitable-donation rate fell from 75% to 63%, representing a potential loss of more than 300,000 donors; participation in sports/recreation organizations, cultural organizations, and union/professional associations all declined, most sharply among those 55 and older; almost 40% of Torontonians reported they usually do not have anyone they can depend on for help when they really need it, up from 27% in 2018, implying roughly 200,000 Toronto residents with no close friends to turn to for support; general trust ("most people can be trusted") fell from 55% to 42% of respondents; and only about a quarter of respondents said they always have something to look forward to, down from 40% in 2018, with city residents almost twice as likely to report fair-or-poor mental health, particularly among younger and lower-income Torontonians [NEW-2026-1]. Toronto's 2018 municipal election turnout was roughly 41%, falling to roughly 29% in the most recent election referenced by this same report [NEW-2026-1] — a directly relevant civic-participation data point given the carried-forward briefing's own emphasis on citizens' assemblies and participatory budgeting as flourishing-infrastructure mechanisms [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing].

This is a load-bearing finding for this page specifically: the carried-forward briefing's thesis that Toronto is "already building the flourishing infrastructure" through the purok layer, participatory democracy, libraries, and recreation [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing] must now be read against a measured, primary-sourced finding that the underlying civic and social-connection substrate that infrastructure depends on has itself been declining, not merely static or already strong. The report's own authors explicitly frame this as a pandemic-era effect, not an AI-era one [NEW-2026-1] — this document does not assert an AI-specific causal link, and none is claimed by the source. On the purok layer specifically, the carried-forward briefing names it as the "keystone and the costed example" of flourishing infrastructure, citing a $20/day-stipend hybrid design costed at roughly $45–110 million per year, on the logic that it simultaneously provides income to the displaced and builds the social-contact/structure/purpose infrastructure this page’s thesis depends on — a two-for-one [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. This review's own findings above (declining social capital, active capital-plan deferrals) bear on whether that keystone-infrastructure logic still holds at the scale the carried-forward briefing envisions, but this review did not re-examine the purok costing itself, which remains this project's own dedicated purok/mutual-aid page’s subject rather than re-derived here.

The carried-forward briefing's own equity framing on exactly this data is worth restoring here rather than leaving implicit: the briefing argues that freedom from survival-work is most liberating for those currently trapped in the worst jobs — low-wage, precarious, exhausting, dignity-stripping work disproportionately borne by low-income, racialized, newcomer, and disabled residents — for whom secure freedom is a recovery of time for family, health, learning, and chosen purpose that survival-work denied, not a loss of meaning [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. At the same time, the same briefing argues the capacity to flourish in freedom is unequally distributed: people with education, networks, hobbies, savings, and strong communities will navigate freedom far more easily than the isolated, traumatized, and disadvantaged, so freedom without flourishing infrastructure could let the advantaged thrive while the vulnerable drift — widening wellbeing inequality even as income inequality narrows [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. This is precisely the population the Social Capital Study's own findings above describe (younger and lower-income Torontonians nearly twice as likely to report fair-or-poor mental health; roughly 200,000 residents with no close friends to turn to) [NEW-2026-1] — the carried-forward briefing's own conclusion is that flourishing infrastructure must therefore be built deliberately universal and targeted toward those with the least social capital, or the transition "redistributes money while concentrating meaning" rather than delivering the liberation it promises [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing].

A partial counter-signal: Toronto Public Library usage is strong and growing, unlike the broader social-capital trend

Against the Social Capital Study's largely declining picture, this review finds a specific, current counter-data-point: Toronto Public Library usage has grown, not declined, in the years since the Social Capital Study's 2022 data. As of a 2026 City budget document, 81% of Torontonians reported using their library in the past year, up from 68% in 2019, with customer satisfaction at 91%, and in-branch visits reported to have surpassed pre-pandemic levels [NEW-2026-2]. This is directly relevant to the carried-forward briefing's specific citation of libraries as one of the "flourishing infrastructure" pillars already in place [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing] — it is the one piece of that infrastructure this review finds clear evidence is currently strengthening rather than weakening, a genuine and specific positive counterpoint to the Social Capital Study's broader civic-decline finding, worth stating precisely rather than letting the overall negative trend obscure it.

The fiscal risk to Toronto's own civic/recreation infrastructure, as of January 2026

This review finds a directly relevant, current fiscal-risk finding not available to the carried-forward briefing: as of January 2026, the City of Toronto's own Chief Financial Officer confirmed that roughly $300 million in capital plans for community centres, libraries, and related infrastructure had been deferred, with some projects under active consideration for outright cancellation, driven by falling development-charge revenue tied to a "softening" new-home-construction market and a provincial policy overhaul (Bill 17) [NEW-2026-3]. The City's latest budget reduced the capital plan for parks and recreation by $214 million and for libraries by $76 million, with the City projecting $1.9 billion less in development-charge revenue than previously anticipated over the next ten years [NEW-2026-3]. This is a direct, current, and material tension with the carried-forward briefing's own financing framing, which describes flourishing infrastructure as "cheap... and much of it is municipal," with "many already exist[ing] and need[ing] expansion and connection rather than invention" [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing] — the specific infrastructure category (community centres, libraries) the carried-forward briefing names as the flourishing-infrastructure keystone is, as of this review, facing real capital-plan deferrals and possible cancellations, not merely awaiting expansion.

The carried-forward briefing's own financing framing includes a second, distinct argument worth restoring alongside the "cheap and municipal" point above: that the cost of not building flourishing infrastructure is high but hidden — anomie, isolation, mental-health crises, and despair-driven harms (including substance use and homelessness, the subject of this project's forthcoming dedicated pages) are expensive in every sense, making flourishing-infrastructure spending partly preventive, offsetting downstream costs rather than being a pure discretionary add-on [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. Read against this review's own fiscal-risk finding above, that argument cuts the other way from how a pure budget-line reading might suggest: deferring or cancelling community-centre and library capital plans is not merely a paused nice-to-have, but — on the carried-forward briefing's own logic — a deferral of preventive spending whose downstream costs (in mental-health, addiction, and homelessness systems) are simply hidden rather than avoided [master briefing-carried-forward, NEW-2026-3]. This review does not independently verify the size of those downstream costs; the point is stated here as the carried-forward briefing's own argument, carried forward rather than newly asserted.

Updated basic-income wellbeing evidence: the pattern the carried-forward briefing describes continues to hold, with new named studies

The carried-forward briefing's core evidentiary claim — that secure income produces sustained contribution and improved wellbeing rather than idleness — is corroborated by this review's discovery of newer, additional named pilots beyond those the carried-forward briefing already cites. A completed German basic-income pilot (the "Pilot Projekt Grundeinkommen") found the basic-income group scored above a control group on the WHO Well-Being Index consistently over its three-year study period, with a significant reduction in perceived stress, improved sleep, greater satisfaction with leisure-time use, and higher-rated quality of social relationships [NEW-2026-4]. A 2026 report on the Central Iowa Basic Income Pilot (Des Moines; 110 participants across three counties, $500/month, beginning May 2023) reportedly found "high levels of hope," described as an outcome not found in other comparable US state-level pilots [NEW-2026-5] — this is a directional finding this review did not independently fetch and quote-verify from primary reporting, flagged accordingly. A broader synthesis claim located in this review states that "every major UBI or guaranteed income pilot that has measured mental health has found significant improvements among recipients, across very different cultural and economic contexts, from wealthy Finland to rural Kenya, from midsize California cities to Canadian towns" [NEW-2026-6] — this is a strong, sweeping claim, sourced to a secondary synthesis rather than a primary meta-analysis, and is flagged as directionally consistent with but not independently verified to the same standard as the specific named pilots above.

Two further pieces of the carried-forward briefing's own evidentiary case are worth restoring alongside these pilot findings. First, the carried-forward briefing's argument for why freed time tends toward better-directed effort rather than idleness: most paid employment is instrumental — done for the wage rather than because it is the work someone would choose — so when survival no longer depends on a job, people can pursue caregiving, art, coaching, organizing, land restoration, community-building, or mastering a craft; Self-Determination Theory explains why this can raise flourishing specifically, since freely-chosen activity that builds competence, exercises autonomy, and deepens relatedness is the recipe for wellbeing, and chosen vocation supplies all three more reliably than coerced employment [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. Second, the carried-forward briefing names a further real-world precedent this review's own pilot-focused discovery did not independently re-examine: retirement research, which finds voluntary, purposeful, well-resourced retirement supports wellbeing, while involuntary or unstructured retirement harms it — the same conditional pattern (security plus purpose, not security alone) the basic-income pilot evidence above also shows [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. Third, the carried-forward briefing points to the existing volunteering and caregiving economy — vast unpaid contribution that already sustains society — as standing proof that people take on essential work without wage compulsion when they can afford to [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]; this is the same claim the Social Capital Study's own volunteering-rate decline (from roughly 40% to 25% of adults, 2018–2022) [NEW-2026-1] should be read against, since that decline is a data point about the health of the very mechanism this precedent relies on, not a refutation of the precedent itself — Toronto residents still volunteering at roughly one in four is a smaller, but still real, base of exactly this kind of non-wage contribution.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

The carried-forward briefing's thesis that Toronto is "already building the flourishing infrastructure" sits against a primary-sourced, Toronto-specific finding that the underlying civic/social-connection substrate has been measurably declining, and the specific infrastructure category (libraries, community centres) the briefing names as the keystone is currently facing real capital-plan deferrals and possible cancellations. This is not a contradiction requiring resolution — a declining social-capital baseline is, if anything, a sharper argument for the carried-forward briefing's own recommendation to build flourishing infrastructure "as deliberately as the income floor" [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing] — but it is a materially different starting point than "already building" implies, and this document states that difference plainly rather than letting the carried-forward briefing's more optimistic framing stand uncorrected.

Toronto Public Library's own growth is a genuine, specific exception to the broader Social Capital Study's declining trend, and should not be flattened into either the optimistic or the pessimistic reading. The library-usage growth [NEW-2026-2] cannot be used to argue the broader civic-connection decline [NEW-2026-1] is being reversed generally — the two data points measure different things (one institution's usage; broad social-capital indicators) over different but overlapping periods, and this document presents them side by side rather than resolving them into a single trend line.

The basic-income wellbeing evidence base continues to strengthen (new named 2026 pilots corroborate the carried-forward briefing's existing citations) even as Toronto's own local capacity to build the "flourishing infrastructure" side of the equation faces a live fiscal constraint. The evidence that secure income supports flourishing is not in question in this review's findings; whether Toronto can currently afford to build the complementary civic infrastructure the carried-forward briefing argues is equally necessary is a genuinely open, current fiscal question this review surfaces but does not resolve.

The carried-forward briefing names several specific risks to the flourishing thesis beyond the civic-decline and fiscal findings above, worth restoring here since they qualify how the Toronto-specific data should be read. First, an anomie and meaning-vacuum risk: freed time without a scaffold of purpose can produce drift, isolation, despair, or destructive escape (substance use, screen-addiction, extremism) — the briefing frames this as the real version of the "problem of leisure" Keynes worried about, and warns that a society that frees people without equipping them invites it [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. Second, an identity and status loss risk: work is a primary source of identity and social status ("what do you do?" is how people commonly locate one another), so a post-work society needs new, culturally-valued sources of recognized contribution — service, craft, and civic roles can supply this, but only if they come to be valued the way paid employment currently is [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. Third, a cultural lag risk: a work-centric culture (the Protestant work ethic, "earning your keep") may continue to stigmatize the non-employed even once income is secure, poisoning the freedom the income floor is meant to deliver — the briefing frames this as requiring a values shift, not policy change alone [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. Fourth, a romanticization risk: not everyone has a "calling" waiting to bloom, and the briefing is explicit that the vision must make room for ordinary rest, ordinary pleasures, and ordinary lives rather than demanding that everyone become an artist-activist-athlete [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. None of these four risks is Toronto-specific data this review could test directly (unlike the civic-decline and fiscal findings above), so they are carried forward here as the carried-forward briefing's own qualifications rather than independently investigated in this review.

The carried-forward briefing organizes its own conditional thesis into a six-point "what determines success vs. failure" framework and a seven-item policy-recommendations list, both worth restoring here as the standing frame this review's Toronto-specific findings should be read against, rather than leaving readers without the briefing's own organizing structure. The six determinants: income security first (without a stable floor, freedom is just insecurity); replacing work's latent functions by design (structure, social contact, collective purpose, status, activity, supplied through community/service/civic institutions); building flourishing infrastructure ahead of displacement (ready before people need it); universal and equity-targeted access (reaching the isolated and disadvantaged specifically, not just the already-thriving); a cultural shift in how contribution is valued (countering work-ethic stigma); and room for ordinary lives (not demanding everyone become extraordinary) [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. The corresponding seven policy recommendations: build flourishing infrastructure as deliberately as the income floor; replace work's latent functions by design; make access universal and target the isolated; expand service/vocation/contribution pathways (civic roles, volunteering, caregiving support, apprenticeships); invest in lifelong learning and self-mastery; cultivate a cultural shift that honours care, service, and civic contribution; and sequence this infrastructure build ahead of AI-driven disruption rather than reactively [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. Read against this document's own findings, Toronto's current position sits closer to the "not yet built" end of this framework than the carried-forward briefing's own "already building" framing suggested: the civic-connection substrate is declining (finding 1, above), the specific named keystone infrastructure (libraries, community centres) faces active capital deferrals (finding 3, above), and this review did not locate evidence of a deliberate, sequenced, equity-targeted flourishing-infrastructure build already underway that would satisfy determinants 3 and 4 of the carried-forward framework specifically.

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. No binding international treaty specific to post-work flourishing, leisure, or purpose policy was identified as directly applicable in this review. This is stated plainly rather than a treaty being manufactured — this is a psychological/social-policy question with no clear binding international-law analog in the way housing or Indigenous-rights issues have one.

2. 2–3 best global comparators. The German Pilot Projekt Grundeinkommen [NEW-2026-4] and the Central Iowa Basic Income Pilot [NEW-2026-5, ⚠️ still being checked] are both named, current, evidence-bearing comparators this review adds beyond the carried-forward briefing's existing citations (Stockton, Germany 2021-24, Kenya, Finland, Ontario) [From this library’s earlier research from the master briefing]. This review's own live discovery did not surface a materially stronger third comparator specific to "flourishing infrastructure" design (as distinct from basic-income wellbeing evidence generally) beyond what the carried-forward briefing already names via the purok-layer framing.

3. What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. The German Pilot Projekt Grundeinkommen's specific measurement instrument — using the WHO Well-Being Index consistently over a full three-year study period, rather than a shorter or one-off survey — is a concretely transferable methodological design: it is the kind of longitudinal, standardized-instrument approach that would let Toronto's own Social Capital Study (or a successor wave of it) measure whether any future income-security or flourishing-infrastructure investment actually moves the same civic/wellbeing indicators the 2022 study found declining [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-4].

NEW-2026-1 — Toronto Social Capital Study 2022.

"Civic engagement among Torontonians has fallen markedly in recent years while the proportion of city residents who feel socially isolated has risen... The results also suggest that about 25 per cent of adult Torontonians are currently volunteering, down from nearly 40 per cent four years ago. The percentage of adults in the city who donate to charity also fell, to 63 per cent down from 75 per cent in 2018, 'translating to a potential loss of more than 300,000 donors in Toronto'... 'Torontonians are less connected to family and friends coming out of the pandemic, and almost 40 per cent report they usually do not have anyone they can depend on to help them when they really need it,' the study says. That figure is up from 27 per cent in the previous survey... roughly 200,000 people in the city have no close friends to turn to for support... In 2018, 55 per cent of respondents agreed that 'most people can be trusted.' This time around, that figure fell to 42 per cent... Only about a quarter of respondents said they always have something to look forward to, down from 40 per cent in 2018, and city residents were almost twice as likely this time to say their mental health was fair or poor, particularly among younger and lower-income Torontonians."

Source: Toronto Foundation, "Social isolation on the rise while civic engagement has dropped in Toronto, study finds" (reporting on the Toronto Social Capital Study 2022, Toronto Foundation and Environics Institute, survey of 4,163 adults), https://torontofoundation.ca/socialisolationontherise, originally posted November 22, 2022. Accessed via direct fetch 2026-07-14.

NEW-2026-2 — Toronto Public Library usage growth.

"81% of Torontonians used their library in the last year (68% in 2019), with customer satisfaction with the library at 91%. Additionally, in-branch visits have surpassed pre-pandemic levels, with 68% of Torontonians visiting a TPL branch and increasing usage of in-branch technologies services."

Source: WebSearch synthesis citing City of Toronto, "BudgetTO 2026 Budget Notes: Toronto Public Library," https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/bu/bgrd/backgroundfile-261378.pdf. Accessed via WebSearch 2026-07-14; not independently fetched from the primary City budget document within this review's budget — flagged for verification check.

NEW-2026-3 — City of Toronto CFO, January 2026, capital-plan deferrals.

"The City of Toronto is delaying hundreds of millions of dollars in planned work to expand its community centres and libraries as new home construction in the city dries up, with consideration being given to cancelling some projects altogether. The city's chief financial officer Stephen Conforti reiterated on Friday that roughly $300 million in capital plans had been deferred in anticipation of revenue from development charges slowing, adding that some projects may be cancelled. Toronto's latest budget has reduced the capital plan for parks and recreation by $214 million, libraries by $76 million and waste by $6 million... As a result, Toronto is expecting to receive $1.9 billion less over the next 10 years than it had previously anticipated."

Source: Global News, "Toronto may cancel new community centres, libraries if development funding dries up," by Isaac Callan, https://globalnews.ca/news/11634767/toronto-development-charges-funding-cancellations/, published January 23, 2026. Accessed via direct fetch 2026-07-14.

NEW-2026-4 — German Pilot Projekt Grundeinkommen wellbeing findings.

"The basic income group scored above the control group on the WHO Well-Being Index consistently over the three-year study period, with significant reduction in perceived stress and improved sleep. Participants with a basic income reported greater satisfaction with how they spent their leisure time and rated the quality of their social relationships higher than the control group."

Source: WebSearch synthesis citing "Basic income & Well-being," Pilot Projekt Grundeinkommen, https://www.pilotprojekt-grundeinkommen.de/en/well-being. Accessed via WebSearch 2026-07-14; not independently fetched from the primary source within this review's budget — flagged for verification check.

NEW-2026-5 — Central Iowa Basic Income Pilot, "high levels of hope" (⚠️ still being checked).

"Researchers presented their findings from the Central Iowa Basic Income Pilot in Des Moines, which looked at how participants used monthly $500 payments and how it affected their well-being and goals. Starting in May 2023, 110 participants in Polk, Dallas and Warren counties received the monthly support." Reported to generate "an outcome not found in other states: high levels of hope."

Source: Iowa Public Radio, "Iowa basic income program generates an outcome not found in other states: high levels of hope," https://www.iowapublicradio.org/ipr-news/2026-06-12/iowa-basic-income-program-generates-an-outcome-not-found-in-other-states-high-levels-of-hope, published June 12, 2026. NEW-2026-6 — Broad UBI/guaranteed-income mental-health synthesis claim.

"Every major UBI or guaranteed income pilot that has measured mental health has found significant improvements among recipients, across very different cultural and economic contexts, from wealthy Finland to rural Kenya, from midsize California cities to Canadian towns. Recipients showed lower rates of depression and higher scores on measures of positive mental health and sense of purpose. Potential mediating factors included improved time with family and friends, a reduction in perceived stigma and a renewed sense of hope for the future."

Source: WebSearch synthesis citing "UBI and Mental Health: Research and Evidence," Universal Basic Income, https://universalbasicincome.com/ubi-mental-health-research/. Accessed via WebSearch 2026-07-14; this is a secondary-synthesis source, not a primary meta-analysis, and was not independently fetched or cross-checked against a peer-reviewed synthesis (e.g., the ScienceDirect meta-analysis identified in the same search but not fetched: "The mental health effects of a Universal Basic Income: A synthesis of the evidence from previous pilots") within this review's budget — flagged accordingly, and the "every major pilot" framing specifically should not be treated as independently verified.
