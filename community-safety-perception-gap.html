The Community Safety Perception Gap

Crime stats are falling but fear isn't — why Torontonians feel less safe than the numbers say they should.

Scope

This backgrounder covers: the documented gap between statistical crime/safety trends and Toronto-area public perception of safety, evidenced by directly-sourced 2025 polling data; the Toronto Community Crisis Service (TCCS) as a "right responder" model diverting mental-health-crisis calls from police response, evidenced by the City's own third-party evaluation data; and city safety rankings (the Economist/EIU Safe Cities Index) as one commonly-cited, if dated and imperfect, benchmark for the "actual safety" side of the perception gap.

This document does not cover: policing budgets, staffing, or enforcement outcomes (community-safety-crime-policing); complaint/discipline/oversight questions (police-accountability-oversight); or gun/gang violence-prevention grant programming specifically (gun-gang-youth-violence-prevention), though this document's homicide/shooting figures necessarily touch adjacent ground to that leaf and are cited here only for the perception-gap comparison, not as a violence-prevention program evaluation.

Current state

The perception gap, directly evidenced (December 2025 polling)

The most directly on-point, load-bearing source located in this review's discovery is a Liaison Strategies survey published December 30, 2025 and first reported by the Toronto Star, independently and directly fetched in full this review (https://press.liaisonstrategies.ca/perception-gap-gta-residents-believe-crime-is-rising-even-when-it-isnt). Liaison Strategies describes itself as ranking "#1 in accuracy in the 2025 Ontario election and #2 nationally in the 2025 federal election" and as a member of the Canadian Research Insights Council (CRIC) — cited here as the firm's own stated credentials, not independently audited by this document. The survey was conducted October 22–23, 2025 via Interactive Voice Response (IVR), polling 1,000 Toronto residents (margin of error ±3.09 percentage points, 19 times out of 20) alongside separate regional samples in Brampton, Mississauga, Vaughan, and Markham (800 each) [NEW-CG01].

Per the survey's own published findings: "76% of Torontonians believe crime in Canada as a whole has increased over the past year," and, on homicide specifically, "In Toronto, homicides have plummeted, yet 72% of residents believe they are on the rise" (quoting the survey's principal, David Valentin, directly) [NEW-CG02]. On auto theft: "While auto thefts dropped by over 25% in Toronto, nearly 40% in Peel, and 37% in York, more than 7 in 10 Torontonians and Bramptonians still believe the problem is worsening" [NEW-CG03]. The survey's own regional comparison data shows the same pattern replicated outside Toronto: in York Region, "homicides down 67% and hate crimes down 25% mid-year, yet over half of residents in Vaughan and Markham still perceive these crimes to be on the rise," and in Peel Region, Brampton (73%) and Mississauga (60%) residents perceived homicides as increasing despite Peel Regional Police reporting "only 10 occurrences through October 2025, compared to 19 for the full year of 2024" [NEW-CG04]. The survey's own summary framing states: "What we have found is that citizens in every major city feel crime is going up regardless of whether crime has increased, remained stable or declined" (quoting Valentin) [NEW-CG05].

The master briefing document this page inherits from separately reports an earlier, transit-specific perception figure — that 60% of residents think TTC safety has worsened — flagged in that source itself as [confirm] ⚠️ still being checked (carried-forward; not independently re-fetched or re-surveyed this review, and not necessarily the same survey wave or methodology as the Liaison Strategies poll above). It is reported here as a second, separately-sourced data point consistent with the same qualitative perception-gap pattern documented above, not as an independently confirmed figure. Taking both the statistical trend and this scale of public fear seriously is central to this page’s own subject: a real perception gap does not make the underlying fear illogical or the concern about transit safety specifically something to dismiss — the honest position is that both the direction of the crime data and the scale and legitimacy of public concern are real and need addressing together, not that one cancels the other out.

The underlying trend data the perception gap is measured against

Multiple, partially inconsistent figures for Toronto's actual 2025 homicide and auto-theft trends were located in this review's discovery, and this document reports the range honestly rather than picking one. On homicides: one search-surfaced source states Toronto recorded 42 homicides in 2025 ("the lowest rate since 1986"), while another states 39 homicides as of December 18, 2025, "a 55% decrease from the 81 homicides during the same period in 2024" [NEW-CG06, ⚠️ still being checked — both figures surfaced via search-result summary, not independently fetched from the Toronto Police Service's own primary data portal this review]. On auto theft: search-surfaced sources report year-over-year 2025 declines ranging from approximately 24% to 34%, depending on the specific source and measurement window [NEW-CG07, ⚠️ still being checked — same caveat]. This document does not resolve the exact figures; the qualitative pattern (a real, substantial, multiply-reported decline in both categories) is well-supported even though the precise percentage is not settled among the sources located this review. The Toronto Police Service's own Public Safety Data Portal (data.torontopolice.on.ca) was identified as the authoritative primary source for both metrics but was not independently fetched and queried this review — flagged in "Open questions" below.

The master briefing document this page inherits from (carried-forward) reports two further baseline figures relevant to the "actual safety" side of the gap, neither independently re-fetched this review: Toronto's overall crime rate as roughly 31% below the national average, and its violent-crime rate roughly 28% below the national average — both flagged [confirm] in that source itself, carried here with the same hedge, ⚠️ still being checked. The same source also documents a real, more recent reversal that the 2025 decline figures above should not be read as erasing: a 2024 spike in gun violence, with shootings up approximately 34% to 461 and 84 homicides — a five-year high — with youth increasingly involved and the average age of those involved reported as dropping from 25 to 20; the master briefing itself flags the specific claim that 58% of those involved were youth as [confirm], a hedge preserved here rather than resolved, ⚠️ still being checked. This is reported for the perception-gap comparison specifically — i.e., as part of the "actual safety" trend line the public perception is being measured against — and not as a violence-prevention program evaluation, which is the distinct territory of the sibling gun-gang-youth-violence-prevention backgrounder. Read together with the 2025 decline figures above, the honest picture is one of real volatility (a sharp 2024 increase followed by reported 2025 declines) rather than a single clean trend line in either direction — which is itself part of why a durable public perception of rising danger can coexist with genuinely improving recent-year statistics.

City safety rankings as a benchmark (dated figure, honestly flagged)

The master briefing document cites Toronto's 2024 ranking on the Economist Intelligence Unit's (EIU) Safe Cities Index as "6th... the safest large North American metro, though down from 2nd in 2021" with a link to the City of Toronto's own Progress Portal page. This review independently fetched that exact City of Toronto URL and found it displays outdated 2017 data (Toronto ranked 4th of 60 cities that year) rather than a 2024 or more recent figure [NEW-CG08] — the City's own webpage has not been updated to reflect the ranking the master briefing cited. A separate live-discovery search this review found a different, more recent claim: "In 2024, a ranking of 60 large cities by The Economist ranked Toronto as the 6th safest major city in the world, and the safest major city in North America," with "Calgary and Vancouver representing Canada in the 5th and 7th positions" [NEW-CG09, ⚠️ still being checked — surfaced via search-result summary, not independently fetched from the EIU's own report or a primary news source reporting on it directly, and notably conflicts internally with itself: Toronto is described as both "6th safest... and safest in North America" while Calgary is separately placed 5th, which would make Calgary safer by rank — this internal inconsistency is not resolved by any source located this review and is flagged as a data-quality concern with this specific figure, not silently smoothed over]. This document therefore treats the specific 2024 Toronto ranking as unconfirmed pending a direct fetch of the EIU's own report or index materials.

The "right responder" model: Toronto Community Crisis Service (TCCS)

The Toronto Community Crisis Service is a non-police, community-led crisis-response program for mental-health-crisis and wellness-check calls, launched in early 2022 as one of the priority actions under the City's SafeTO Community Safety and Well-Being plan. This review independently and directly fetched the City of Toronto's own January 16, 2023 news release reporting the pilot's first published third-party evaluation (https://www.toronto.ca/news/toronto-city-council-receives-toronto-community-crisis-service-mid-year-progress-report-confirming-positive-outcomes-community-impact/), covering the pilot's first six months (March 31–September 30, 2022), conducted by third-party evaluators the Provincial System Support Program and Shkaabe Makwa, both housed at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH) [NEW-CG10]. Per the City's own release, quoting the evaluation directly: "TCCS successfully diverted 78 per cent of 1,530 calls received from 911 with no police involvement." Of a total 2,489 calls received (from 911, 211, and directly to community anchor partners), 84% resulted in mobile teams being dispatched. Emergency-service backup was rarely needed: "TCCS made requests for police attendance during 2.5 per cent of calls attended and requested ambulance attendance during 1.7 per cent of calls attended." Crisis teams completed 485 post-crisis follow-ups within a 48-hour service standard, enrolled more than 25% of service users in post-crisis case management, and made 799 community-based referrals (231 to mental health/substance-use supports, 185 to housing supports, 91 to general healthcare) [NEW-CG10]. The City's 2023 operating budget included a stated 25% funding increase for TCCS "to support enhanced service delivery and strengthened dispatch processes" [NEW-CG10].

More recent, but not independently fetched this review: search-surfaced results indicate TCCS had grown substantially by 2025–2026, with a reported 2025 budget of $31.7 million and a proposed $35.3 million for 2026, over 200 staff, and continuing to report a 78% no-police-involvement diversion rate on 911-originated calls, with planned expansion onto Toronto Transit Commission subway platforms [NEW-CG11, ⚠️ still being checked — surfaced via search-result summary, not independently fetched from a primary City budget document or evaluation report this review]. The consistency of the 78% figure between the original 2023 six-month pilot evaluation and the more recent, unverified 2025-era figures is notable but not independently confirmed as the same metric measured the same way across that multi-year span.

Toronto: the case for and against

Section merged 2026-08-11 from a companion Toronto-specific brief (Lane L2a Toronto brief-merge pass).

FOR — the strongest case that this is a real, addressable data/communication problem:

  • The perception-gap finding itself rests on a directly and independently fetched, dated, methodologically transparent survey (IVR polling, named margin of error, named firm with stated election-prediction accuracy credentials) — not a vague impression or an unsourced claim.
  • The gap is not unique to Toronto within this page’s own data: Peel and York Region residents show the same pattern, with Peel Regional Police reporting only 10 homicide occurrences through October 2025 against 19 for all of 2024, while a majority of Brampton and Mississauga residents still perceived homicides as rising — suggesting a regional, not Toronto-specific, phenomenon that a purely local explanation would not fully account for.
  • The City's own public-facing safety-ranking citation page was found, on direct fetch, to display nine-year-old data (2017 rankings) rather than any current figure — a concrete, fixable example of exactly the kind of stale public communication that could plausibly contribute to residents lacking access to current, authoritative safety data.
  • TCCS's original evaluation used a genuinely independent, credible third-party evaluator (CAMH-affiliated), which is a stronger methodological standard than the self-reported program metrics documented in this repo's sibling gun-gang-youth-violence-prevention backgrounder — suggesting the City has already demonstrated it can commission credible, independent safety-program evaluation when it chooses to.

AGAINST — the strongest case for caution before acting on any single figure:

  • This page’s own discovery could not resolve the exact 2025 Toronto homicide count (conflicting reports of 39 and 42) or the exact auto-theft decline percentage (conflicting reports between roughly 24% and 34%) — meaning the "actual safety" side of the perception-gap comparison is itself not settled with the precision the survey's framing implies, a limitation any policy response should account for rather than treat the specific percentages as fixed facts.
  • The commonly-cited Economist Safe Cities Index ranking for Toronto is genuinely unclear from this review's discovery: the City's own citation page is nine years stale, and a search-surfaced alternative figure (Toronto 6th globally, "safest in North America") is internally inconsistent with a separately-cited Calgary 5th-place figure in the same result — this specific comparator should not be treated as settled without a direct fetch of the EIU's own current report.
  • TCCS's headline 78% diversion-rate figure is confirmed only for a specific, much smaller six-month pilot window in 2022 — its persistence in more recent reporting, at a program roughly ten times larger in budget and newly expanding into transit environments the original evaluation did not cover, has not been independently re-confirmed in this page’s sources, and should not be assumed automatically transferable to the current, much larger program.
  • No source in this page’s discovery independently analyzes why the perception gap exists in the Toronto-area context specifically (media coverage patterns, social-media amplification, visible-disorder effects, or genuine localized experience of specific incidents) — the survey documents the gap's existence and scale convincingly, but this brief does not have independently verified causal evidence to draw on, and any policy response premised on a specific causal story about why the gap exists would be reasoning ahead of this page’s actual evidence base.

Toronto-specific figures: No source in this page’s discovery provides a specific cost figure for conducting or commissioning safety-perception polling at the scale of the Liaison Strategies survey. TCCS's own reported budget figures ($31.7 million 2025, $35.3 million proposed 2026) are carried at “still being checked” status, not independently fetched from a primary City budget document this review. The original 2022 TCCS pilot evaluation's own specific cost was not located in any source this review.

Outcome: confirmed for its original scope (six months, four pilot areas); not independently re-confirmed at the program's current, much larger scale.
  • The Economist Intelligence Unit's Safe Cities Index: an international, standardized comparator ranking mechanism across 60 major cities. Toronto bottom line: A real, independently confirmed gap exists between Toronto-area residents' perception of rising crime and multiple, if imprecisely reconciled, reports of substantial 2025 declines in homicide and auto theft — a pattern that replicates across neighbouring Peel and York Regions and is therefore unlikely to be a purely Toronto-specific artifact; at the same time, the City's own public safety-data communication (an EIU ranking citation page displaying nine-year-old data) and this page’s own inability to pin down a single authoritative current homicide or auto-theft figure across otherwise-credible sources suggest the "actual safety" side of this comparison deserves the same scrutiny and clarity the perception side has already received from independent polling.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

This section documents that a tension exists in the evidence on each side, not which side is correct.

A directly, independently confirmed perception gap sits alongside internally inconsistent "actual safety" figures. The Liaison Strategies survey data is the strongest, most directly sourced material in this backgrounder — a named, credentialed polling firm's own published, dated, methodologically documented survey. But the crime-trend figures it is measured against (Toronto's exact 2025 homicide count, exact auto-theft percentage decline, and current EIU Safe Cities Index rank) are themselves inconsistently reported across the sources this review located, with real numeric disagreement between sources on the homicide count (39 vs. 42) and auto-theft decline (24% vs. 34%), and an internally self-contradictory Safe Cities Index ranking claim. The qualitative finding — that a real, substantial safety improvement occurred in 2025 while public perception moved the opposite direction — is well-supported by the direction of multiple independent sources even where their exact figures disagree; this document treats the direction as well-supported and the precise figures as unsettled, rather than picking one source's number and presenting it as more authoritative than the others.

A "right responder" model with strong six-month pilot evaluation data, but a multi-year gap in this page’s own independent verification. The 78% diversion rate is a real, third-party-evaluated (CAMH-affiliated) figure — but it dates to a specific six-month window in 2022. This document did not independently verify whether the same rate has held consistently as the program scaled from a four-area pilot to its current, much larger 2025–2026 scope, or whether the evaluation methodology remained consistent — the persistence of the identical "78%" figure across multiple years in search results is treated here as worth flagging for independent verification, not as automatic confirmation that the metric has been re-measured and re-confirmed at each stage of the program's growth, rather than simply carried forward as a citation.

A "right responder" model exists partly because trust in the default responder (police) has been damaged, and that history bears directly on this page’s own subject. The master briefing document this page inherits from (carried-forward) documents that Toronto's own policing history includes aggressive, discriminatory practices — carding and the Toronto Anti-Violence Intervention Strategy (TAVIS) — that "traumatized racialized communities and eroded the trust safety depends on." This is not independently re-verified or re-fetched this review (⚠️ still being checked), but it is restored here because it is directly relevant, not tangential: a documented history of over-policing in racialized communities is part of the causal backdrop for why a non-police "right responder" model has public and political support, and it names a real, two-sided equity pattern the master briefing itself draws out — the same communities most affected by violence are frequently also the communities most affected by discriminatory over-policing, so a credible response has to address both the harm of violence and the harm of over-policing rather than treating only one as real. This document does not have independently fetched, current-pass source material on carding/TAVIS's own record or any reform outcomes since — it is carried forward here as an inherited historical claim requiring independent verification, not as newly confirmed fact, and the general policing-reform and oversight territory this touches belongs to the sibling police-accountability-oversight backgrounder, which this document does not duplicate.

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. No binding international treaty or UN framework directly governs public perception-of-safety measurement or non-police crisis response design — this is a domestic public-safety/service-delivery area without a clear treaty anchor. No connection is manufactured here; none was found.

2. 2–3 best global comparators. (a) CAHOOTS (Crisis Assistance Helping Out On The Streets), Eugene, Oregon — widely cited internationally (including, per general awareness reflected in this repo's own baseline benchmark documents) as an originating model for the "right responder" concept TCCS itself belongs to; this document did not independently fetch a current CAHOOTS evaluation this review and names it only as a checkable, real, named program worth a future dedicated fetch, not as a verified comparator with confirmed current figures. (b) The Economist Intelligence Unit's Safe Cities Index itself functions as an international comparator mechanism, ranking 60 major world cities on a standardized methodology (76 safety-related indicators across health, digital, infrastructure, and personal-safety security, per the 2017 methodology description independently fetched this review) — useful precisely because it gives Toronto's "actual safety" claims an external, cross-city benchmark rather than relying only on local, potentially locally-biased reporting; its current (post-2017) data was not independently confirmed this review, a real limitation for its use as a comparator here.

3. What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. The most concrete, transferable and already-partially-adopted element is TCCS's own third-party evaluation design — engaging an external evaluator (CAMH-affiliated Provincial System Support Program and Shkaabe Makwa) from the pilot's outset, rather than relying solely on internal City reporting, which is a specific, nameable methodological choice distinct from (and stronger than) the self-reported metrics documented in the sibling gun-gang-youth-violence-prevention backgrounder's own program table. This document notes this descriptively as an internal Toronto practice worth extending (e.g., to the TCCS's own more recent, not-yet-independently-verified 2025–2026 figures, and to other City-run safety programs), not as an imported foreign model.
