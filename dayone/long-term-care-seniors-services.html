Long-Term Care: Toronto Does It Well, the Province Hasn't Finished Fixing What Failed — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/long-term-care-seniors-services.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/long-term-care-seniors-services.md, research/cards/long-term-care-seniors-services.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Ontario's Long-Term Care COVID-19 Commission spent a year and released a 300-plus-page report telling the province exactly what went wrong and, largely, what to do about it. Toronto's own 10 long-term care homes are, on the numbers, one of the system's better performers. This brief reads what the Commission found, what the City's own homes report, and what the Auditor General has since confirmed actually happened — and doesn't happen — with the response.

What the Commission actually found

Long-term care residents made up half a percent of Ontario's population and 61% of its COVID-19 deaths in 2020. The Commission's own explanation wasn't a mystery illness or an unforeseeable shock: "chronic underfunding, severe staffing shortages, outdated infrastructure and poor oversight" — problems it said plainly were not news to anyone who worked in or advocated for the sector. The province had let 90% of its post-SARS emergency PPE stockpile expire and be destroyed, without replacing it. When outbreaks hit, some homes lost up to 80% of their staff overnight, either to infection or to fear.

The Commission didn't dodge the for-profit question, but it also didn't take the simplest available shot at it. It pointed out that "for-profit" and "not-for-profit" are legal tax categories, not a measure of how a home is actually run, and proposed splitting the two things that get conflated: let private capital keep building the buildings, but require that whoever actually delivers care be "mission-driven" rather than purely commercial. Independent peer-reviewed research since then (a CMAJ study of every Ontario home during the first wave) found for-profit homes did have worse outcomes — a 16.6% COVID-19 positivity rate against 5.4% at municipally-owned homes — though the same study attributes much of that gap to older buildings and chain ownership specifically, which is consistent with, not a contradiction of, what the Commission itself said.

Toronto's own homes: a real bright spot, not spin

The City runs 10 long-term care homes directly, currently at 97.4% occupancy with over 7,500 people on the waitlist. On the two quality indicators the City's own division has spent the last two years actively working to improve — residents put on antipsychotics without a diagnosis, and residents who fall — Toronto beats the provincial average by a wide, consistent margin: 14.2% versus 19.4% on antipsychotic use, 9.9% versus 15.4% on falls, holding steady across four straight quarters. Families and residents report 93% overall satisfaction. This isn't a cherry-picked number; it's the same measure published every quarter, showing the same gap every time.

What we couldn't confirm — and that's the point of saying so

The province's single biggest post-Commission promise was four hours of direct hands-on care per resident per day, phased in by March 2025. We looked for confirmation that it was actually met by that deadline. We didn't find it. What we found instead was a narrower, related target — 36 minutes a day specifically from allied health professionals — confirmed as exceeded throughout the plan period. That's a real result, but it is not the same claim as the headline four-hour target being met, and nothing in this pass lets us say the bigger number was hit.

The Auditor General's own tracking, as of November 2025, found only 24% of its 2023 recommendations on resident-centred care fully implemented — five years after the Commission's report, four years after the province's own staffing plan began. More than 30% of homes visited had been denied funding for dedicated behavioural-support staff. And the most recent province-wide figure we could find for how many of the promised new long-term-care beds have actually opened is from August 2022: fewer than 2,000 of roughly 60,000 planned. We looked for something newer. We couldn't find it, which is itself worth knowing.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't resolve whether Toronto's own strong local numbers reflect something structurally different about how the City runs its homes, or whether it's simply better-resourced than the provincial average for reasons this pass didn't isolate. It doesn't confirm whether the Commission's own proposed reform — separate the building from the care — has been adopted anywhere in Ontario. And it doesn't update the stale 2022 bed-pipeline number against Toronto's own waitlist, which has grown to over 7,500 applications as of last year.

The three moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Make Toronto's own staffing-hours data as public as its antipsychotic and falls data already is (RC-LTC-01) — the City already publishes two hard quality numbers every quarter; a third, more central one is missing from public view. 2. Ask the Province directly whether it adopted its own Commission's construction/care-separation idea (RC-LTC-02) — a specific, named 2021 recommendation whose current status this pass could not find published anywhere. 3. Ask for a current bed-pipeline number (RC-LTC-03) — the most recent one available is from August 2022, and a system this consequential shouldn't be running on a four-year-old snapshot.

None of these invent a new number or a new fight. They ask the province and the City to publish, plainly, whether the promises already made in public have actually been kept.
