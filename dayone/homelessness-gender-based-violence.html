Gender-Based Violence and Toronto's Homelessness System — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/homelessness-gender-based-violence.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/homelessness-gender-based-violence.md, research/cards/homelessness-gender-based-violence.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Women in Toronto's homeless population are dying, on median, at an age less than half that of the general population's women. The gap isn't marginal — it's structural, and it shows up in exactly the part of the shelter system that's supposed to serve them.

What's actually happening

The median age at death for women experiencing homelessness in Toronto is 38, against 85 for women generally — a 47-year gap, wider than the 28-year gap for men. A 2026 peer-reviewed study found 76% of women's deaths in this population were overdose deaths, compared with 48% of men's. That's not a small disparity sitting inside a larger, similar pattern — women are dying of overdose at a meaningfully higher rate within the same homeless population.

Here's where it gets structural: the same study found women hold access to only about 18% of adult shelter beds in Toronto, through women-only shelters — the smallest bed-capacity share of any shelter type, even though women make up 41% of the homeless population. And harm reduction, the service most directly relevant to that 76% overdose figure, is available in only 26% of women-only shelters — the lowest rate across every shelter type in the system. The shelter type most available to women is the one least equipped with the service most likely to save their lives.

The funding exists. Whether it reached Toronto doesn't.

The federal government committed $315.4 million over seven years, starting in 2021, specifically for GBV-survivor housing, plus a separate $250 million reallocation toward roughly 560 units of transitional housing and shelter space. That's real money, real intent, at the national level.

What's missing is any confirmation that it reached Toronto. This project checked — including the federal government's own March 2026 National Housing Strategy progress report — and found no reconciliation of either 2021 commitment to a Toronto- or Ontario-specific deployment figure. Alberta built a direct $7,200/year cash benefit for survivors with this same federal money. British Columbia built a $4.6 million provider-capacity and staff-training program with it. Neither of those is a criticism of Ontario — it's a statement that this pass could not find Ontario's own equivalent, and that gap is worth someone actually chasing down.

What already works, somewhere else

Washington State's Domestic Violence Housing First model isn't a theory — it's a program with a real, published two-year evaluation following more than 400 survivors and their children. It gives survivors flexible funding and housing-focused advocacy instead of routing them through a fixed shelter-bed system. The evaluation found recipients were more likely to get and keep stable housing, reported less continued abuse, and had better mental health outcomes — and the effect held across race and ethnicity. That's the single most concrete, evidence-backed idea this file surfaces: a funding model that doesn't depend on there being a bed in the specific ~18%-of-capacity shelter type that's already thinnest on the ground.

The Indigenous connection is not a footnote

The National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls named housing insecurity and homelessness directly in its own account of the conditions surrounding violence against Indigenous women, girls, and 2SLGBTQQIA people — not something this project is inferring, but something survivors and family members told the Inquiry directly, across 2,380 participants and two years of hearings. Toronto already has a real, evaluated example of what Indigenous-led housing delivery looks like: Na-Me-Res's Mino Kaanjigoowin program, where a 2019 evaluation found two-thirds of participants achieved positive housing transitions. This document doesn't repeat the deeper Indigenous-specific research this project has already done elsewhere — that lives in the homelessness-indigenous-overrepresentation leaf — but the connection to this file's own GBV finding is real, sourced, and not decorative.

What this doesn't settle

This does not resolve whether Ontario has its own version of Alberta's or BC's delivery model — it might, and this pass simply didn't find it, which is a different claim than "it doesn't exist." It doesn't establish a causal link between the unreconciled federal funding and the shelter-capacity gap; both are real and documented, but no source ties them together directly. And the international comparator's own effect-size numbers are sourced from the program's public materials, not a direct read of the underlying peer-reviewed article — worth a harder look before anyone treats those specific percentages as settled.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Pilot a flexible, survivor-directed housing fund modeled on Washington State's Domestic Violence Housing First program (RC-GBV-01) — the most concrete, evidence-backed design idea in this file. 2. Get CMHC and Women and Gender Equality Canada to publish a Toronto/Ontario-specific reconciliation of the 2021 funding commitments against what's actually been deployed (RC-GBV-02) — a diagnostic ask, not a spending ask, aimed at replacing an unreconciled national figure with a real local one.

Neither of these invents a new number or a new crisis. They read what's already documented — including the parts, like the 18%-of-capacity figure, that don't have an easy answer yet.
