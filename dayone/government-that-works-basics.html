A Government That Works: Service Delivery Basics — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from: our research file for that page and that page's recommendation cards. No claim in this document goes beyond what those two files already establish. Provenance: earlier research plus this review, as carried from the backgrounder/cards; see those documents for full citations.

The case

Most people experience city government through the pothole, the permit, and the 311 call — and Toronto has real strengths to build on: a 311 line running 24/7 in more than 180 languages, and, as of this review's discovery, genuine, dated movement on the specific gaps this page’s own founding research flagged [backgrounder, "Current state"]. The City launched a public 311 Service Standard Dashboard in October 2025, is actively expanding an AI-assisted predictive pilot for pothole management, and grew its digital permitting program (ApplyTO) through 2025 to cover more building types [backgrounder, "311," "Predictive/proactive service," "Digital service modernization"].

The honest catch

This page’s original research made one specific, load-bearing critique: Toronto's open-311 data reportedly covered only 30-35% of requests and 6 of 45 divisions — a transparency hole. That original research was honest enough to flag its own figure as unconfirmed against a primary source, and this review could not resolve it either way. What changed is that a real dashboard now exists where there used to be only an unresolved question — a genuine improvement, but not proof the original gap has closed by any specific amount [backgrounder, "Key tensions / tradeoffs"].

The same pattern holds elsewhere. Permit-processing figures found this review suggest actual turnaround still runs longer than the Building Code Act's own statutory maximums, even as a real digital-modernization program expands. And the pothole-filling numbers keep growing — over 250,000 filled in 2025 — but nothing in this page’s evidence separates how much of that is the predictive pilot actually working versus simply more crews doing more reactive filling. Several of the newest figures in this document (the 2025 pothole total, ApplyTO's specific "28 days faster" claim, the permit-turnaround ranges) come from secondary, aggregator-style sources rather than the City's own primary data, and are flagged as needing confirmation rather than presented as settled [backgrounder, "What the evidence does and doesn't support"].

Two places to start

Publish the actual coverage number, not an unconfirmed one. The City already runs a real 311 transparency dashboard as of October 2025 — the missing piece is a specific, numeric, regularly-updated statement of what share of 311 requests and how many of the City's 45 divisions it actually covers, replacing a figure that has circulated publicly without ever being confirmed against a primary source. This is a documentation addition to a program that already exists and is already funded — genuinely low-cost, genuinely high-clarity [card a recommendation card].

Prove the prediction is doing something, before scaling it further. Toronto is expanding an AI/forecasting pilot for pothole management, and the raw output numbers look good — but output volume alone doesn't show whether prediction is finding problems before residents report them, or whether the city is just spending more on the same reactive approach. A targeted evaluation, comparing prediction-flagged locations against a fair comparison group, would answer that question directly before the City commits to expanding the approach into new service categories [card a recommendation card].

What's not yet settled

Neither the original 30-35% transparency figure nor its replacement has been confirmed against a primary source — this document does not pretend the question is closed just because a dashboard now exists. The predictive pilot's own causal effect on outcomes is genuinely unknown, not just under-documented — nobody has yet isolated it from the general growth in maintenance spending. And several of the newest, most specific figures in this page’s evidence — the 2025 annual pothole count, ApplyTO's turnaround-reduction claim, current permit-processing time ranges — rest on secondary sources that should be checked against the City's own data before anyone repeats them as settled fact. The deepest question from this page’s original research also remains open: how much of Toronto's service performance gap is underfunding, and how much is management — this review's discovery of new programs doesn't resolve that question, it just shows the City is actively working on delivery in the meantime.
