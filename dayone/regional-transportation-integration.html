Regional Transportation Integration — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Status: DRAFT · Layer: L-PLATFORM Built from: our research file for that page (L4) + that page's recommendation cards (a recommendation card, a recommendation card). No claim below is freehand — every figure traces to a card or the backgrounder it draws from.

---

Toronto transit riders already live with the consequences of a governance choice nobody put to them directly. The TTC answers to City Council. The subway lines actually under construction right now — the Ontario Line, the Scarborough extension, the Yonge North extension — answer to a board the province appoints entirely on its own. And the fare discount that saves a rider real money crossing from a 905 bus onto the TTC works automatically for some agencies and requires a manual form for others, depending on which side of an invisible administrative line your commute happens to cross.

One: give the region's own mayors a real seat at the table, the way Metro Vancouver already does. Toronto has no joint body of GTHA mayors with approval power over Metrolinx's fare decisions or multi-year investment plans. Metro Vancouver does — a Mayors' Council of the region's 21 municipalities formally approves those decisions, and the province there is limited to appointing just two of the transit board's seats. That's not a hypothetical reform; it's a functioning Canadian model, checked directly against TransLink's own governance page. Toronto City Council should formally ask the Province to build something like it here — not to cut the Province out, but to give the municipalities whose residents actually ride this system a formal, statutory voice in decisions currently made without one (a recommendation card).

Two: stop making regional fare integration a matter of which agency you happen to be crossing. The One Fare Program works well and automatically for TTC riders connecting to GO or four named 905 systems. But Guelph Transit riders get a different, more restrictive deal — a manual sign-up process and a 40-minute connection window instead of One Fare's two-to-three hours. Standardize it: put every bilateral fare arrangement like Guelph's onto the same automatic mechanism everyone else already gets (a recommendation card).

What we don't know, said out loud. We don't know if Edmonton's regional transit commission — approved by Alberta in 2021 as a genuine municipal-led alternative model — actually finished its planned transition to a unified authority; the only source we have is the original announcement, not a current check. We don't know for certain that Calgary's regional coordinating board was actually dissolved in 2025, or why, if it was — that came from a single source description we didn't independently verify. We don't know how many other municipal agencies besides Guelph have their own bilateral fare deals with GO Transit, or how many riders depend on them. And we don't know whether the One Fare Program's own funding — a real $128 million commitment for this fiscal year — has been extended past this year, because the province's own fiscal watchdog said this was the last year it had committed to, and nobody has said otherwise since.

The throughline: Toronto-region transit integration is real, not fake — money is being spent, fares are being connected, agreements are being signed. But it runs through a structure that gives the region's own municipalities less formal say than a comparable Canadian region already gives its own, and it delivers a more consistent experience to some riders than others depending on which agency lines they cross. Both are fixable without inventing anything new — the fixes already exist somewhere in this country. We just haven't asked for them here.
