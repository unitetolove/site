Food Security as an Income Problem — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto's own food banks and its own research partners have already published the diagnosis. This brief reads what's already out there and shows what the City is, and isn't, doing about it.

What's actually happening

Toronto food banks recorded 4,124,313 visits in the year covered by Who's Hungry 2025 — a 340% increase since before the pandemic, and the highest number ever recorded. More than one in ten Torontonians now rely on food banks, up from one in twenty just three years ago. The shift isn't just volume: for the first time since 2020, most visits now come from people returning again and again rather than new clients showing up for the first time, and 112,554 people turned to a food bank for the first time this year alone.

The research behind Toronto's own December 2024 emergency declaration says this isn't a food problem. The University of Toronto's PROOF program, the authoritative Canadian source on this question, published data in May 2025 showing 25.5% of people in the ten provinces lived in a food-insecure household in 2024 — the third consecutive annual record — and stated plainly: "all the research points to the need to provide low-income households with more adequate and secure incomes." Food insecurity concentrates hardest among Black households (46.7%), Indigenous people living off-reserve (39.9%), and children (nearly a third of all kids nationally).

The lever the City actually has, and what it costs

Income policy sits almost entirely with the federal and provincial governments. What the City controls directly is school food, and it's moving on it: a four-phase rollout to universal morning meals by the 2026/2027 school year and a strategic path to universal lunch by 2030. The City's own June 2025 report to Executive Committee puts a real number on what's left: $21.721 million to bring the next 155 unfunded school communities online, split between $7.492 million in capital and $13.454 million in annual operating cost.

The honest number sitting next to that commitment: Ontario's own per-student contribution to school food hasn't moved since 2015. It's currently $17 a year — nine cents a day — the second-lowest of any province or territory in the country, behind only Saskatchewan. The federal National School Food Program covers less than 4% of elementary meal costs nationally. Toronto's own programs currently get government funding for only 37% of what they actually spend on food, and the City's own report says plainly that shortfall "forces many programs to reduce service days, close early, or offer smaller portions" — right now, before the expansion is even finished.

Where the two honest sides of the argument actually are

For the school food investment: it's real, it's costed, and it delivers a direct, quantified relief effect to families right now — an estimated $129 to $378 a month in grocery savings depending on household size, according to the City's own numbers. This is squarely inside municipal authority and doesn't require permission from anyone else.

For staying honest about what school food can't fix: food banks have expanded their capacity substantially — Daily Bread's own food-purchasing budget grew from $1.5 million before the pandemic to $29 million this year — and food-insecurity prevalence kept rising anyway, in the same years, nationally. That's exactly the pattern PROOF's own research says you'd expect if the underlying problem is income, not food supply or food-charity capacity. Toronto's own emergency declaration says this too: it explicitly calls on the federal and provincial governments to raise and index income supports (EI, OAS, Ontario Works, ODSP, the Canada Disability Benefit), because the City knows the deepest fix isn't its own to make.

Both of these are true at once. Fund the school food gap because it's real, it's yours to fund, and it helps families today — and don't let doing that substitute for the income fight that's the actual cure, because the City's own cited research says charity and food-access programs, however well-funded, haven't moved the underlying number.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't confirm the specific "3 to 7 times more food-insecure people than food-bank users" ratio that circulates in this project's own earlier research — that figure traces back to PROOF's own published work, but this review didn't independently re-fetch the specific page behind it, so it's carried forward as inherited, not freshly re-verified. It doesn't confirm whether a newer PROOF release covering 2025 data (referenced on the page this review did fetch, but not itself pulled) shows things getting better, worse, or flat since the 2024 numbers this brief relies on. It doesn't establish whether Toronto-specific food-insecurity prevalence, as opposed to the national and provincial figures PROOF publishes, is even available at the city level in Statistics Canada's public data — a real gap if anyone wants to track this locally rather than by proxy through food-bank visit counts. And on the money question this whole brief keeps circling back to: nobody has published who, if anyone, actually profits from food insecurity persisting in Toronto specifically. There's a live, credible thread — Canada's Competition Bureau has found the grocery sector concentrated among five companies, and there's ongoing public dispute over how much grocery-chain profits have grown since the pandemic — but it hasn't been run through this project's own entity-registration process, so it stays outside this file rather than getting cited as if it were.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Fund the Student Nutrition Program's remaining $21.721 million gap directly, on the City's own already-planned timeline, rather than letting a provincial funding freeze that's held since 2015 set the pace (a recommendation card). 2. Track the actual food-insecurity rate, not just food-bank visit counts, as the City's own emergency-response metric — visit counts can rise for reasons other than worsening need, and the City's own cited research treats them as an imperfect proxy for the thing that actually needs fixing (a recommendation card).

Neither move invents a new number or a new program. Both ask the City to act on the diagnosis its own emergency declaration already accepted.
