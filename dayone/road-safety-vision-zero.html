Road Safety and Vision Zero — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Status: DRAFT · Layer: L-PLATFORM Built from: our research file for that page (L4) + that page's recommendation cards (a recommendation card, a recommendation card). No claim below is freehand — every figure traces to a card or the backgrounder it draws from.

---

Toronto's Vision Zero plan has a real number behind it: 39 people died on the city's roads in 2025, half as many as when the plan started in 2016. That's not spin — it's the City's own dashboard, and it holds up against nine years of data. But the same data says something the headline number doesn't: 83% of serious injuries and deaths happen on just 21% of the road network — the arterials. And eight months ago, the province pulled the one enforcement tool with a named study behind it, on a "cash grab" theory, promising $210 million in traffic-calming money instead — but what's actually landed on Toronto's streets from that promise isn't yet clear.

One: point the money at the roads the data actually points to. Toronto knows exactly where people are getting hurt — arterial roads, four times over-represented relative to their share of the network. Make that finding a standing rule for where safety infrastructure dollars go next, not just a statistic in an annual report. Seniors are also over-represented in pedestrian collisions by nearly double their population share; the same targeted approach should account for that directly (a recommendation card).

Two: don't let a provincial press release be the end of the sentence. The province banned automated speed cameras in November 2025, calling them a revenue grab. Maybe some of them were. But a Toronto-specific study from SickKids and Toronto Metropolitan University found cameras cut school-zone speeding by 45% and cut the worst speeding — 20 km/h or more over the limit — by 87% [independently re-verified 2026-07-16, corrected from 88%]. The camera company itself said the quiet part out loud: it didn't choose to leave Ontario, the law made it leave. The province did name a number and a date for the replacement — $210 million, of which $42 million was released immediately to municipalities (Toronto's specific share is reported as $10 million but was not confirmed against a primary source as of 2026-07-16) — but Toronto should demand a public, corridor-by-corridor accounting of where that money is actually going, starting with school zones, where the removed tool's own evidence was strongest (a recommendation card). A Toronto staff report considered by council in mid-2026 found speeding had in fact increased at 101 of 104 comparable locations since the ban — the first hard local evidence of the ban's on-the-ground effect [independently re-verified 2026-07-16].

What we don't know, said out loud. We don't know exactly how Toronto's $10 million (or any further provincial instalment) has actually been spent on the ground — the announcement is confirmed, the on-street deployment is not, and even the $10 million Toronto-specific figure itself remains unconfirmed against a primary source. We now have a direct read of the SickKids/TMU study itself confirming the 45%/87% figures [independently re-verified 2026-07-16] — the camera company's press release was accurate other than a minor 88%-vs-87% rounding slip. We don't know if Toronto's own infrastructure spending is already quietly following the arterial-road pattern the data points to, or if this is a genuine gap. And most of Toronto's own road-safety data — 40 of 46 facts in our claims register — is still sitting at "checked once" rather than "checked twice," the more thorough bar this project holds real claims to.

The throughline: Vision Zero's headline number is real, and it's worth defending. But a plan that's cut deaths in half owes it to the next round of victims to spend its next dollar exactly where the data says people are actually dying, and to say plainly when a province-level decision takes away a tool that was working rather than let a vague promise stand in for a real one.
