Cycling & Bike Share Scaling — Day-One Paper

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/cycling-bikeshare-scaling.md

Status: DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from research/cards/cycling-bikeshare-scaling.md (RC-CBS-01-RC-CBS-02) and research/backgrounders/cycling-bikeshare-scaling.md; every claim below traces to one of those two documents.

What a mayor does in week one on this file

Toronto doesn't need to be convinced cycling works — Bike Share's ridership has grown every year for a decade, from about 665,000 trips in 2015 to 7.8 million in 2025 (RC-CBS-01, backgrounder). The week-one job is narrower and more concrete: deliver the program that's already authorized, on corridors with real death and injury records, and be straight with residents about the one constraint this office does not control.

1. Fund and deliver the full 2025-2027 bikeway program, including the contested segments. Council's November 2025 report authorized 20.5km of new bikeways at an estimated $30.4 million, on corridors including Kingston Road (1,910 collisions, 22 serious injuries, 6 fatalities over 2014-2024) and Trethewey Drive (1,315 collisions, 11 serious injuries over a decade) (RC-CBS-01). At least one segment — Kingston Road — is genuinely contested: roughly 51% of surveyed residents were unsupportive, 43% supportive. Week one is not about avoiding that fight; it's about funding the whole authorized program rather than letting the contested pieces quietly disappear from the capital budget while the uncontroversial ones proceed (RC-CBS-01).

2. Tell residents plainly what the City does and doesn't control on bike lanes right now. Every single project in the current program avoids removing a vehicle travel lane — not by accident, on the evidence, but because doing so would risk triggering the same provincial law, Bill 212, that already forced (and, for now, a court has blocked) the removal of 19km of existing lanes on Bloor, Yonge, and University (RC-CBS-02, backgrounder). A Superior Court ruled in July 2025 that the province's own removal plan was arbitrary and unconstitutional — a real win, not just an advocacy claim — but the province's appeal, heard in January 2026, was still undecided as of the most recent information available this pass. A second schedule of that same bill, Bill 60 (the same omnibus law that also reformed the Landlord and Tenant Board, in a separate schedule), separately restricts any future Toronto bike lane that would remove a car lane, regardless of how that appeal comes out. Week one: say this out loud to residents rather than let them assume City Hall has the authority it doesn't currently have.

3. Stand up a City-run public tracker for the Bloor/Yonge/University litigation. Right now, the only public source tracking whether those 19km of lanes remain legally protected is Cycle Toronto, the advocacy organization that brought the case — the City itself was not even a party to the litigation (RC-CBS-02). This costs the City almost nothing and gives residents who ride those specific lanes daily a City-sourced answer to "are these still legal?" instead of relying on one advocacy group's own updates (RC-CBS-02).

What this office does not control, and won't pretend to

The province, not the City, currently sets the outer boundary of what protected-lane network Toronto can build. Bill 212 already forced one 19km removal fight into court; Bill 60 closes the door on new lane-removal-based construction going forward regardless of that case's outcome (backgrounder). Toronto City Council has already voted four separate times, under two mayors, to approve the Bloor West lanes specifically, and passed a resolution opposing Bill 212 as jurisdictional overreach — and still had "limited options," in Cycle Toronto's own words, because "cities are a creature of the province." Nothing in this paper changes that; it names it.

The honest bottom line

Toronto has proven the demand (a decade of continuous Bike Share growth) and has a funded, Council-authorized program targeting real, documented safety problems — both squarely within this office's own authority to deliver. What this office cannot promise is the size of the network Toronto will be allowed to build beyond what's already authorized, because that answer sits with a provincial court and a provincial legislature, not City Hall. The right posture is to build everything currently authorized, including the contested pieces, while being plain with residents about exactly where the City's authority currently ends.

Uncertainties this paper inherits from its cards

Whether the Cycle Toronto v. Ontario appeal has been decided since this pass's sources (current to June 17, 2026) is unknown here. The specific ridership-growth-from-protected-lanes figures this paper's underlying case partly rests on (21%-171%, the Bloor Street jump) are carried from an inherited source at its own confidence level, not independently re-confirmed. None of these gaps are resolved here — they are named so this paper isn't read as more certain than its own sources are.
