Immigration, Mutual Learning, and Integration — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Status: DRAFT · Layer: L-PLATFORM Built from: our research file for that page (L4) + that page's recommendation cards (a recommendation card, a recommendation card). No claim below is freehand — every figure traces to a card or the backgrounder it draws from.

---

Most of the time, when a city talks about "integrating" newcomers, the question underneath is one-directional: how do they adapt to us? There's a real, internationally used alternative to that question, and it turns out well over 160 cities and regions have already formally adopted it — including six municipalities right here in Canada. Toronto, on the evidence gathered here, hasn't.

One: this isn't a vague feel-good idea — it's a real framework, with a real name, and a real track record. Since 2008, the Council of Europe has run something called the Intercultural Cities Programme — cities that formally commit to a specific idea: not just letting different cultures coexist side by side, but actively building exchange between them, in both directions. It's grown to well over 160 member cities and regions, and it comes with a structured self-assessment tool cities can use to check their own progress rather than just declaring victory. This is real and checkable, not aspiration.

Two: Toronto already brands itself around diversity — but that's not the same thing as this framework, and other Canadian cities have already joined it. Toronto has long promoted a "diversity is our strength" civic identity, and the City does run real newcomer programming (a Newcomer Office, credential-mentoring partnerships, and similar). None of that is nothing. But nothing found in this research shows the City has ever formally looked at, or adopted, the specific Intercultural Cities approach — and Toronto's existing newcomer programs are mostly built to help newcomers adapt here, not to structure exchange in both directions. Those are different things, and conflating them would be dishonest (a recommendation card). Six Quebec municipalities, including Montreal, are already confirmed members of this exact framework — so this isn't a hypothetical import from Europe with no North American track record; it's something other Canadian cities have already done (a recommendation card).

Three: the honest starting move is to check the fit, not to assume it. Before recommending Toronto join anything, the responsible first step is a straightforward scoping question: what would participation actually involve, and what can Toronto learn from how Montreal and the other Quebec member cities approached it? Non-European membership is already confirmed open — Canada, Australia, Japan, Mexico, and others already participate — so that part is no longer a live uncertainty. That's a small, low-cost piece of homework — not a commitment — and it's the kind of thing that should happen before anyone builds a bigger program on an assumption nobody's checked (a recommendation card).

Four: the City already has the convening tools to pilot something small, right now. Toronto's existing Newcomer Office and its partnership network already bring people together for programming. Nothing about running a handful of deliberately two-way exchange events — not a festival, not an orientation session, but something actually designed for exchange in both directions — requires new infrastructure. It requires deciding to try it, using rooms and staff time the City already has (a recommendation card).

What we don't know, said out loud. This is one of the more honest gaps in this whole research project: the underlying case for "mutual learning" — that newcomers bring community and family traditions that could help a lonelier, more isolated society — is a real, coherent argument, but it's a conceptual one, not a proven one. The briefing behind this paper says so itself: this is "largely conceptual and values-based," with far fewer hard numbers than almost every other topic in this research library. We don't know if a Toronto pilot would actually produce measurable results. We now know that Quebec's own municipalities (Montreal, Sherbrooke, Drummondville, Gatineau, Repentigny, Rimouski) are already formal members of this exact Council of Europe framework, coordinated through a network called RÉMIRI since 2021 — so the open question isn't whether this fits a Canadian context at all, but whether it specifically fits Toronto's. And we don't know whether Toronto has quietly looked at any of this before and decided not to pursue it, for reasons this research didn't uncover.

The throughline: a real, working international model for "integration goes both ways" exists, other Canadian cities have already joined it, and Toronto — a city that already talks about diversity as a defining strength — hasn't formally engaged it. The honest next step isn't a big new program. It's checking whether the idea fits, learning from Montreal and the other Quebec cities that have already done this, and trying a small, real pilot to see what actually happens when it's tested here instead of just talked about.
