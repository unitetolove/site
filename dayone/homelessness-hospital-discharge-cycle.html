Discharged Into Homelessness — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/homelessness-hospital-discharge-cycle.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/homelessness-hospital-discharge-cycle.md, research/cards/homelessness-hospital-discharge-cycle.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

A housed person leaving hospital goes home. A person experiencing homelessness leaving hospital has nowhere the system considers "home" — and what happens next is expensive, measurable, and, according to the people who study it, mostly preventable.

The numbers, straight from the national health-data agency

Canada's national health-data body tracked nearly 30,000 hospital stays across the country in a single year that were coded specifically for homelessness. Each one cost, on average, more than double what an ordinary hospital stay costs — $16,785 versus $7,803. Ninety-three percent of those admissions came through the emergency department, not a scheduled or referred visit, and the data agency's own read on that number is blunt: it suggests people aren't getting primary care until they're sick enough to end up in an ER. In Toronto specifically, one teaching hospital's own five-year study found homeless patients cost about $2,500 more per stay than housed patients, even after accounting for age, sex, and how resource-intensive the case was. For psychiatric admissions specifically, people discharged without stable housing come back within 30 days at nearly double the rate of everyone else — 17% versus 10% — and they're more than 50% less likely to make it to a follow-up appointment with a psychiatrist.

Worth flagging for anyone tracking this over time: the data agency tightened its own coding rules in March 2025, so a hospital stay now only gets coded as "homelessness" if the person is currently homeless, not formerly or at-risk. That's a good change for data quality, but it means any future count that looks different from the 2022-2023 figures above needs to account for the stricter definition before anyone calls it a trend.

One hospital already fixed this, and it's spreading

St. Michael's Hospital in Toronto built a program around one simple idea: put someone on staff whose entire job is walking a homeless patient through discharge — not a doctor or nurse, but someone experienced specifically in homelessness, who helps arrange a shelter bed, follow-up appointments, medication, and supplies. It's called Navigator, and it started small. As of this year, it's grown to over 1,000 patients served — and it's no longer just a Toronto program. A partnership with a national retailer funded its expansion to a hospital in Vancouver, and a launch in Montreal was announced for later in 2025. A separate, independent study out of England found essentially the same idea — non-clinical, housing-focused discharge support instead of a purely medical approach — was dramatically more cost-effective than the alternatives, by a factor of more than ten.

The part nobody's fixed

Here's the uncomfortable fact underneath all of this: Ontario doesn't have a provincial policy for any of it. Researchers who looked specifically at this found, in their own words, that there's no provincial strategy for discharging homeless patients from hospitals, and it's hard to even find a hospital that's published its own guidelines on the subject. Interviews with hospital workers, shelter staff, and other frontline people in Toronto turned up the same story from a different angle: patients fall through the cracks because hospitals are under pressure to move people out quickly, and shelters have their own eligibility rules that don't always line up with who a hospital is trying to discharge. Nobody designed this gap on purpose. It's what happens when nobody owns the problem.

So the honest picture is: one hospital built something that works, and it's now working in three cities instead of one. But it took a corporate partnership to make that happen, not a provincial policy — and no second Toronto hospital, despite Toronto having its own concentration of exactly this problem, has adopted the model.

What this doesn't settle

Nobody has costed out what a province-wide version of this would actually take to run. The "no provincial strategy" finding itself comes from a source this project couldn't fully re-read directly — a technical access block, not a reason to doubt it, but a reason to flag it as not yet independently double-checked word for word. And nobody knows yet whether what makes Navigator work at St. Michael's — specifically, staff with lived experience of homelessness — works the same way at its new sites in Vancouver and, soon, Montreal.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Bring the Navigator model to a second Toronto hospital (RC-HDC-01), using the same kind of partnership funding that got it to Vancouver — this is a proven design, not an experiment, and Toronto's own hospital system has more than one site with the volume to justify it. 2. Get the Ministry of Health to actually write the policy that doesn't exist (RC-HDC-02) — so the next hospital that wants to do the right thing on discharge doesn't have to invent it from scratch, the way St. Michael's did.

Neither of these requires new science. The evidence already exists, in Canada's own national data and in one hospital's own track record. What's missing is a policy that makes the good version the default, instead of the exception one hospital happened to build.
