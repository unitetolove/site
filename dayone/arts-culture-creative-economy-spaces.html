Arts, Culture, and the Creative Economy — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto keeps losing the small venues where its music scene actually gets built — and the City's own budget shows two things happening at once: real money finally landing behind a culture plan, and venues closing anyway.

What's actually happening

Toronto's creative sector is genuinely large — culture and the arts contribute an estimated $11.3 billion to the city's economy and around 174,000 jobs, by the figures this project inherited from an earlier research pass (not independently re-checked here, so treat the exact number with some caution). At the same time, a 2023 study by Wavelength Music and the University of Toronto found that 13 percent of Toronto's music venues had permanently closed, and three-quarters of the venues surveyed considered themselves endangered. That's not old news: in 2025, two more well-known venues confirmed they were done — The Phoenix Concert Theatre, after 33 years on Sherbourne Street, and The Velvet Underground on Queen Street West. The Phoenix's building is set to be torn down for a new residential development.

The money that's actually showing up

Here's the part that cuts the other way. Toronto adopted a ten-year culture plan back in 2024, and it would be easy to assume that kind of multi-year commitment quietly fades once the next tight budget year hits — that's happened before, and this project's own research flags it as the sector's oldest, most familiar risk. Checking the City's actual 2026 budget directly, that's not what happened this time: the Toronto Arts Council is getting a real $2 million increase this year, on track for a five-year, $10 million total; a fund supporting neighbourhood arts organizations outside the downtown core got its own $379,000 bump; and a further $224,200 inflationary top-up is reaching more than 120 arts and culture organizations across the city. That's not a plan on paper — that's money that already moved.

The part that doesn't resolve cleanly

Both of those things are true at once, and this brief doesn't pretend they cancel each other out. The City is funding culture more seriously than it has in years. Venues are still closing. The honest reason those aren't a contradiction: the money the City controls (grants, programs) and the forces actually closing venues (rising commercial rents, redevelopment, a building's land being worth more as condos than as a concert hall) are mostly different things. The City's own prior research on this is blunt about it — the core problem is "mostly downstream of forces the City only partly controls." Real estate economics and the lack of any provincial rule protecting commercial tenants from being priced out sit above what a municipal arts grant can fix.

A concrete idea worth stealing

One piece of this is fixable at the City level, though: some of these closures come from noise complaints after new condos go up next to a venue that was there first. The UK has a planning rule for exactly this called "Agent of Change" — whoever creates the new conflict pays to fix it. If a developer builds housing next to an existing music venue, the developer pays for the soundproofing, not the venue. It doesn't touch the bigger real-estate-economics problem, but it's a specific, adoptable rule that stops one particular way venues get squeezed out, and it costs the City nothing directly since the developer bears it.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't prove the City's culture plan will still be funded on schedule in 2029 — it's only two years in, and the sector's whole history is City funding getting cut first when budgets tighten. It doesn't prove any specific developer or landlord is deliberately profiteering from a venue's closure; no such finding turned up in this research pass, and none is claimed here. And it doesn't settle whether the City's existing tools — a small property-tax break for buildings that keep rents low for artists, and a newer project aimed at saving music venues specifically — are actually working; nobody has published a number on that yet, one way or the other.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Adopt Agent of Change for venues (a recommendation card) — make new development pay its own noise-mitigation cost when it moves in next to an existing, licensed venue, instead of leaving the venue to absorb complaints and enforcement risk it didn't create. 2. Track the culture plan's own delivery, publicly, every year through 2029 (a recommendation card) — a one-page scorecard, published with each budget, comparing what the 2024 plan promised against what actually landed that year. Costs almost nothing, and it means if a future budget starts quietly under-delivering, everyone sees it that year, not five years later when the damage is already done.

Neither move fixes the deeper real-estate economics squeezing venues out. Both are cheap, concrete, and aimed at making sure the City doesn't undo, through either neglect or a future budget crunch, the real progress its own 2026 numbers show it just made.
