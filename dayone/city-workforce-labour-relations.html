City Workforce, Labour Relations, and Municipal HR — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

The people who run Toronto just finished three years of contract talks, one of them decided by an arbitrator instead of a handshake. The garbage-collection deal that was supposed to save the city money for over a decade quietly stopped saving money years ago, and City Hall renewed it anyway. Here's what's actually on the record.

What just got settled

Three separate groups of city workers came to three separate outcomes. The roughly 27,000 people who work "inside" — recreation staff, law clerks, nurse practitioners, the people who keep buildings and programs running — signed a new four-year deal in March 2025. For the first time in the City's history, nobody in that union works for minimum wage anymore; every single job in it now pays above the provincial floor. The 4,200 "outside" workers — the crews doing parks, water, roads, waste — signed their own four-year deal two weeks before Christmas 2024. Both got wage increases in the 3-to-4% range each year through 2028.

The 1,400 paramedics didn't sign anything. They turned down the deal their bargaining team reached, went back to the table in the spring of 2025, still couldn't agree, and an arbitrator settled it for them in June — because paramedics, as an essential service, don't have the legal right to strike, so an arbitrator decides when talks break down. The arbitrator actually gave them more than the original deal on the table: up to 5.5% in the first year for the more senior classifications. That makes Toronto's paramedics the second-highest-paid in Ontario as of 2025-2026. About half to all of that cost is picked up by the province, not the city alone, depending on the paramedic's classification.

All three of these deals were negotiated free of a law that used to cap public-sector raises at 1% a year. That law, Bill 124, got thrown out by the courts for violating workers' bargaining rights, and rather than fight it further or leave it half-standing, the province scrapped the whole thing in February 2024. This round of bargaining happened in the after — nobody at that table was working under the old ceiling.

The garbage contract nobody's talking about enough

In 2011, under a different mayor, the city handed garbage collection west of Yonge Street to a private company, GFL Environmental, with a promise of roughly $10 million a year in savings. That deal has been renewed twice since. In 2024, it came up again — a new five-year, $289 million contract running to 2026, with room to stretch to 2033. Here's the part that should have been the headline: the city's own head of solid waste told the committee approving the renewal that the contracted service now costs about the same as it would to just run it in-house. The savings case that justified going private in the first place isn't there anymore. One councillor on that committee put it plainly — the city has built a company so big it's now hard to negotiate against, which is what a monopoly looks like even when nobody planned it that way. Council renewed the contract anyway, while also asking staff to study bringing it back in-house. We don't yet know what that study found.

Two surveys, two stories

The Association of Municipalities of Ontario surveyed roughly 2,400 municipal employees across the province in 2024 and found people mostly stay for the pension and job security, and mostly want a raise. CUPE Ontario ran its own survey of frontline members the same year and found something sharper: real wages, adjusted for inflation, have basically gone nowhere, and low pay is the number-one reason members say they'd leave. Both surveys are real, both are about the same workforce, and they don't fully agree with each other. Nobody has published a clean explanation for why yet.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Make the city show its work before renewing a big contract (a recommendation card). If a recurring service contract like the garbage deal comes up for renewal, the city should have to publish, every time, a real cost comparison against doing it in-house — not just repeat the sales pitch from a decade ago. The city already has the numbers; it just needs to be required to show them before every renewal, not only when a councillor happens to ask.

2. Reconcile the two workforce surveys, publicly (a recommendation card). Put the AMO and CUPE Ontario numbers side by side and figure out, on the record, whether frontline workers are genuinely worse off than the broader survey suggests, or whether the two surveys are just measuring different things. Either answer is useful. Not knowing isn't.

What this doesn't settle

Neither card touches the actual wage number in the next round of bargaining — that's what collective bargaining is for, not a research brief. Neither card decides whether garbage collection should go back in-house — that's what the city's own feasibility study, whenever it surfaces, is for. What both cards do is close a visibility gap: right now, whether the city's biggest contracting-out decision still saves money, and whether its own workers are falling behind on pay, are both questions the public has to piece together from a single councillor's question or a union's own survey, rather than something the city puts on the table on a standing basis.
