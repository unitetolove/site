Warming Centres: Empty Beds, Turned-Away Callers, and What's Still Unconfirmed — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto's own Auditor General already did the hard work here. In 2025, her office looked at one winter season of warming centres and winter respite sites and found something specific: 12,742 bed-nights sat empty while Central Intake was turning away or failing to match an average of 174 callers a night, in the same system, in the same season.

The number that should bother everyone

Of those 12,742 empty bed-nights, 4,331 were lost specifically because Warming Centres were in the process of shutting down for the season — no new admissions accepted, existing clients gradually discharged, beds sitting empty the whole time. That's not a scarcity problem. That's a process problem, and the Auditor General's own report includes a staff quote about what it's like to explain to someone in the deactivation window why they can't come inside to a site with visibly empty beds.

The money problem, stated precisely

Nearly $2.9 million in surplus funding paid to third-party operators for that same 2023/24 season hadn't been returned to the City eight months after winter ended. One case example: a Warming Centre budgeted to run the full 153-day season for $861,795 actually ran 89 days, at less than full capacity — and still reported the full season's catering budget, $116,127, because the operator had quietly used that site's money for other programs it ran, without telling the City. Nobody caught it until the Auditor General asked. As of January 2025, $1.5 million of that $2.9 million had been recovered. The remaining $1.4 million was TSSS's own stated expectation for two weeks later — not a confirmed recovery. This library doesn't collapse those into one "$2.9 million recovered" number, because they're not the same kind of fact.

What actually changed for winter 2025/26

Real things did change. The City expanded to roughly 1,275 additional spaces for the season. Warming Centres were open to new admissions on 98 nights, 20 more than the year before. That's genuine, and it's worth saying plainly. But it's also not the same measurement the Auditor General used — "more nights open" isn't "fewer bed-nights lost to deactivation." Nobody has published the number that would actually let you compare 2025/26 against the 4,331-bed-night baseline directly. That's the single cleanest, most answerable gap in this file.

What a hospital is seeing right now

In March 2026, the Canadian Press filed a report from inside St. Michael's Hospital's emergency department — a doctor there, Dr. Evelyn Dell, described the ritual her team does after a patient dies from the cold: "We take a pause at the end of the case and just acknowledge that there's a human here, a person, someone who has community, someone who is loved." Dr. Stephen Hwang, who directs the MAP Centre for Urban Health Solutions at the same hospital, used provincial health data to find something specific: cold-related emergency-department visits by homeless people rose 46 percent last winter compared to the one before — a jump that didn't happen in the general population over the same colder winter. Most of that — 72 percent — happened at temperatures warmer than -15°C, above Toronto's own surge-activation threshold. His own words: "We know that there's often not enough space for people in shelters, and warming centres won't turn people away, but they're frequently packed." The hospital's own response has been to partner directly with a nearby shelter, sending dozens of patients there rather than discharging them into the cold. That's a real institution, right now, working around a capacity problem this library's own Auditor General findings already documented from a completely different angle.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't confirm whether the Auditor General's 15 recommendations have actually been implemented — nobody has published a 2026 follow-up confirming that either way. It doesn't confirm whether that remaining $1.4 million was ever recovered. And it deliberately doesn't restate the specific year-by-year hypothermia death counts the same March 2026 hospital report contains — that's a separate page’s job, and duplicating it here would blur two different kinds of evidence that should stay distinct.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Publish the actual comparable metric — bed-nights lost to deactivation, not just nights open to admissions — for 2025/26 and every season after (a recommendation card), so "more nights open" and "fewer bed-nights wasted" stop getting treated as the same claim. 2. Finish and publish the system-wide review the Auditor General's own Recommendation 15 already called for — checking whether the same financial-control gaps exist across all of TSSS's third-party-operated shelter programs, not just the winter ones (a recommendation card).

Neither of these asks for new money or a new program. Both ask the City to finish measuring what its own auditor already told it to measure.
