Development Charges & Building Permits — Day-One Paper

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/development-charges-building-permits.md

Status: DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from research/cards/development-charges-building-permits.md (RC-DCBP-01-RC-DCBP-02) and research/backgrounders/development-charges-building-permits.md; every claim below traces to one of those two documents.

What a mayor does in week one on this file

Toronto's development-charge rules have changed five separate times since 2022, from both Council and Queen's Park, while the province's own documents now admit its 1.5-million-homes target won't be hit at the current pace. The week-one job on this file isn't picking a side in a fight over whether DCs are too high or too low — it's fixing the predictability problem that's real regardless of which side is right, and being honest about a legislative change (Bill 17) this office doesn't yet have a clear read on.

1. Put a public timeline and a stability commitment on the Comprehensive Development Charges Review. The City's own Review — the vehicle for any serious rethink of Toronto's DC framework — is "in the early stages... expected to continue into 2027," with no published interim milestones (RC-DCBP-01). Even AMO, the body that watches this most closely, had to substantially revise its own estimate of Bill 23's cost after the fact (from a preliminary $5.1 billion over nine years down to roughly $1 billion a year) — a sign that policy volatility itself is a cost, on top of whatever the substantive rate changes turn out to mean. Week one: direct staff to publish real milestones and commit to minimum notice before any further structural change outside the Review's own conclusion (RC-DCBP-01).

2. Get a straight answer on what Bill 17 actually does to Toronto's revenue. The City's own page confirms Bill 17 changed the Development Charges Act again in 2025, with provisions taking effect June 5 and November 3 — but nothing in this office's current evidence base says what those changes are worth to Toronto in dollars, unlike the well-documented effects of the 2022 and 2024 changes (RC-DCBP-02). This is a plain, named gap, not a political dodge: week one directs Finance staff to produce the same kind of dollar estimate AMO built for Bill 23, using the same methodology, and publish it.

What this office does not control, and won't pretend to

The pace of provincial legislative change on development charges — three DC Act amendments in three years — is not something Toronto sets or can reliably predict. Neither is the broader macroeconomic climate the province's own 2026 Budget blames for "subdued" 2026 construction activity. Nothing in this paper promises to hit the province's 1.5-million-homes target on Toronto's own authority; that target's own shortfall, on the government's own numbers, is a provincial-scale problem this office's DC and permitting levers touch only at the margin.

The honest bottom line

Neither action here is a rate cut or a rate increase — both are predictability and transparency fixes to a framework that has genuinely whiplashed policymakers, developers, and City Finance staff alike since 2022. The province's own target math is the harder, bigger problem, and it's not this office's to solve alone; what this office can do is make sure Toronto's own piece of the framework stops adding avoidable uncertainty on top of a housing shortfall everyone — including the province itself — now agrees is real.

Uncertainties this paper inherits from its cards

Bill 17's specific dollar effect on Toronto's DC revenue is genuinely unknown as of this writing (RC-DCBP-02) — this paper does not guess at it. AMO's restated Bill 23 cost figure's original publication was not independently located this pass. Whether DC-framework volatility has itself measurably slowed housing starts, as distinct from broader macroeconomic conditions, is not established by any source in this paper's evidence base, and this paper does not claim that connection. None of these gaps are resolved here — they are named so this paper isn't read as more certain than its own sources are.
