Waste management and diversion — Day-One Brief

Toronto's own numbers don't agree with each other — and neither does its contracting math.

What's actually going on

Toronto diverted 50.6% of its residential waste from landfill in 2025, down slightly from 51.7% the year before — but that number hides a wide gap: single-family homes hit 60.3% diversion, while multi-residential buildings — apartment towers, condos — managed only 26.3%. Nobody in the City's own published materials explains why the gap is so wide; it's just there, year after year, more than double.

Behind that sits a harder deadline: the City's own Green Lane Landfill, its only owned disposal site, is projected to fill up by 2034 or 2035. That wouldn't be so bad if Ontario had somewhere else to put the overflow, but the province's own landfill capacity is projected to run out around the same year, and roughly 30% of Ontario's waste already depends on shipping it across the border to Michigan, New York, or Ohio — a dependency that a border closure or a fee hike could disrupt on short notice. New landfill siting in Ontario now effectively requires sign-off from every neighbouring municipality, which City staff themselves have called a "veto" power that makes building a new one from scratch extremely hard.

On the policy side, Ontario finished a multi-year transition of its Blue Box recycling program on January 1, 2026: producers, not municipalities, now pay for and run the system. Toronto has already banked real savings from it — an estimated $2 million a year from removing over half a million now-unnecessary recycling bins and shrinking warehouse space.

The number that doesn't add up

Here's the part that should raise an eyebrow: Toronto's own news releases don't agree on how much waste the city actually manages. A June 2023 release says the City "manages approximately 450,000 tonnes of garbage every year" — and, three sentences later in the same document, says it "manages close to 900,000 tonnes of waste each year." A November 2024 release says the city "managed close to 830,000 tonnes of waste" the year before. These might be different things being measured — garbage alone versus garbage-plus-everything-diverted, or a real year-over-year change — but no source anywhere reconciles them into one number. If you've seen a single Toronto waste-tonnage figure quoted somewhere, it's worth asking which of these three it actually is.

The contract nobody re-checked

In 2011, Toronto privatized garbage collection west of Yonge Street, awarding the contract to what's now GFL Environmental, on the strength of a projected $10 million a year in savings — a figure the City's own 2014 Auditor General review was asked to check. That review found real savings had been achieved and made 15 recommendations to tighten contract monitoring.

Fast-forward to September 17, 2024 (⚠️ corrected in this pass — an earlier draft of this brief said "September 2025," a full year later than when this actually happened): the City's own head of Solid Waste Management Services told a council committee, on the record, that those savings are gone — the contracted service now costs "very close to what the cost of an internal service provider would cost." In the same meeting, that same committee voted to award the same contractor a new five-year, $289 million contract, with options extending it to 2033 (full Council ratified it the following month, October 2024). One councillor pushed back, warning the arrangement risks a spot where "the company builds that capacity, and then it's very hard for us to negotiate" — a real, on-the-record concern about losing leverage over your own city's garbage collection. Nobody produced a fresh, independent cost comparison before the vote; the City is relying on informal staff testimony about cost convergence, more than a decade after the last time anyone formally checked — and, with the date now corrected, that testimony itself is nearly two years old as of this brief, not a matter of months.

What the evidence doesn't yet say

No source checked this pass explains why multi-residential buildings divert so much less than houses — is it access to bins, building-management incentives, resident education, something else? Nobody's said. Nor does anything reconcile Toronto's conflicting tonnage figures, or say what specific practices in Nova Scotia — repeatedly named as an outperforming province in Ontario's own audit and in a separate peer-reviewed study — Ontario would actually have to copy to close the efficiency gap the province's own Auditor General documented.

Two things worth doing, since the City's own numbers already make the case

Pilot a metered incentive program for multi-residential organics, the way Seoul does it. Seoul weighs food waste per household using RFID bins and, as of December 2025, pays residents in credit for cutting what they throw out by 10% or more. It's not a Toronto-specific answer — no source prices what a comparable Toronto pilot would cost — but it's a real, working mechanism aimed at exactly the kind of gap Toronto's own numbers show: a diversion system that works well for houses and works far less well for towers.

Require a fresh, independent cost check before the next waste-contract renewal, not the last one. The City already has the tool — it used it in 2014. Fourteen years is a long time for a cost-savings finding to keep being cited as if it still holds, and the City's own staff have now said, out loud, that it doesn't. The next time a collection contract comes up for renewal, put a current comparison in front of Council before the vote, not after.

Where this comes from, and where it doesn't yet reach

Every figure above traces to either an existing ledger claim (26 CL-#### rows, mostly at VERIFY status — independently sourced but not yet independently re-checked a second time) or a document fetched live on 2026-07-14: two City of Toronto news releases, a Global News report citing a 2021 Ontario Waste Management Association study and a May 2023 City staff report, a Korea Herald report on Seoul's food-waste program, the Toronto Auditor General's 2014 District 2 contract review (summary only, not the full 15-recommendation text), and CBC's September 17, 2024 reporting on the GFL contract renewal (⚠️ this date was corrected during this adversary pass from an earlier "September 2025" misdate). No advocacy position or academic study beyond what's named above was drawn on, and this brief doesn't manufacture a Toronto-specific cost or outcome figure where none was found.
