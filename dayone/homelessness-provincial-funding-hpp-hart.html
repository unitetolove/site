Queen's Park's Money: HPP, HART Hubs, and a Report Card Nobody's Updated — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/homelessness-provincial-funding-hpp-hart.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/homelessness-provincial-funding-hpp-hart.md, research/cards/homelessness-provincial-funding-hpp-hart.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Ontario is the biggest single funder of the province's own homelessness response — not the federal government, not municipalities. That's not a talking point; it's the Auditor General's own arithmetic: 68% of all funding provincial in 2020/21, and 85% of provincial-plus-federal money in 2022/23. This brief pulled that number by fetching the actual Auditor General PDF directly, not by trusting a summary of it.

Two very different programs, one Ministry split

The Homelessness Prevention Program (HPP) is the big one — roughly $695 million a year, built from a 2022 base plus a 2023 top-up, run by the Ministry of Municipal Affairs and Housing. It doesn't cover everything: violence-against-women shelters and domiciliary hostels are funded through a different Ministry entirely.

HART Hubs are a separate animal, funded by the Ministry of Health, not MMAH — $560 million spread across 29 sites as of the province's own mid-2026 statements. They replaced Ontario's supervised consumption sites starting in 2025, betting that abstinence-based treatment paired with housing would do more good than supervised drug use spaces. Add the two programs together and Ontario's own combined provincial homelessness-and-addiction commitment recomputes to roughly $1.26 billion.

The report card that says "incomplete" — confirmed directly

In 2021, the Auditor General told the province, in plain language: you don't have a real strategy. Recommendation 1 asked for one — coordinated, specific actions, real targets, real timelines. The Ministry said yes, it would build one.

Two years later, the Auditor General checked. We didn't take that secondhand — we pulled the actual PDF. Here's what it says, in the province's own oversight body's own words: 37% of the recommended actions were fully done. Another 23% were in progress. And 40% — twelve of thirty specific actions — showed little or no progress at all. That includes things you'd think would already exist: consistent rules for who gets prioritized for housing based on actual need, tracking whether people referred for help actually got it, real provincial shelter standards, and surprise inspections to make sure shelters are safe. None of that was confirmed done as of November 2023.

Nobody has published anything newer. That report card is now pushing three years old.

HART Hubs: a real housing promise with a real asterisk

The province's own reference document for HART Hubs says it plainly: residents have no legal right to stay. Housing is described as a goal of the program, not something anyone's contractually owed. A year and change into the rollout, that shows up unevenly on the ground — one Ottawa hub was still mostly offering showers and meals, not full treatment. One Toronto site closed because the City had provincial approval in principle but never actually got the funding letter to make it real.

And there's a genuine, unresolved argument sitting in the middle of this. Paramedic calls for suspected opioid overdoses jumped hard in several cities through early 2026 — up 115% in Toronto, 199% in Hamilton, comparing the same five months year over year. The government's answer to that isn't to dispute the calls; it's to point somewhere else entirely — a coroner-sourced 41% drop in actual opioid deaths since the hubs started opening. Those are two different measurements of two different things — emergency response volume versus confirmed fatalities — and nobody has put them side by side in one place where you could actually judge them against each other. This brief isn't going to pretend it can either.

What Ottawa's paperwork reveals

Ottawa, unusually, publishes granular detail Toronto doesn't. Two things stand out. First: when the province announced a $202-million-a-year funding increase in 2023, Ottawa's actual share of that new money was $845,100 — four-tenths of one percent of it. The Mayor's own quoted reaction: "We thought there maybe was a zero or two missing." Second: Ottawa's own separate municipal homelessness fund is split into five categories that can't be moved between each other, even when the need shifts — the City's own report calls that "a significant administrative barrier." Whether Toronto's own HPP allocation has either of these same problems isn't something this pass could confirm — but it's exactly the kind of thing worth checking for, given a comparably-sized program running the same way.

The three moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Make the province finish its own homework (RC-PROV-01) — a public, dated plan for the twelve specific actions the Auditor General already found stalled, plus a request for a fresh follow-up audit instead of leaving 2023 as the last word. 2. Put the two overdose numbers in one place (RC-PROV-02) — a single public dashboard showing both paramedic-call trends and coroner-confirmed deaths side by side, so the argument can actually be settled with data instead of dueling press releases. 3. Check whether Toronto has Ottawa's fixed-envelope problem (RC-PROV-03) — Ottawa found and documented a real design flaw in how its funding categories work. Nobody's confirmed whether Toronto's own allocation has the same flaw. That's worth finding out before assuming it doesn't.

None of these invent new provincial spending. They ask the Province to finish work it already agreed to do, publish data it already collects, and check a design problem it's already been shown exists somewhere in its own system.
