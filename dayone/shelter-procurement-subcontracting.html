Who the City Pays, and How It Decided To — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/shelter-procurement-subcontracting.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/shelter-procurement-subcontracting.md, research/cards/shelter-procurement-subcontracting.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Every contract, dollar figure, and finding in this brief is already public — sitting in Council agenda items, an Auditor General report, and a named news outlet's own reporting. What isn't public, in any single place, is the whole picture. This brief puts it together.

The legal basis, stated plainly

Toronto can sole-source a contract — skip the competitive bid process entirely — under specific, named exceptions in its own procurement bylaw: a genuine emergency, or a documented case where sticking with an existing vendor makes clear operational sense (a "compatibility" exemption). There's a built-in check: no vendor can hold a non-competitive relationship worth more than five years without a special Council vote to extend it. That check is actually being used — City records show catering, nursing, and IT contracts all having to come back to Council specifically because they hit that five-year wall. The system, on paper, isn't a rubber stamp.

Nine contracts, one report, over $100 million

A single April 2024 staff report — verified directly against the primary document for this brief — itemizes nine vendor contracts: two new non-competitive catering deals (one with a hotel group, one with a specific hotel) worth over $45 million combined; a Canadian Red Cross lodging contract for asylum seekers doubling to $61 million; a family-accommodation hotel contract nearly quadrupling; and several more. The same report states, in the City's own words, exactly why it chose not to competitively tender the largest of these: pursuing alternatives "would increase costs, decrease flexibility, and significantly impact ongoing lease negotiations" with the hotel group in question. That's a real, stated, checkable justification — not a black box.

The audit finding that matters most

Toronto's own Auditor General found the City paid over $13 million in hotel charges, over two years, that didn't match what the contracts actually said. Not an allegation from an outside critic — an audited finding from the City's own independent watchdog. The same audit found emergency shelter costs run three to ten times higher than supportive or subsidized housing alternatives. Both of those facts sit next to a program — the Temporary Hotel Program — that was created as a COVID-19 emergency measure and, six years later, still required over a quarter-billion dollars in funding. An emergency exception doing the work of a permanent program is exactly the kind of drift a five-year sunset clause is supposed to catch, and it's worth asking whether it has.

A reform that might actually work — if anyone checks

In December 2025, Council extended one catering contract specifically to buy time for something new: letting individual shelter operators piggyback on the City's own competitively-negotiated pricing instead of each operator (or the City, on their behalf) negotiating separately. The City's own staff report says this "would result in cost savings." That's a real, sensible-sounding reform with a real legal basis already confirmed by the City's own lawyers. What doesn't exist yet, anywhere in the public record: a number. No savings figure. No confirmation any operator has actually used it. The deadline is August 31, 2026. If nobody publishes what happened by then, a genuinely good idea quietly disappears into "did that work? nobody knows."

$109 million in guards, and a real question about what they're for

As of December 2025, the City confirmed directly to a news outlet that it holds eight private security contracts worth roughly $109 million combined, with $35.2 million already spent that year — money going to guard shelters, encampments, Union Station, and city-wide locations. That's a substantial, newly-verified figure, larger than what was previously documented in this project's own earlier research (a gap this brief treats as the picture getting more complete over time, not a discrepancy to explain away).

A sitting member of Council put a sharp question to it publicly: if the job is mostly "take notes and call the police," is that worth $109 million, or would the money do more good funding actual police officers with the power to act? That's a real, on-the-record policy critique from an elected official — reported here as exactly that, a political argument about value for money, not an accusation against any security company doing anything wrong. Nobody — not the Auditor General, not any independent reviewer — has actually audited whether this spending delivers what it's supposed to. That's the gap. Not "is this corrupt," but "has anyone with no stake in the answer actually checked."

The one item this brief reports carefully, and why

A class action names a specific security subcontractor, alleging its workers were routinely paid late, denied overtime despite 100-hour weeks in some cases, and denied mandated pandemic pay — and separately alleges the City knew about these practices for years while continuing to award contracts. This is reported here exactly as it should be: an allegation made by the plaintiffs' law firm, in a case that has been filed but not decided by any court. Not a finding. Not something this brief treats as established fact. The City's own confirmed direct contract value with this vendor is roughly half what the plaintiffs' firm claims — a real discrepancy, unresolved, stated as such. Nothing here says who's right. It says what's alleged, by whom, and what's actually confirmed.

The three moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Build one public registry for shelter procurement contracts, modelled on New York City's own working system, so the next researcher doesn't have to reconstruct this picture from a dozen scattered Council items the way this brief had to (RC-SPS-01). 2. Actually report whether the piggyback reform worked, by its own August 2026 deadline, instead of letting a good idea's outcome go unrecorded (RC-SPS-02). 3. Get an independent audit of the $109 million in security spending — not because anyone has proven it's wasted, but because nobody's checked, and a sitting Council member has already asked the question publicly (RC-SPS-03).

None of these require a new law, a new authority, or a finding against anyone. They ask the City to finish work it has already, visibly, started.
