Purok-Style Hyperlocal Organizing — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-13 · File: research/dayone/purok-hyperlocal-organizing.md

DRAFT v1.1 — L-PLATFORM layer · File: research/dayone/purok-hyperlocal-organizing.md · Date: 2026-07-13 Built only from: research/backgrounders/purok-hyperlocal-organizing.md and research/cards/purok-hyperlocal-organizing.md. No claim in this document goes beyond what those two files already establish. Trust classes: INHERITED + NEW, as carried from the backgrounder/cards; see those documents for full citations.

Toronto already paid for the answers — this work reads it all, integrates it, and shows the work, including the uncertainties.

The case

When Typhoon Haiyan hit the Philippines in 2013, one community came through it in a way its neighbours didn't — because its households were already organized, block by block, into a system built for exactly this. That system is called the "purok": a household-level organizing unit — twenty to fifty-plus households, below the barangay, run by a volunteer coordinator. The Camotes Island municipality of San Francisco built the purok system that won the 2011 UN Sasakawa Award for Disaster Risk Reduction, and it's the same system credited with far lower casualties than surrounding areas when Haiyan hit — a finding independently corroborated this pass via Wikipedia's own account, and, in a later correction pass, via the UN's own disaster-reduction agency (UNDRR) fetched directly. That direct UNDRR fetch narrows the claim in one important way worth stating plainly: UNDRR's own article documents the evacuation of one specific ~1,000-resident island (Tulang Diyot, off San Francisco), not an independently audited casualty count for the Camotes island group as a whole — a real, well-sourced local outcome, but a narrower one than the commonly repeated "island group" framing suggests [backgrounder, "The evidence for"]. Indonesia's RT/RW system shows the same idea can run entirely unpaid and durable; Brazil's Community Health Agents show the paid, professionally supervised version delivers large, measured health gains at a household caseload almost identical to what's proposed for Toronto [backgrounder, "The evidence for"]. The proposal on the table: a funded layer of roughly 10,000 block-level coordinators beneath Toronto's 158 neighbourhoods, costed by this leaf's spine at roughly $45–110M/year in its leanest workable design.

The honest catch

The same structure that saves lives in Camotes is, elsewhere, a surveillance tool: China's "grid management" uses household-level monitors to report on residents' "affairs, words and thoughts" to the state. And the purok's own formalization has a real authoritarian lineage, not just a folk-custom one — Presidential Decree 557 (1974) formalized it, and this pass found an earlier decree, days after martial law was declared in 1972, that built similar household-reaching "Citizens Assemblies" into the same government's toolkit [backgrounder, "Origin," flagged VERIFY pending a primary-text read]. A purok also has no independent legal status in Philippine law — it exists only by municipal ordinance — meaning Toronto would be inventing an entirely new structure, not adapting an existing formal tier.

The closest thing North America has to this model is much thinner than the international evidence suggests. Block-captain and neighbourhood-watch programs are commonly credited with a 16% crime reduction, but that figure comes from a literature with no large-scale, rigorously controlled evaluation ever done — and volunteer burnout is common enough that program guidebooks build in deliberate recognition practices just to manage it [both flagged VERIFY, backgrounder]. And Toronto is not an empty field waiting for this idea: the City already runs a Neighbourhood Grants program (capped at $5,000/neighbourhood/year), real organic mutual-aid pods are still running from the pandemic in Parkdale and Davenport, and — separately, right now — a City Hall committee has just passed a motion asking for $250,000 to $500,000 per neighbourhood per year in ongoing funding for existing neighbourhood associations, heading into the 2027 budget process [backgrounder, "Toronto's existing hyperlocal infrastructure"]. A purok proposal would be one more bid in an already-active, already-contested funding debate, not a novel ask into empty space.

Two places to start

Pilot it small, evaluated, before scaling. Fund paid-anchor-supported, stipended block stewards in 2–3 neighbourhoods (roughly 127-190 puroks, using the backgrounder's own 158-neighbourhood frame — not wards, a unit that doesn't appear in this leaf's evidence base), with the evaluation framework — what gets measured, what counts as success, what triggers stop-scale-redesign — written and adopted before launch, not after. No North American city has demonstrated this model works at purok scale; a bounded pilot is how Toronto would find out rather than guess. Rough cost: roughly $600,000-$2.1 million a year for 2-3 neighbourhoods, not a proportional slice of the full citywide estimate, since the fiscally realistic pilot design sits at the lean end of the spine's own costing, scaled to the number of neighbourhoods actually proposed, not its "daily-service" ceiling [card RC-PH-01].

Fund what already exists before building something new and parallel. Mutual Aid Parkdale already runs over twenty neighbourhood pods; the Davenport Mutual Aid Network is a second active example. Both are volunteer-run, already trusted, and structurally the same idea this whole proposal is reaching for. The single clearest risk this leaf's own evidence names is that a funded, city-designed layer could "bureaucratize and crowd out the very solidarity it wants to scale." Before inventing a new coordinator tier, ask the people already doing this work whether City funding and partnership is wanted — or whether it would look like exactly the co-optation risk the evidence warns about [card RC-PH-02].

What's not yet settled

Neither card resolves the deepest tension here: whether a purok-branded, city-designed structure is the right vehicle at all, given the model's own dual-use history, its thin North American track record, and Toronto's own live, competing funding debate over its existing neighbourhood-association landscape. The martial-law-origin claim, the 16% crime-reduction figure, the Milwaukee cost comparator, and several Toronto-specific mutual-aid details all rest on search-result summaries this pass, not primary sources read directly — real leads for the next discovery pass, not settled facts. Whether existing pod leaders in Parkdale and Davenport would even want City partnership is untested. Neither recommendation is costed at a scale better than a rough comparator-based estimate, and no funding source is confirmed for either.
