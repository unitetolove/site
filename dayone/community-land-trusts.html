Community Land Trusts — Day-One Paper

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/community-land-trusts.md

Status: DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from research/cards/community-land-trusts.md (RC-CLT-01) and research/backgrounders/community-land-trusts.md; every claim below traces to one of those two documents.

What a mayor does in week one on this file

More than 500 tenants in Parkdale already live in homes a community land trust owns and will never sell out from under them — 86 properties, held permanently affordable since 2014. That's not a pilot or a proposal; it's already working, in this city, today. Toronto isn't starting from zero on non-market housing — it already runs one of the more credible acquisition programs of any Canadian city, and hosts a recognized cluster of operating community land trusts. The week-one job isn't inventing a new tool; it's turning a program that has worked in pieces (funding round by funding round) into a program the sector can actually plan around.

1. Commit to multi-year MURA funding, not another one-off round. The Multi-Unit Residential Acquisition program has already committed roughly $165 million since 2021 and secured more than 1,000 permanently affordable homes — including $102 million in 2024 alone (RC-CLT-01). That is real, working infrastructure, not a pilot. The single biggest thing week one can do is convert MURA from episodic funding rounds into a standing, multi-year capital commitment with a published annual budget line, so community housing providers and the roughly dozen Toronto-area CLTs can plan acquisitions years out instead of reacting to each new round's announcement (RC-CLT-01).

2. Be honest about the gap between current pace and what "moving the needle" actually costs. The backgrounder's own arithmetic — explicitly flagged as an illustrative estimate, not an audited number — suggests meaningfully closing the distance between Toronto's roughly 3.5% non-market housing share and the OECD's ~7% average would take on the order of $6 billion in public capital over 20 years, several multiples of MURA's current annual pace (RC-CLT-01, backgrounder). Week one doesn't pretend to close that gap. It names it publicly, alongside the honest options for a dedicated revenue stream — a share of Vacant Home Tax revenue is the most concrete option already in this research collection, though it is one of several competing claims on that pot, not a blank cheque (RC-CLT-01).

3. Use the City's land, not just its cash. Public land transfer or long-lease to trusts is described as the City's single most powerful non-cash lever — it removes the largest cost input in the ground-lease model and lets modest capital go further (backgrounder). Week one directs staff to identify a first tranche of surplus City land suitable for CLT transfer or long-lease, alongside the MURA funding commitment above, rather than treating land and capital as separate conversations.

What this office does not control, and won't pretend to

The strongest evidence behind this file — Burlington's Champlain Housing Trust, the only rigorous long-term study of whether the CLT model actually delivers on its own terms — comes from a market explicitly smaller and less financialized than Toronto's. How far that result transfers here is genuinely uncertain, and this paper does not promise Toronto-scale results on Vermont-scale evidence (backgrounder). Acquisition also competes directly against financialized, deep-pocketed investors for the same existing buildings — the same dynamic this research collection's real-estate-financialization work documents independently — meaning a scaled MURA program is working against a real headwind, not a clear field.

The honest bottom line

Toronto's advantage on this file isn't that it needs a new idea — it's that it already has a working one, proven at a scale most Canadian cities haven't reached. What's missing is not proof of concept; it's a funding commitment sized to match the scale of the problem the City's own numbers describe. Week one is about saying that plainly, converting MURA into something the sector can plan around for years rather than one round at a time, and pairing it with the City's land — the one input that costs nothing to commit and does the most to make every acquisition dollar go further.

Uncertainties this paper inherits from its card

The $6 billion/20-year scale figure this paper cites as context is explicitly an illustrative order-of-magnitude estimate in its own source, not an audited projection — it should not be repeated as a precise fiscal target (RC-CLT-01). No source in the evidence base quantifies the local economic (jobs, induced spending) or environmental impact of scaling acquisition specifically, as distinct from new construction — both are named as genuine gaps, not filled with borrowed figures (RC-CLT-01). MURA's actual cost-per-unit relative to new construction, and its long-term unit-retention record, remain open questions this paper does not resolve.
