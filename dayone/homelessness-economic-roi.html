Toronto Already Paid For The Answers — The Economic Case for Housing First

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-13 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.1 · Date: 2026-07-13 Provenance: L-PLATFORM (rendered from that page's recommendation cards and our research file for that page only; no new claims introduced here)

Toronto already paid for the answers. The Auditor General has already audited what a shelter bed costs. A national clinical trial that ran in this city has already measured what housing someone costs instead. Nobody needs another study to know which number is bigger — including the uncertainties in both.

The number that matters. Toronto's audited shelter cost is $49,640 per person per year. The audited net cost of housing that same person through the Housing First model is $6,311 per year. The difference — $43,329 saved for every person moved from a shelter bed into permanent housing — is not a projection. It is arithmetic on two numbers the city and a peer-reviewed clinical trial already measured independently. Applied to Toronto's roughly 12,000 chronically homeless residents, that comparison alone points to about $521 million a year in avoidable municipal shelter cost.

Why the city pays and someone else saves. The City bears the shelter bill directly. But when someone is housed instead of sheltered, the largest share of the resulting savings shows up somewhere else: Ottawa stops paying refugee-claimant shelter reimbursements and health-transfer costs tied to emergency-department overuse; the Province stops paying the hospital and corrections costs that come with chronic homelessness. A 2026 province-wide update independently confirms the pattern this creates: between 2021 and 2025, homelessness grew 49% across Ontario while total funding grew only 32% — and municipalities absorbed the largest share of that gap, their own funding rising 48% just to keep pace. The order of government that invests the most is not the order of government that saves the most. That is not a moral failure. It is a fiscal design flaw, and it is fixable.

Three fixes, aimed at the three different governments actually holding the money.

First — Toronto should know its own real number. Right now the City's public accounting runs almost entirely through one department's operating budget. It leaves out TTC's own $26 million a year in shelter-adjacent safety spending, active legal exposure — including both the unresolved five-plaintiff Lamport Stadium suit over a 2021 encampment clearance and a separate, live $50 million proposed class action, filed in 2025, over the City's denial of shelter beds to refugee claimants — and, using the federal government's own official method for costing a life in public policy, the value of the 59 shelter-resident deaths this city documents most years (both 2024 and 2025 came in at 59, per the City's own published statistics). That last figure alone, calculated the way Ottawa's own Treasury Board requires its departments to calculate it, is larger than every other single line item in this cost case — and it is not yet added into the $1.14 billion headline total elsewhere in this document; the two figures sit side by side, not stacked. Toronto should publish one consolidated number every budget cycle, not leave it scattered across departments for outsiders to reassemble by hand.

Second — the Province should stop offering an all-or-nothing choice. Raising Ontario Works to a livable rate for everyone on it costs the Province roughly $2.3 billion a year, and most of the savings it would generate land at the City and federally — which is exactly why it keeps not happening. A narrower, cheaper instrument already sits in this evidence base: a $500-a-month top-up paid only to someone who already has a signed lease and housing-benefit approval, modelled at roughly $72 million a year for 12,000 people. That is the actual bottleneck — not the base rate for everyone, but the last-mile gap for people who have already cleared the harder barriers. Thirty times cheaper, aimed at the specific problem.

Third — Ottawa should be repaid, not just asked. The federal government has the strongest return on investment of any order of government in this case — a modelled payback in a little over two years, a well-reasoned upper bound of $1.8 billion in ten-year value from a $500 million investment (a range this document states honestly runs as low as $37 million depending on assumptions, not a single flattering number). A bond-style capital instrument, repaid specifically from the refugee-shelter and health-transfer savings this same investment already generates, ties Ottawa's incentive to the outcome instead of asking it to fund housing on faith every budget cycle.

What this is not. This is not a claim that money alone fixes homelessness — the same 2026 provincial data shows spending grew in the years homelessness also grew, and that within that growth, emergency-shelter spending rose faster than housing-supply spending. That is not a reason to doubt the Housing First math; it is a reason to insist any new money follows the lever that is actually audited to work, not the lever that is easiest to fund one more year at a time.

Every figure above traces to an audited public source, a peer-reviewed clinical trial, or a named 2026 government-data update — cited in full, with its own honest range, in the backgrounder and cards this document renders from. Toronto already paid for the answers. The remaining question is which government is willing to act on the one it's already holding.
