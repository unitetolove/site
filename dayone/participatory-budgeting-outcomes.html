Participatory Budgeting Outcomes — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from: our research file for that page and that page's recommendation cards. No claim in this document goes beyond what those two files already establish. Provenance: earlier research plus this review, as carried from the backgrounder/cards; see those documents for full citations.

The case

Participatory budgeting lets residents directly decide how to spend a defined slice of public money — and 35 years of global practice say it works when the money is real and the commitment is durable, and disappoints when it isn't [backgrounder, "Current state"]. Brazil supplies the cleanest causal evidence: adopting PB shifted municipal spending toward sanitation and health and measurably reduced infant mortality, by redirecting existing budgets toward the poor [backgrounder, "The causal evidence: Brazil"]. Where PB has scaled seriously — Paris committing roughly 5% of its capital budget, New York City running a continuous program since 2011 — participation has scaled with it, reaching well over 90,000 voters in a single recent NYC cycle [backgrounder, "International comparators"].

The honest catch

Toronto already ran this experiment, at the wrong scale. Its 2015-17 pilot gave three wards $150,000-$250,000 each; turnout fell as the budget grew, residents said the projects were too small to matter, and the City did not continue it [backgrounder, "Toronto's own 2015-17 pilot"]. That result matched the global pattern: PB fails when it's a token gesture bolted onto business as usual.

This review chased down a lead a prior session had flagged but not confirmed — and the answer changes the picture. Montreal's citywide participatory budget, the closest large-Canadian-city comparator Toronto's own evidence had pointed to, has ended. Not because it failed: across three editions from 2020 to 2025-26 it invested $101.5 million, and its final round still drew more than 28,000 voters on a $45 million budget. It ended because the City administration, facing pressure to eliminate 1,000 municipal positions to balance its budget, chose not to renew it [backgrounder, "Montreal: confirmed program discontinuation"]. That is a different failure mode than Toronto's — not "too small to matter," but "big enough to be an easy budget line to cut when the money gets tight, with nothing legally protecting it." Paris's program, still running in restructured form, and New York City's, still running but recently shrinking (24 districts and $30 million down to 22 districts and $22 million in one budget cycle), round out a picture where durability, not just initial sizing, is the real unresolved design problem [backgrounder, "International comparators"].

Two places to start

Size it seriously, and build in a real floor against reversal. A Toronto PB program anchored to a defined share of the capital budget — even 1% would be roughly $60 million a year, an order of magnitude above the failed pilot — paired with a rule that makes cutting it harder than an ordinary majority budget vote. This directly answers both failure modes on the record: Toronto's "too small" pilot and Montreal's "big enough, still cut" ending [card a recommendation card].

Check on, and revive, the program Toronto already has. Toronto Community Housing has run tenant participatory budgeting since 2001 — roughly $9 million a year, allocated through a needs-and-equity formula, Canada's longest-running example of exactly this idea. It was paused in 2017 for redesign, and nobody in this page’s evidence knows its current status. That's worth finding out before designing something new from scratch, when a working local proof-of-concept may already be sitting unused [card a recommendation card].

What's not yet settled

Montreal's ending sharpens the central design question rather than answering it: what specific mechanism would have actually protected a $101.5 million, well-attended program from a single fiscal-pressure budget decision — legal entrenchment, a supermajority requirement, something else — is not tested anywhere in this page’s evidence, because no comparator city with that kind of protection was identified. Toronto Community Housing's own program's current status is unknown, not just unconfirmed in an old document — a real gap, not a rhetorical one. And the deepest open question from this page’s original spine remains: what pot size and design would actually make PB worth Toronto residents' time, given that even Montreal's much larger, well-run program still didn't survive. This document names that as still open, not answered by anything found this review.
