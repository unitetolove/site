76 Percent Think Crime Is Up. The City's Own Numbers Say Otherwise — Except Nobody Can Agree What the Numbers Are — Day-One Paper

DRAFT

Status: DRAFT · v1.0 · 2026-07-14 · Draws only from: our research file for that page and that page's recommendation cards Layer: L-PLATFORM (opinionated synthesis; every factual premise traces to the backgrounder/cards above)

Seventy-two percent of Torontonians think homicides are rising. They're not — by most accounts, they fell sharply in 2025. But ask exactly how sharply, and you get two different answers depending which report you read. Toronto's own website, meanwhile, is still citing a safety ranking from 2017. If the city wants residents to believe the good news, it should probably start by getting its own number straight.

Where things stand

A December 2025 poll from Liaison Strategies — a firm with real, checkable election-prediction credentials, not an anonymous online survey — found that 76% of Toronto residents believe crime in Canada rose over the past year, and 72% believe homicides in Toronto specifically are rising. The same poll found the identical pattern next door: in Peel Region, most Brampton and Mississauga residents believe homicides are increasing, even though Peel Regional Police reported just 10 homicides through October 2025 versus 19 for the whole of 2024. In York Region, homicides were reportedly down 67% — and still, over half of Vaughan and Markham residents think they're going up.

Here's the frustrating part for anyone trying to check the other side of that gap: the actual 2025 numbers aren't as clean as the perception-gap headline suggests. Toronto's homicide count for the year gets reported as either 39 or 42, depending on the source. The auto-theft decline gets reported as anywhere from about 24% to 34%. And when this research went looking for Toronto's current standing on the Economist's well-known Safe Cities Index — the kind of ranking that should be able to settle "is Toronto actually safe" pretty definitively — the City's own website turned out to be citing 2017 numbers. Not 2024. Not even 2021. Nine-year-old rankings, sitting on a live City of Toronto page, being used as if current.

There's a real success story buried under all this — the Toronto Community Crisis Service, a non-police response for mental-health crisis calls, diverted 78% of relevant 911 calls away from police entirely in its original, independently-evaluated 2022 pilot. That's a genuinely strong number from a genuinely credible evaluator. The problem is the program has since grown roughly tenfold in reported budget and is expanding onto subway platforms — environments the original evaluation never tested — and the same 78% figure keeps getting cited as if it still applies at that scale, without this research finding evidence anyone's gone back to check.

Two things worth doing, at minimal cost

First: build one authoritative, current public page for Toronto's actual crime trends, and retire the stale one. The City already has the underlying data — the Toronto Police Service runs its own public data portal. What's missing is one clear, quarterly-updated, plain-language page that says, in one place, what's actually happening with homicides, shootings, and auto theft — replacing a Safe Cities Index citation that's been sitting unrefreshed since before the pandemic. This costs next to nothing; it's a consolidation job, not a data-collection one. It won't single-handedly close a 76-point perception gap — nothing this cheap could — but it removes the excuse that the real numbers are hard to find, because right now, even a motivated fact-checker runs into conflicting figures.

Second: go back and re-evaluate the Crisis Service at its actual current size. The original third-party evaluation was rigorous and independent — that's exactly why it's worth repeating now that the program is ten times bigger and operating somewhere new. If the 78% diversion rate still holds at scale and on transit platforms, that's a genuinely strong result worth restating with fresh evidence instead of a three-year-old pilot number. If it doesn't hold, that's exactly the kind of thing a city should want to know before scaling further, not after.

What this doesn't settle

This paper doesn't explain why the perception gap exists — media coverage, social media, individual high-profile incidents, or something else entirely. Nobody in this research pass found a study that actually answers that question for Toronto specifically, and guessing wouldn't be honest. It doesn't settle the exact homicide or auto-theft numbers for 2025 — that requires a direct pull from the Toronto Police Service's own data portal, which this review flagged as the obvious next step but didn't complete. And it doesn't claim the Crisis Service is failing at its new scale — only that nobody has independently checked, which is a different and more fixable problem. What's proposed here is cheap, boring, and exactly the kind of unglamorous data hygiene that a perception gap this large deserves before anyone reaches for a bigger, more expensive answer.
