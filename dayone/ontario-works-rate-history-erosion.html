The Number That Hasn't Moved Since 2018 — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Seven hundred and thirty-three dollars. That's what Ontario Works pays a single adult every month. It was $733 in 2018. It's $733 today. In between, Ontario's own minimum wage went from $14.00 an hour to $17.60, on its way to $17.95 this October — we checked the government's own current schedule to be sure. This brief exists because that gap is not an accident of inflation. It's a policy choice, made and remade every year the number didn't move.

The number in context

In 1990, Ontario welfare paid a single adult about 75% of what full-time minimum-wage work paid. Today it's about 25%. That's not two disconnected facts — it's one number falling by fifty percentage points over thirty-six years, through three distinct periods: a 21.6% cut under the Harris government in 1995, over a decade of inadequate increases under the Liberals that never closed the gap the cut opened, and the current freeze — eight years and counting — under the sitting provincial government.

The comparison that makes it concrete

Here's the part we could independently check, not just repeat: Ontario runs two major social-assistance programs, Ontario Works and the Ontario Disability Support Program. They're administered under the same broader framework. In 2024, one of them got an automatic 4.5% increase because it's tied to inflation. The other — OW, the one this brief is about — got nothing. We pulled that fact directly from Maytree, an independent non-profit that publishes its own methodology every year, not from this project's own prior research. It confirms, independently, what this page’s other material already said: Ontario already has a working inflation-protection formula. It's already running. It's just not applied to Ontario Works.

What "36% of the poverty line" actually means

Maytree tracks something called the Market Basket Measure — Canada's official poverty line — and compares it every year to what someone on social assistance actually receives, once you add in every tax credit and benefit they're entitled to, not just the bare welfare rate. For a single adult on Ontario Works in Toronto, that combined number in 2024 was $10,455 for the year. The poverty line was nearly three times that. Their income came in at 36% of it — the lowest point in the 23 years Maytree has been tracking this. And every single one of those 23 years, that same household type lived below an even lower bar: the Deep Income Poverty threshold, which is 75% of the poverty line itself. Not poor. Deep poor. For 23 straight years.

The part that compounds it

If you lose your housing while on Ontario Works, the province's own regulation doesn't just fail to help — it actively pays you less. A recipient with no fixed address gets $343 a month. Not the $733 a housed recipient gets. The shelter portion is removed entirely, because the rule is written to only pay a shelter allowance to someone who has an address to send it to. Read that again: the moment someone loses housing — the exact moment they'd need the most support to get it back — the province's own rule cuts their income by more than half.

And Toronto's own average bachelor apartment rent in 2024 was reported at $1,456 a month, per CMHC-sourced figures this brief didn't re-check against CMHC directly. That's more than the full $733 rate on its own, before food, transit, or anything else.

What this doesn't settle

This brief doesn't calculate what it would actually cost the province to fix either of these problems — indexing OW to inflation, or restoring the shelter allowance for people without an address — because doing that honestly requires knowing how many people are currently on Ontario Works, and specifically how many are currently getting the reduced no-fixed-address rate. We didn't find a current, verified number for either in this review, and we're not going to invent one to make a cleaner-sounding card. That's a real, named gap, not a rounding error.

It also doesn't settle why the province indexes one program to inflation and not the other. No public rationale for that specific distinction turned up in this review.

The three moves this page’s cards propose

1. Turn on the switch that already exists (a recommendation card) — apply ODSP's own already-working inflation-indexing formula to Ontario Works. Not a new program. A formula already running, applied to the program currently excluded from it. 2. Stop punishing people for losing housing (a recommendation card) — restore the full shelter allowance to OW recipients without a fixed address, so losing your home doesn't also mean losing more than half your income at the exact moment you need it most. 3. Make the province publish its own report card (a recommendation card) — a standing, official dashboard showing OW and ODSP rates against Ontario's own poverty line, instead of leaving that calculation to an outside non-profit's annual report that most people will never see.

None of these invent a new program. Two of them turn on mechanisms that already exist somewhere in Ontario's own system. The third asks the province to publish, officially, a number an outside organization already calculates every year for free.
