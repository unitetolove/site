Geopolitical Resilience & Trade Shocks — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

This is a fast-moving file, so here's what's actually true today rather than what was true when this project's master briefing was first written. The short version: the big trade agreement didn't collapse, the tariffs that matter most to Ontario are still on, and Toronto already has a real (if narrow) Buy Canadian rule on its own books. Nobody should be told either "it's all fine" or "the sky is falling" — both would be wrong, and both are avoidable if you just read what the governments involved have actually published.

What's actually in force

On July 1, 2026, Canada, the US, and Mexico held the mandatory six-year check-in on their trade agreement (CUSMA, also called USMCA). The US said no to renewing it as written. That sounds bigger than it is: the agreement itself didn't expire. It stays in force, with the same tariff rules, the same rules about where products count as "made here," and the same investment protections — right through to 2036 — while a new yearly check-in process now kicks in until the three countries either agree to extend it or it runs its full course. Nothing about your day-to-day cross-border trade changed on July 1. What changed is that a scheduled renewal that could have quietly happened, didn't.

Separately, and more concretely: Canada still has counter-tariffs on American steel, aluminum, and automobiles. Those were extended on June 3, 2026 for another year, out to mid-2027, alongside real federal money — a $5 billion fund, a $1 billion financing program for metal manufacturers, and targeted regional support. Most of Canada's other counter-tariffs on US goods — the ones on orange juice, appliances, and dozens of other everyday products — were actually removed back in September 2025, because the US kept letting most Canadian goods in tariff-free under CUSMA. So the picture isn't "tariffs everywhere" or "tariffs nowhere" — it's specifically steel, aluminum, and cars, with everything else mostly back to normal.

There's a separate layer of US tariffs — a broad 10% one from February 2026 and steel/aluminum/copper tariffs running as high as 50% from April — that came up in this review's research but couldn't be independently confirmed against a US government page directly. That's stated honestly here as a gap, not glossed over: the steel/aluminum/auto picture above is solid, primary-source-confirmed; this additional layer is reported but not yet double-checked.

The diversification story, and its honest asterisk

Canada really has been selling less to the US and more to everyone else — that trend, first documented for 2025, is still showing up in 2026 trade data. But the honest caveat that was already flagged before still applies, and this review found it's still true: a good chunk of that "selling to new markets" story is gold. Gold prices have been elevated, gold exports (especially to the UK) have jumped, and that shows up in the numbers as diversification even though it's really a price effect. The real diversification is real. It's just smaller than the headline number alone would suggest, and that was true in 2025 and it's still true now.

What Toronto actually controls

Trade deals, tariffs, and foreign policy are Ottawa's job, not the City's. That hasn't changed and isn't going to. What Toronto does have, and what this review confirmed is real and already happening — not just talk — is its own purchasing rules. Back in March 2025, City Council changed its own procurement bylaw: for smaller contracts, the City now buys Canadian only; for bigger contracts, it can give Canadian and trade-partner suppliers a leg up, and can turn away a US-based bidder if that's judged to be in the City's interest. Ontario did something similar at the provincial level, and so did the federal government for its own big contracts. All three governments say the same thing about their own rules: these are temporary, tied to the tariffs being there in the first place, and would get walked back if the tariffs go away.

There's a real legal ceiling on how far any of this can go, and it's worth naming plainly: Canada is bound by international procurement rules (through the World Trade Organization and a stack of trade deals) that stop any government here from just shutting American suppliers out across the board. That's exactly why Toronto's own rule only fully applies below a certain contract size — above that size, it's a "preference," not a ban. This isn't a loophole city hall invented; it's the actual legal boundary every government in Canada is working inside.

The three moves this page’s cards propose

1. Make the City's own "this is temporary" promise checkable (a recommendation card). The City's Buy Canadian rule says it'll go away if the tariffs do. Nobody currently publishes a report tracking whether that's actually true year to year. This card just asks for that report — using data the City already has.

2. Find out what the City itself is actually exposed to (a recommendation card). Nobody could find a public document showing which of the City's own major purchases — construction materials, vehicle parts, water treatment supplies, IT — actually depend on US suppliers or tariff-hit goods. Before doing anything bigger, the City should know its own numbers, the same way Toronto Community Housing already did for its own purchasing in a March 2026 board report.

3. One page that tells people what's actually true (a recommendation card). This backgrounder took real digging through several federal government pages — including finding one that hadn't been updated since January while a more current one existed elsewhere — just to answer "what tariffs are actually on right now." A resident or small business owner shouldn't have to do that themselves. A short, plain-language, regularly-updated City page fixes that.

What this doesn't settle

None of this tells you whether the trade relationship gets better or worse from here. The steel and aluminum tariff rate itself was reportedly up for renegotiation again around July 21, 2026 — after this brief was written — so that specific number could already be different by the time you're reading this. Nobody has published a full, current, side-by-side list of every US tariff still active, so treat any specific percentage in wide circulation as something to double-check against a government source, not something to take on faith. And nobody in this review's search turned up a credible, named example of who specifically profits from this uncertainty continuing — which sounds like a gap, but is actually the honest answer: it's a genuinely hard thing to establish without a much deeper investigative dig than a general search can do, and pretending otherwise would be worse than saying so plainly.
