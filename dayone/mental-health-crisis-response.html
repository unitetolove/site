Mental Health Crisis Response — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-13 · File: research/dayone/mental-health-crisis-response.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.1 · Date: 2026-07-13 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/mental-health-crisis-response.md, research/cards/mental-health-crisis-response.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto already paid for the answers here. The City's own budget office, its own police service, and its own crisis-service data already measured who calls, who responds, and what happens next. This brief reads what the City has already published, integrates it into one page, and shows the uncertainties rather than smoothing over them.

What's actually happening

The Toronto Community Crisis Service — mental-health workers instead of police — has been operating citywide since July 2024 and is still growing. In November 2025 it started piloting embedded teams on the busiest subway stations for people in crisis. In 2026 its call-dispatch function is moving into Toronto Paramedic Services. Its reported diversion rate (crisis calls resolved without police) has climbed from 78 percent in 2024 toward a 90 percent target by 2027.

The Toronto Police Service's own budget documents back this up from the other side: mental-health calls fell 18.5 percent in 2025 versus 2024, while referrals from police to the crisis service rose 63 percent over the same stretch. That is two separate City documents, produced by two different divisions, independently telling a consistent story. That kind of cross-checking is rare and worth taking seriously — but the two trends being consistent with each other is not the same as proving one caused the other. Attributing the drop in police-handled mental-health calls cleanly to TCCS substitution is loose: both trends could be true at once without the crisis service being the reason police volume fell. Nothing found this pass proves or disproves that causal link either way.

The number that doesn't add up

Here is the honest part. This project's own earlier research says the crisis service "diverted 7,800" calls out of 10,339 received. But the City's actual 2026 budget notes, read directly this week, say something different: 7,800 is the total number of 911-sourced calls received, and 6,891 of those — not 7,800 — were the ones actually resolved without police. Those two numbers describe two different things and can't both be the "78 percent" figure everyone quotes.

There's a second problem, and it's the City's own arithmetic, not a citation mix-up: 6,891 divided by 7,800 is 88.3 percent, not the 78 percent the City's own sentence states right next to those two numbers. Ten percentage points is too big to be rounding. This brief isn't guessing which of "6,891," "7,800," or "78%" is the one the City actually meant — all three come from the same sentence in the same document, and at least one of them is wrong. Until someone at the City, or a future verification pass reading the underlying TCCS/SafeTO report the budget notes summarize, confirms which figure to trust, treat the 78 percent diversion rate — the single most-quoted number in this whole file — as reported but not confirmed accurate.

This isn't a reason to distrust the program. It's a reason to distrust quoting its own numbers without checking them first. Nobody has reconciled this yet. It's flagged here rather than fixed, because fixing it would mean guessing.

The comparator programs aren't what they used to be

Two American programs get cited constantly as proof this model works: CAHOOTS in Eugene, Oregon, and Denver's STAR program. Both are usually described using their best years — CAHOOTS's roughly 24,000 calls with a 0.6 percent police-backup rate in 2019, Denver STAR's zero-backup first six months in 2020.

Checking their current status, not their highlight reel, tells a different story. CAHOOTS stopped serving Eugene entirely in April 2025 — the city where it was invented for over three decades couldn't keep funding it. It still runs in the neighbouring city of Springfield, but not in its own hometown. Denver STAR is still operating, five years in, but it never reached the round-the-clock coverage it set out to achieve, and the reporting on its fifth anniversary says that's "unlikely given Denver's current budget woes."

That matters for how this file gets used. If someone cites CAHOOTS or Denver STAR as the proof that this model is stable and proven, the honest answer as of 2025 is: less than it used to be, in their own cities. Toronto's own program, by contrast, is the one still expanding. That's a genuinely different, and better, story than the one usually told — but it means Toronto's case rests on Toronto's own evidence, not on borrowed American success stories that have since developed real cracks.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't resolve which figure — 78 percent of 7,800, or something else — is the right one for TCCS's own diversion rate, because no source found this week settles it. It doesn't confirm what TCCS itself actually costs as its own line item, separate from the broader $50.7 million community-safety budget category it sits inside; a $31.7-to-$35.3-million figure circulates but wasn't confirmed against a primary City document in this pass. It doesn't update TCCS's own safety record — how often its own workers still need police or ambulance backup — past a 2022 pilot-era snapshot (2.5 percent police, 1.7 percent ambulance), because no more recent figure was found. And it doesn't say whether any provincial mental-health dollars, as opposed to City tax dollars, actually fund the service.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Get an independent, non-self-reported look at what happens after the crisis call ends (RC-MHCR-01) — not just whether police were avoided, but whether people stay out of the ER, stay housed, stay stable, months later. The program's own numbers don't yet answer that, and don't even fully agree with themselves. 2. Use the City's own Auditor General's list — six specific, already-named call types (well-being checks, noise complaints, and four others) that could go to a crisis team instead of police — and actually pilot routing some of them there (RC-MHCR-02), instead of leaving a finding the City already produced sitting unused.

Neither requires inventing a new number or a new program. Both require reading what the City already published, including the parts that don't reconcile cleanly, and the parts about other cities that don't flatter the case as much as the highlight reel usually does.
