International Students & Recent Grads — Day-One Paper

DRAFT File: research/dayone/international-students-recent-grads.md

File: research/dayone/international-students-recent-grads.md · Status: DRAFT · v1.1 · 2026-07-13 Issue slug: international-students-recent-grads · Draws only from: research/backgrounders/international-students-recent-grads.md and research/cards/international-students-recent-grads.md Layer: L-PLATFORM (opinionated synthesis; every factual premise traces to the backgrounder/cards above)

FROM-SCRATCH leaf: the evidence base here was assembled entirely this run, thinner than any spine-backed leaf's day-one paper in this project — and this paper says so throughout, rather than dressing up a thin file as a settled one.

Toronto already paid for the answers it has — the trouble is how few of them are actually about Toronto, including the uncertainty in that gap itself.

Where things stand

Ontario's international student enrolment fell 6% in 2024–25 and then 36% in 2025–26, under a federal cap whose stated goal is pushing temporary residents below 5% of Canada's population by 2027. Nobody has yet produced a Toronto-specific version of that number. Per one student-press account of 2024 food bank data — corroborated by a second, independently worded account of what appears to be the same underlying report, but with the primary "Who's Hungry 2024" report itself not yet directly fetched — 56% of student food bank clients in the Toronto area are international students — a disproportionate share of a population that's shrinking under federal policy and, by every account found here, still struggling now. A Toronto Metropolitan University student newspaper investigation this year documented individual, named-but-anonymized cases of landlords asking international students about their immigration status during unit viewings, subdividing rooms into unsafe "flex rooms" marketed specifically to this population, and telling a tenant who raised safety concerns "if you don't like it, you can leave." None of this is a City of Toronto program failing. It's closer to the opposite problem: there isn't a City program here to fail, because almost every lever that actually shapes this issue — the cap, the Post-Graduation Work Permit's eligibility rules, tuition regulation — sits with Ottawa or Queen's Park, not City Hall.

And even the federal rules keep moving under people's feet. The PGWP-eligible-fields list changed three times in eight months in 2025 alone: narrowed on June 25, partially reversed nine days later on July 4, then frozen with no further updates as of January 15, 2026. A graduate's work-authorization eligibility can shift without their own choices changing at all, based only on which of those three versions was in force on the day they applied for their study permit.

Two things worth doing, at realistic cost

First: give international students one real place to learn their rights and report exploitation, instead of relying on whichever institution's office they happen to attend. The City already runs a Newcomer Office and a Local Immigration Partnership coordination table — funded partly through IRCC, not built from nothing — that could host a plain-language, multilingual hub covering tenant rights, how to report suspected exploitation to RentSafeTO (which the Mayor's office is already expanding) and the province's Rental Housing Enforcement Unit, and a clear path to Rent Bank and eviction-prevention supports. Order of magnitude: low hundreds of thousands of dollars a year — a floor-level guess, not a costed proposal, because despite a targeted search, no City budget line or genuine dollar comparator for a program of this kind was found at all (not even for the City's own existing newcomer-orientation programs, which have no publicly quantified budget either). It would not fix the underlying vulnerability documented in this leaf's own reporting. It would mean the next international student who gets asked about their immigration status during a rental viewing has somewhere obvious to go instead of quietly deciding, like more than one person in this year's reporting did, that it's easier to just stay quiet and stay put.

Second: publish one City-endorsed page that actually tracks the PGWP pathway as it changes, instead of leaving graduates to reconstruct three rule changes in eight months on their own. This costs less — low tens of thousands a year, essentially a communications function — and requires nothing from Ottawa or Queen's Park beyond what already exists; it just makes it legible in one place, refreshed quarterly, with links to the settlement and employment organizations the City already coordinates with. Neither of these fixes the volatility itself. Both make the volatility survivable for the people living through it.

What this doesn't settle

Neither recommendation touches the actual cap, the actual PGWP field-of-study rules, or actual tuition regulation — all of it federal or provincial, none of it something a City program can reach. Neither recommendation is backed by outcome data, because none exists yet for either idea at this scale — every "who benefits, how much" claim in the underlying cards is stated as directional, not modeled, and this paper isn't going to pretend otherwise. And the single biggest gap in this whole file is one no card can close: nobody in this pass found actual Toronto-specific numbers for how many international graduates of this city's own institutions stay, how many leave, or what happens to the ones whose PGWP runs out before their next step is settled. Toronto already paid for the answers to plenty of questions. This is a question it hasn't paid to answer yet — and the honest version of this paper says that plainly, instead of filling the gap with a number that sounds right.
