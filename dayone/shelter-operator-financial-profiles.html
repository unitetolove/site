Who Gets Paid, How Much, and Who Doesn't Have to Say — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/shelter-operator-financial-profiles.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/shelter-operator-financial-profiles.md, research/cards/shelter-operator-financial-profiles.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Every organization named in this brief is a registered charity in good standing with the Canada Revenue Agency, delivering a contracted public service under public disclosure rules that already exist. Nothing here alleges wrongdoing by anyone. What it does is put side by side what those existing disclosure rules actually produce — and where they produce nothing at all.

The spectrum nobody would guess from the outside

Toronto's shelter sector isn't one funding model. Homes First Society runs on roughly 94-95% government money. Fred Victor Centre: 82.8%. Dixon Hall: 86%. On the other end, Covenant House Toronto is donor-funded first — 71.9% from donations, only 9.4% from government. Scott Mission goes further still: just 26% government-funded, the rest from cash and in-kind donations, and it holds the highest independent charity rating (5 stars from Charity Intelligence) of any operator in this file. Four confirmed operators alone combine for roughly $154.5 million a year in government funding — and that's before counting the roughly 75-81 operators the City actually contracts with system-wide.

That spread matters because it means "how a shelter operator is funded" and "how well-run a shelter operator is" are two separate questions, and the public record answers them separately, not together.

Reserves: two operators, two completely different stories, both defensible

Covenant House Toronto holds $48.5 million in reserves — 1.55 years of its own program spending, the largest reserve position in the sector by a wide margin. YWCA Toronto sits at negative $42.4 million. Read those two numbers next to each other with no context and one looks flush, the other looks troubled. Neither read is right. YWCA's negative position is a $56 million mortgage taken out specifically to build housing — a deliberate capital bet, not distress. Covenant House's reserve reflects an operator that runs mostly outside the City's per-diem contractor relationship, funded overwhelmingly by donors who expect a cushion. Two legitimate, different financial strategies, not a good number and a bad number.

The disclosure gap that isn't a scandal, but is real

Ontario's Sunshine List discloses every public-sector employee earning over $100,000. It's the main public window into what shelter-sector executives actually make. WoodGreen's CEO tops the list among operators in this file at $358,492. Dixon Hall's CEO: $306,000. Fred Victor's: $206,371.

Homes First Society and Covenant House Toronto aren't on it at all. Not "hard to find" — genuinely absent, confirmed by a dedicated historical search. Homes First's last Sunshine List record is from 2011, a single $114,000 entry. Covenant House has no record in any year checked. Both organizations' compensation, through their own separate CRA filings, is confirmed to clear the disclosure threshold by other measures. Nobody in this file's source material asserts either organization is doing anything wrong — the Sunshine List's coverage rule is keyed to a specific funding-source and employer-classification test, not simply "gets public money," and there's a real structural lead for at least one of the two (Homes First operates as two separate CRA-registered entities, an operating society and a foundation) that hasn't been chased down far enough to actually explain the gap. It's an open question, stated as one.

Overhead: a number that means less than it looks like it means

Overhead rates (admin plus fundraising as a share of spending) range from 21% at Homes First to 55% at Eva's Initiatives, with Fred Victor and Dixon Hall both around 31%. There's no Toronto-shelter-specific benchmark for what's "too high" — Charity Intelligence's own methodology flags Eva's 55% as outside its reasonable range, but that's one rating agency's general nonprofit-sector standard, not a shelter-sector-calibrated one. A reader who wants to use overhead rate as a report card should know the report card doesn't have an answer key specific to this sector.

What this file doesn't have

Seven real, confirmed Toronto shelter and housing-support operators — Na-Me-Res (a 69-bed Indigenous-led shelter), Sojourn House (76-bed refugee shelter), Houselink and Mainstay, Margaret's, the John Howard Society of Toronto, Fife House, and Christie Ossington Neighbourhood Centre — have no locatable financial profile through the secondary database (Charity Intelligence) this research relied on. That's almost certainly a coverage gap in the aggregator, not a disclosure failure by the organizations — each files directly with the CRA regardless — but it means this file's own comparative picture is incomplete for exactly the smaller and more specialized operators a reader might most want visibility into.

And the headline "four operators = 32.7% of the shelter budget" figure that circulates in this space is measured against a $471.79 million TSSS budget figure that is not the current one. The 2026 TSSS operating budget is $786.068 million. Anyone repeating that percentage should recompute it against the year they're actually talking about, not the year the original calculation happened to use.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Extend compensation disclosure to every CRA-registered charity holding a Toronto shelter contract (RC-SOFP-01) — a City-level contract condition that would close the Homes First/Covenant House gap directly, without waiting on a provincial legislative fix. 2. Publish a stated reserves-adequacy benchmark for purchase-of-service shelter operators (RC-SOFP-02) — so the next reader comparing Covenant House's $48.5 million to YWCA's -$42.4 million has an actual standard to measure against, instead of two numbers and no context.

Neither requires inventing a new authority or a new number. Both ask the City to make explicit what it currently leaves implicit.
