Public Health Emergency Resilience — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.1 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto's emergency-response system got a real, funded upgrade this year. At the same time, the province revealed a different kind of failure that complicates the easy story about "just invest more in preparedness."

The paramedic story: real progress, real limits

Toronto's ambulances have been under strain for years — this project's own earlier research found zero ambulances available across the whole city 1,200 times in 2023, up from just 29 times in 2019. That strain didn't stop in 2024: the Paramedic Service's own newest staffing analysis found the total time the city spent with low ambulance availability rose 300 percent compared to before the pandemic. In response, City Council has approved a real plan: 331 new frontline paramedics added between 2025 and 2028, with a specific, public target of increasing ambulance availability by up to 20 percent by the end of that period.

That's genuine progress — a costed, dated, public commitment, not just an advocacy talking point. But it doesn't fix the actual root of the problem. The paramedic service's own numbers show most of an ambulance's time isn't spent driving or treating patients — it's spent waiting in hospital hallways to hand a patient off, because hospitals don't have capacity to receive them quickly. Adding more paramedics adds more capacity to wait in more hallways unless that hospital-side bottleneck also gets fixed, and that's a provincial responsibility, not a City one. There is some good news on that front too: a provincially-funded program using dedicated hospital nurses to speed up ambulance handoffs supported over 40,000 patient handoffs in a single recent year — a real, working mitigation, even if nobody has published data on how much time it actually saves per handoff.

Winter is handled — for now

Toronto's plan to get people experiencing homelessness through the cold this winter is detailed and substantial: about 1,275 additional spaces, split across permanent housing, warming centres that open at minus five degrees, new shelter beds, and extra surge capacity for the coldest nights. The City is currently sheltering almost 9,000 people a night. But the City says this plainly, in its own words: this "momentum is at risk without stable funding agreements between all three orders of government." Translation: this year's plan is funded. Nobody found in this research has confirmed next year's is.

The PPE story nobody was expecting

Here's the part that complicates the simple "we didn't stockpile enough before COVID, so let's stockpile more" lesson. In December 2025, Ontario's independent Auditor General found the province has written off more than $1.4 billion worth of expired protective equipment — masks, gowns, gloves — since 2021. The province signed contracts back in 2020 and 2021, at the height of pandemic panic-buying, that lock it into buying 188 million surgical masks every single year regardless of whether anyone actually needs them. Last year it only used 21 percent of that contracted volume. Nearly $126 million more is projected to expire and go to waste in the next five years if nothing changes.

The part that should really sting: despite all that stockpiled equipment, only 2 percent of it actually reaches hospitals — and hospitals say the province can't meet their needs. Ontario also burns its expired stock for heat energy, while British Columbia, right next door, recycles the same kind of expired material instead. The Auditor General pointed that out directly, by name.

This isn't a story about under-preparedness anymore. It's a story about a government that overcorrected after COVID, locked itself into inflexible contracts under crisis pressure, and never built the basic inventory tracking to know what it actually has or where it should go. The good news: Supply Ontario, the agency responsible, has agreed to fix all six things the Auditor General recommended. The bad news: nobody found in this research knows what share of any of this stockpile — used or wasted — actually reaches Toronto specifically.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't confirm whether the paramedic service's "300 percent increase in low availability" and last year's "1,200 times with zero ambulances" describe the same thing at the same scale — they're two different measurements from two different reports. It doesn't have a current Toronto Public Health budget number for 2026. It doesn't know how much time the dedicated offload-nurse program actually saves per patient, only how many patients it's touched. And it doesn't know who holds Ontario's long-term PPE supply contracts — the Auditor General's report, as covered by the wire services this research reviewed, doesn't name the company or companies collecting guaranteed annual payments for masks nobody's using.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Make the paramedic service publicly report, every year, whether it's actually hitting its own 20-percent-by-2028 target (a recommendation card) — so a real, funded promise doesn't quietly become an unmeasured one, the way the previous staffing plan's own results were never found published anywhere in this research. 2. Ask the province for a Toronto-specific accounting of where the PPE stockpile actually goes (a recommendation card) — riding on reforms the province has already agreed to in principle, to find out whether Toronto's own hospitals and care facilities are among the 2 percent getting served, or among the majority that isn't.

Neither move requires new legislation or a big new budget ask. Both just require someone to check whether promises already made are being kept.
