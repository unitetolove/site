The Number Everyone Cites Isn't the Number You Think It Is — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/homelessness-population-estimate-methodology.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/homelessness-population-estimate-methodology.md, research/cards/homelessness-population-estimate-methodology.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Every homelessness number you've seen for Toronto is real. It's also almost certainly not the number you think it is. The City runs at least four separate counting systems, each measuring a genuinely different thing, and none of them are designed to answer the question most people actually want answered: how many people, total, don't have secure permanent housing in this city right now.

What the official count actually measures

Toronto's Street Needs Assessment — the headline number, an estimated 15,418 in October 2024, down to an estimated 12,196 by October 2025 — is a single-night census. It counts people in shelters, people visible outdoors, and people in a handful of provincially run settings, on one specific night, following a national standard. The City says this plainly, in its own words, on its own methodology page: the count "did not include people experiencing hidden homelessness, such as people temporarily staying with friends or family." That's not a critic's complaint. That's the instrument's own stated scope. The 21% drop between 2024 and 2025 is mostly a falling refugee-claimant caseload, not a uniform improvement — outdoor homelessness's share actually doubled in two boroughs over the same period, even as the citywide total fell.

Three more numbers, three more questions

Ask "how many people used the shelter system last year" and you get 24,200 — the full-year distinct-persons count, at least as of the 2024 TSSS Annual Report. Ask "how many people are actively cycling through the system right now" and you get 10,569 — everyone who's used a shelter at least once in the trailing three months. Ask "how many beds are full tonight" and you get roughly 7,800-9,800 depending on the night and the season — a nightly occupancy snapshot. These are not three ways of saying the same thing. They're three different populations measured over three different time windows, and the City's own Shelter System Flow Data page states the tension directly: nightly occupancy in 2023 was 9,010, and close to 22,000 different people used the system that year. Citing any one of these as if it were the others is exactly the kind of conflation this whole project's own corrections history has already caught happening.

The number nobody wants to own

Buried in a government transition binder — reported by name, by a bylined journalist, and republished by eight regional outlets with no contradicting follow-up — is an "unofficial estimate" that put total Ontario homelessness at roughly 234,000 people. That's nearly nine times what the province's own Auditor General's math implies from the last hard municipal count. A government spokesperson called it "inaccurate" after it became public. They never offered a corrected number. Two different ways of scaling that figure down to Toronto both land in the same rough place — 30,000 to 45,000 people, two to three times the official SNA count. That range isn't confirmed. It also isn't a fringe number someone made up for effect. It came from the government's own internal paperwork.

The fix nobody's built yet

There's a real, peer-reviewed, already-proven method for measuring hidden homelessness directly instead of extrapolating it from a disputed provincial figure — the Chicago Coalition for the Homeless built a census-microdata model for exactly this, and it's already been used successfully in at least one major North American city. Nobody has run it for Toronto. That's not a criticism of anyone currently doing this work; it's a specific, nameable, fundable gap this leaf's own evidence base identifies as the single most useful thing that could happen next.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't tell you which number to use in an argument — that's a downstream, card-level question, not this document's job. It doesn't confirm whether the By-Name List's estimated 18-20% undercount and the Shelter Flow data's own approximately 18% figure are the same statistic counted twice or two separate numbers that happen to agree — flagged ⚠️ VERIFY and unresolved as of this pass. And it doesn't resolve — deliberately, and on purpose — where the line sits between this leaf and a possible future one specifically about reconciling the official count against higher field and advocate estimates. That boundary question is flagged as open in this project's own backlog, not settled here, and not an invitation to spin up a new leaf before that decision gets made deliberately.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Commission a real Toronto-specific hidden-homelessness study, using the Chicago model or an equivalent published method, instead of continuing to extrapolate from a disputed provincial figure (RC-HPEM-01). 2. Publish one plain-language page explaining which number means what — SNA, Shelter Flow, nightly occupancy, By-Name List — cross-linked from every dashboard that currently reports one of these numbers without saying which one it is (RC-HPEM-02).

Neither requires inventing new data collection from scratch. One asks for a study using a method that already exists. The other asks the City to explain, in one place, what it's already publishing in four.
