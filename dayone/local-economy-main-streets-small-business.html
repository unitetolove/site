Local Economy, Main Streets & Small Business — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/local-economy-main-streets-small-business.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/local-economy-main-streets-small-business.md, research/cards/local-economy-main-streets-small-business.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto invented the main-street support model and still runs the biggest version of it on Earth. That part of the story hasn't changed. What's changed since this project last looked closely is that some of the City's own promises have actually landed, and some of the pressure the City can't touch has kept building anyway.

What actually landed

The tax break for small businesses that was only a plan a year ago is now real. Council approved it, the Province agreed to match it on its side of the bill, and as of this year it's a 20% discount on municipal property taxes for about 28,000 businesses across roughly two-thirds of the city's commercial properties. That's not a promise anymore — it's a line item that's already showing up in owners' tax bills. Alongside it, the City added a few smaller but real pieces: patio and accessibility grants opening this spring worth up to $7,500 a business, a hotline for businesses to flag red tape that's becoming a permanent service instead of a pilot, and a promised one-stop small-business office still being built out. The City's Business Improvement Area network — the century-old model Toronto itself invented in Bloor West Village in 1970 — has grown again too, now 86 districts strong, still expanding, still the largest network of its kind anywhere.

The City's own ten-year economic plan, "Sidewalks to Skylines," reports it's gotten through the vast majority of what it set out to do in its first year — more than nine in ten of its original actions either done or underway. A newer piece, an Inclusive Economic Development Framework adopted this spring, is meant to tie land-use planning itself to keeping commercial space affordable and diverse, not just handing out grants after the fact.

What hasn't moved

None of that touches the actual mechanism driving small businesses out of their spaces: the lease itself. Ontario still has no commercial rent control. A landlord can double or triple the rent at renewal and there's no ceiling on it, the same as it's always been. The City's own plan includes a promise to lobby the Province for commercial rent protection by 2030 — but nothing found in this pass shows the Province has moved on it, and it's not even clear from the public record whether that specific promise counts as "done" or "still in progress" inside the City's own 90%-complete number.

And there's real evidence the pressure hasn't let up. Commercial and retail lease rates across the Toronto region rose by about 18% in a single year — from roughly $25 a square foot to nearly $30 — while the number of actual retail leases and sales being signed dropped over the same stretch. That's not proof of any one story, but it's the shape you'd expect if rising asking rents are pricing some tenants and buyers out rather than drawing more of them in.

The City's own plan names a specific tool aimed at exactly this problem — a Main Street Resiliency Fund meant to protect independent businesses from construction disruption and give them some kind of formal "replacement policy" protection when redevelopment threatens their space. It's a real, named commitment. But this pass couldn't find out whether it's actually running yet, how much money is behind it, or whether a single business has been helped by it so far. That's not the same as saying it's failing — it might just not be reported publicly in a findable way yet. Either way, it's a gap worth naming plainly rather than assuming the best.

The two ideas this leaf's cards propose

1. A "legacy business" grant, layered on top of the tax break that already exists (RC-LEM-01). San Francisco runs a program that does something Toronto's current tax break doesn't: it rewards businesses specifically for having stuck around — thirty years or more in one place, genuinely part of a neighbourhood's identity — with an actual annual grant, not just a lower tax bill available to any qualifying property regardless of who's renting it. Toronto's version of this would be modest at first, built on top of the tax subclass rather than replacing it, aimed at the kind of long-standing, often immigrant- or family-owned businesses that give a street its character and that are usually the first to get priced out.

2. Making the Main Street Resiliency Fund's status public (RC-LEM-02). If the Fund is working, Council and the public should be able to see that clearly in the City's own yearly progress report instead of it disappearing into a "90% of everything is done or underway" headline number. If it isn't running yet, that's worth knowing too. This card doesn't ask for new money — it asks the City to be plain about where an already-announced commitment actually stands.

What this doesn't settle

Neither idea touches the rent itself, because the City genuinely can't — that lever sits at Queen's Park, and nothing in the public record says that's about to change. Neither idea comes with a confirmed price tag yet; both would need real cost figures from a comparable program before Council could act on them seriously. And this brief can't tell you whether small businesses on Toronto's main streets are, on net, doing better or worse than a year ago — the honest answer is that some real numbers point to ongoing rent pressure, some real City programs have genuinely delivered, and no single figure ties the two together. That's not a dodge; it's what the actual public record supports as of this pass, and it's worth saying that way rather than picking whichever half of the story sounds more finished.
