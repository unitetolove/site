The Population Toronto Can Count, and the One It Can't — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Two very different kinds of non-citizens show up in Toronto's shelter system, and the City's own data can only see one of them clearly.

The one the City tracks precisely

Refugee claimants have to disclose their status to get access to claimant-specific supports, which is exactly why Toronto's numbers on this group are solid. Nationally, this group made up 6.6% of Canadian shelter users in 2024 — up from 5.1% the year before, and just 0.9% in 2021. Toronto's own share was 53% in October 2024, roughly eight times the national rate that same year. That's not a stable number, though: by August and September 2025, Toronto's nightly refugee-claimant shelter population had fallen from a peak around 6,500-6,600 down to roughly 3,400-3,500 — tracking a 34% drop in national asylum claims over the same window, not a Toronto-specific policy shift. The City's own target is a stabilized 1,000-bed dedicated refugee shelter system by the end of 2026.

There's a real federal program behind this population — IHAP, the Interim Housing Assistance Program — because refugee claimants are, by definition, inside an active federal process the federal government is funding a response to.

The one the City can't see, by design

Toronto has had a sanctuary-city policy since 2013 — Access T.O. — that tells City staff not to ask about immigration status when someone needs a shelter bed. That's a real, deliberate equity choice, and it means anyone without legal status can get help without fear of triggering an immigration consequence. It also means the City's own data structurally cannot say how many undocumented people are actually in its shelters, because nobody's tracking that category at all. The federal government's own national estimate of the undocumented population runs somewhere between 500,000 and nearly a million people — a genuinely wide range, because nobody can count this population precisely anywhere in the country, let alone city by city.

Here's the sharper point: there is no federal program at all for this group. Not an underfunded one — none. IHAP exists because refugee claimants are in a federal process. People without status aren't in any federal process by definition, so there's no mechanism designed to fund what Toronto's own sanctuary policy commits it to providing them anyway.

A new risk nobody's measured yet

A federal law that got Royal Assent in March 2026 — Bill C-12 — creates new rules that can bar some refugee claimants from a full hearing if they filed more than 12 months after arriving, or crossed from the US and waited more than two weeks to file. As of April 2026, 30,000 people nationally had already gotten a letter warning them this might apply. Here's why that matters for this brief specifically: someone who loses their claimant status doesn't stop needing a shelter bed. They just move from the population the City counts and the federal government funds, into the population the City can't count and nobody funds. Nobody has figured out yet how many of those 30,000 people are in Toronto, or when any of this would actually show up as shelter demand. It's a real, documented mechanism with an unknown Toronto-sized effect.

There's some good news pulling the other way, too. Federal Canada is cutting international student and temporary worker numbers sharply — permits down from over 305,000 to around 150,000 for 2026 — and Ottawa says openly that housing pressure is part of the reason. And a new rule from May 2026 lets people who lose status apply to stay as visitors while they sort out their next steps, instead of falling into undocumented status the moment a permit expires. Both of those cut against, not toward, this population becoming a bigger problem for Toronto's shelters going forward.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't say how big the without-status population actually is in Toronto's shelters — that number doesn't exist honestly, and manufacturing one would be worse than not having it. It doesn't say how many Bill C-12 letter recipients are in Toronto or when any resulting shelter demand would hit. And it doesn't cover the City's 2022-2023 decision to bar refugee claimants from base shelter beds, or the newcomer-settlement-pressure story more broadly — those live in two other documents in this project, on purpose, so this one can stay focused on what the numbers actually show.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Publish a Toronto-specific version of the federal shelter study's own citizenship breakdown (a recommendation card) — so Toronto's numbers can be compared to the national trend on equal terms, not just a single headline percentage. 2. Track Bill C-12's Toronto-specific impact before it shows up as an unexplained increase in an uncountable population (a recommendation card) — using the data relationship the City already has with the federal government through IHAP.

Neither of these asks Toronto to abandon its sanctuary policy or start tracking immigration status directly. They ask it to get better at measuring what it can measure, and to watch, in advance, for the specific mechanism that could quietly grow the population it can't.
