Family Doctors and Primary Care — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Almost 2 million people in this province don't have a family doctor. That number used to be quoted as 2.5 million, and before that there was a forecast of 4.4 million by this year. None of those numbers are wrong exactly — they're measuring slightly different things, at different times, with different counting rules. What hasn't changed is that this is still one of the most concretely felt failures in daily life, and Toronto carries a disproportionate share of it.

What's actually happening

Ontario's own health ministry, working with a research network called INSPIRE-PHC, rebuilt how it counts who has a family doctor. Using the new count, as of June 2025 there were about 1.98 million people in the province without one — lower than the roughly 2.3 to 2.5 million figures that have circulated for the past few years, and much lower than a 2023 forecast that predicted 4.4 million people — 1 in 4 Ontarians — would be without one by this year. That worst-case number didn't happen. That's genuinely good news, though part of the improvement is simply that the province started counting differently, not only that more people got attached.

Toronto's own numbers, from a 2024 release, showed more than 516,000 Torontonians without a family doctor, on track toward nearly a million by 2026. Within the city, the gap isn't even — team-based care, where a doctor works alongside nurses, pharmacists, and social workers, reaches as low as 6 percent of patients in parts of Scarborough, against a provincial rate where almost three-quarters of patients don't have team-based support at all.

The province is actually spending money on this

This isn't just an advocacy ask sitting in a policy paper. Ontario launched a Primary Care Action Plan with the explicit goal of connecting everyone to primary care by 2029, backed by a $2.1 billion investment — and, on budget-coverage reporting rather than a figure pulled straight from the budget document itself, that's since grown toward $3.4 billion with this year's budget. It's already produced results that can be checked: 78 new or expanded primary care teams from the 2024 round now serve more than 490,000 people, beating their own target. In the first nine months of 2025 alone, about 275,000 more people got attached to a provider, putting the province on track to hit its 300,000-person goal for the year. A further round, launched in September 2025, is putting over $250 million toward connecting another 500,000 people.

The province specifically targeted its funding calls at the 125 communities with the highest number of unattached people. Given what we know about Scarborough's numbers, it's a reasonable guess that some of that funding is aimed there. But nothing found in this review actually confirms it. That's a real gap — not a criticism of the program, just an honest "we don't know yet."

A working example, right here

Northwest Toronto has been called a primary care desert — one of the highest rates of unattached patients in the whole Toronto Central health region. Humber River Health and the University of Toronto's family medicine program built something simple: anyone who shows up at the hospital without a family doctor can now be referred straight to an on-site clinic, the same way they'd be referred to any other specialist. By 2026 that clinic is expected to be caring for 10,000 people who didn't have a doctor before. It's not a City of Toronto program — it's a hospital and a university — but it's proof the model works here, not just somewhere else.

What the City itself has actually done — as far as we could find

This is the honest gap in this file. The province holds almost all the real levers here: it pays doctors, it regulates who can practice what, it funds the teams. The City's role, per this project's own earlier research, is supposed to be things like offering land or space for clinics, and pushing loudly for the provincial reforms that actually fix the shortage. But nothing found in this review shows a specific City of Toronto budget line, council motion, or space commitment aimed at this problem. That doesn't mean nothing exists — it means nobody found it this time. It's flagged, not assumed either way.

What this doesn't settle

It doesn't say whether Toronto's own current shortage numbers have moved since 2024 — nobody has recounted them using the province's newer method. It doesn't confirm whether Scarborough is actually getting any of the new provincial funding, despite being exactly the kind of neighbourhood the funding is supposed to prioritize. And it doesn't identify a single confirmed City of Toronto action on this file, which is either a real gap in what the City has done or a gap in what this review could find — those are different claims, and this file doesn't collapse them into one.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Offer City-owned space to provincially funded clinics, starting in the neighbourhoods already proven to need it most (a recommendation card) — Scarborough and northwest Toronto specifically, using buildings the City already owns rather than waiting for a new capital project. 2. Get Council to formally ask the province exactly which Toronto communities its funding is actually reaching (a recommendation card) — a low-cost, almost-free motion that would replace a reasonable guess with a real answer, and give future advocacy something concrete to point at either way.

Neither requires inventing new numbers or a new program from scratch. Both start from what the province and a Toronto hospital have already built, and ask the City to either use what it already owns or simply ask a direct question it hasn't asked yet.
