Housing Supply & Affordability — Day-One Paper

DRAFT v1.1 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis Date: 2026-07-13 · File: research/dayone/housing-supply-affordability.md

Status: DRAFT v1.1 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis · Date: 2026-07-13 Built only from research/cards/housing-supply-affordability.md (RC-HSA-01–RC-HSA-03) and research/backgrounders/housing-supply-affordability.md; every claim below traces to one of those two documents.

What a mayor does in week one on this file

Toronto already has the tools most cities are still asking for: as-of-right fourplexes since 2023, a partial sixplex reform, a working development-charge waiver, and a $471.1 million federal Housing Accelerator Fund agreement rewarding exactly this work (RC-HSA-01). The week-one job isn't inventing new policy — it's closing the gap between what Council has already voted to permit in law and what actually gets built, and being honest about the one lever this office does not fully control.

1. Bring city-wide sixplex legalization back to Council, and name why it's urgent. Council's June 25, 2025 vote (18–6) diluted a staff proposal for city-wide as-of-right sixplexes into a ward-by-ward opt-in, covering only Toronto & East York and Ward 23 so far. That reform is one of 35 milestones under the City's HAF agreement, and non-compliance risks a 25%-of-annual-payment federal clawback (RC-HSA-01). This is not a new ask of the province or the feds — Council already has the zoning authority; it exercised it for fourplexes city-wide in 2023 and for partial sixplexes in 2025. Week one: direct staff to bring a renewed city-wide motion back to Council, and be plain with residents in the still-excluded wards (most of Etobicoke, North York, Scarborough) about what's at stake — the housing this unlocks, and the real concerns (parking, privacy, infrastructure capacity) behind the 2025 compromise (RC-HSA-01).

2. Make permitting speed a standing, public number — not a one-time claim. The City's own reporting suggests as-of-right multiplex permits now issue in roughly 10–21 days, down from 6–12 months before 2024 — but that figure is itself unconfirmed in the source it comes from (RC-HSA-03). Rather than repeat it as settled, week one sets up the fix: a fixed processing-time target for all EHON-eligible permits (fourplex, sixplex, garden/laneway suite), queued separately from larger discretionary applications, with quarterly public reporting of actual median times and completed units, not applications filed. This sits entirely within existing municipal authority, costs staff-time rather than capital, and supports the same HAF agreement's approvals-speed commitment (RC-HSA-03). Toronto's own land-use evidence names the permission-to-completion conversion rate as the single biggest unknown between the reform's projected, roughly 54,600 units by 2031 (an estimated 163,785 by 2051) and what actually gets lived in (RC-HSA-01, RC-HSA-03).

3. Send Queen's Park a formal, specific ask on inclusionary zoning — and don't pretend it's the City's call. Toronto's IZ policy, live since August 2025, is capped by the province at a flat 5% of units, 25-year affordability — well below the graduated 5–22% rates rising through 2030 with 99-year affordability that Council's own 2021 bylaw had actually set, before a provincial override replaced it. This is not a City choice to reverse; it's a provincial regulation under the same Planning Act authority that sets municipal zoning power in the first place. Week one: pass a Council resolution asking the Province to restore that discretion and expand IZ beyond its current 800-metre transit-station radius. Say plainly to residents that this ask may go nowhere without provincial political change — the honest position, not a promise (RC-HSA-02).

What this office does not control, and won't pretend to

Two things bound how far any of the above goes. First, market-rate supply — fourplexes, sixplexes, the rest — does not reach the households who need it most: Canada's non-market housing stock sits at roughly 3.5–4% of all housing against an OECD average near 7%, a public-investment question, not a zoning one (RC-HSA-02, backgrounder). Second, a meaningful share of "new supply" isn't becoming secure homes at all — investors owned an estimated 38.9% of Toronto-area condo apartments in 2022, and Ontario's rent-control exemption for units first occupied after November 15, 2018 means new supply offers its own tenants no protection from large increases (backgrounder). Neither is fixed by anything in this paper; both are named so zoning reform isn't mistaken for the whole answer.

The honest bottom line

None of these three actions requires waiting on the province or Ottawa to move first — RC-HSA-01 and RC-HSA-03 are entirely within existing City authority, and RC-HSA-02 is a formal ask, not a dependency. The binding constraint on Toronto's own housing file, right now, is Toronto's own Council process: the same body that legalized fourplexes city-wide in 2023 pulled back from doing the same for sixplexes in 2025. Week one is about finishing what's already been voted on once, measuring it honestly in public, and being clear with residents about which parts of this file the City actually controls — and which parts, like inclusionary-zoning depth, it can only ask for.

Uncertainties this paper inherits from its cards

Whether any ward beyond Toronto & East York/Ward 23 has opted into sixplex permission since June 2025 is unconfirmed as of this writing (RC-HSA-01). The 6–12-month-to-10–21-day permit-timeline improvement this paper cites as motivation for standing public reporting is itself flagged VERIFY, not independently confirmed, in the source it comes from (RC-HSA-03). No source in the evidence base quantifies the expected unit yield from restoring graduated inclusionary-zoning rates (RC-HSA-02). None of these gaps are resolved here — they are named so this paper isn't read as more certain than its own sources are.
