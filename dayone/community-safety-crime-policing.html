Policing: What the Budget Buys, and What the Audits Found Instead — Day-One Paper

DRAFT File: research/dayone/community-safety-crime-policing.md

File: research/dayone/community-safety-crime-policing.md · Status: DRAFT · v1.1 · 2026-07-13 Issue slug: community-safety-crime-policing · Draws only from: research/backgrounders/community-safety-crime-policing.md and research/cards/community-safety-crime-policing.md Layer: L-PLATFORM (opinionated synthesis; every factual premise traces to the backgrounder/cards above)

Toronto already paid for the answers — a 6-minute response-time target the Police Service Board itself approved in 1995, and average response times that are now three to eight times longer. What the city hasn't paid for yet is a budget process that checks whether new money actually buys back that gap, including the uncertainties about whether it does.

Where things stand

Toronto's Police Service Board approved a $1.43 billion operating budget for 2026 — a confirmed $93.8 million increase, independently verified against a directly fetched news report, not just a press release — framed around hiring 360 more officers. That 360-officer hiring figure itself, along with the "~7%" characterization of the increase and the exact board-vote date, is not yet independently confirmed from a primary document (it traces to a police-service social-media statement and search-result synthesis) — flagged here, not carried as settled, even though the underlying $1.43B/$93.8M dollar figures are. That request follows a real, documented problem: the City's own 2019 data shows average response times of 19 and 50 minutes for the two most urgent call categories, against a 6-minute target the board approved back in 1995. Nobody disputes the gap is real.

What's less settled is whether more money reliably closes gaps like it. The Auditor General of Ontario already ran this experiment once, on the OPP: a 26% increase in operating expenditure between 2011 and 2020 coincided with 9% fewer officers and 48% fewer patrol hours, not more of either. Nationally, Canada's Crime Severity Index fell 4% in 2024 — ending three years of increases — in the same broad period police strength per capita hit its lowest rate since 1970. A peer-reviewed study of 20 Canadian municipalities, Toronto among them, found no consistent relationship between rising per-capita police spending and falling crime rates over more than a decade of data — this finding is itself carried at VERIFY confidence, via a news report on the study rather than the study's own text, not yet independently confirmed. None of this proves Toronto's specific request is wrong. It does mean "more officers, more capacity" is a framing the province's own auditor and an independent academic study have both already complicated.

Meanwhile, the oversight body meant to referee exactly this question — whether policing across Ontario meets the province's own "adequate and effective" standard — is brand new. The Inspectorate of Policing has existed since April 2024. In its first reporting year it took in 505 complaints and issued zero formal enforcement actions. That's not evidence the system is broken or working; it's evidence there's no track record yet either way, for a body overseeing 43 municipal services and 88 OPP detachment boards at once.

Three things worth doing, at realistic cost

First: pair every above-baseline police budget increase with a public report card on whether the last increase delivered. Before Council approves spending beyond inflation and population growth, require a published comparison of the service's current response times against its own targets, and its clearance-rate trend — the same categories of data the City's own Auditor General and Statistics Canada already produce. Order of magnitude: low tens of thousands of dollars, since this layers analysis and publication onto data mostly already collected, not a new measurement program. Paid for out of the existing budget-process operating line. This doesn't say whether Toronto's 2026 request should be approved — it says the next one should come with a report card attached, not an assumption.

Second: scale the province's own oversight body to the sector it oversees. The Inspectorate's budget and staffing should be reviewed whenever the total policing expenditure it oversees grows faster than inflation — not fixed once at its 2024 launch and left behind as the sector keeps growing. This is a provincial action; municipalities and their associations can ask for it, but only the Ministry of the Solicitor General can do it. The likely cost is a small fraction of even one percent of the $3.9 billion the Ministry already spends — money isn't the obstacle here, attention is.

Third: put enforcement and prevention spending on the same page. Toronto's 2026 request is framed entirely around uniformed hiring. Nothing in this leaf's evidence shows that's the only lever available, and the "no consistent association" study is a specific, named reason to ask the question rather than assume the answer. Requiring a companion report on the same municipality's non-enforcement violence-prevention funding, submitted alongside any above-baseline police budget request, costs about the same low tens of thousands of dollars as the first recommendation. It doesn't claim prevention spending works better than enforcement spending — no source here proves that either way. It claims Council should see both numbers side by side before voting on one of them alone.

What this doesn't settle

None of this resolves whether Toronto's specific $93.8 million increase (confirmed via independent fetch) is the right amount — that's a budget judgment this paper doesn't make. The 360-officer hiring figure attached to that increase, and its exact board-vote timing, are still flagged for independent confirmation from a primary document, not treated as settled. None of this resolves whether the 20-municipality funding-crime study holds up under closer scrutiny of its own methodology — a peer-reviewed, first-of-its-kind finding, carried here pending a direct read of the study itself. And none of this touches complaint handling, discipline, or reform — a real question that belongs to a different piece of this same broader file. What's proposed here is narrower and cheaper: before the city spends more on the same lever it's already pulled, make it show its work on whether pulling that lever again is what the evidence — including the uncertainties in that evidence — actually supports.
