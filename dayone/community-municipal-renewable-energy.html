Community and Municipal Renewable Energy — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Over 2,000 Torontonians have put more than $80 million of their own money into SolarShare, the city's community solar co-op, and gotten more than $17 million of it back in interest so far, spread across 51 solar projects. That part hasn't changed. Toronto's own City Hall published a 22-page report on exactly this topic two months before this file was written. Most of what follows is reading that report closely, not guessing at it.

What's actually happening

SolarShare is still exactly what it's been described as before. What has changed, confirmed directly by one of SolarShare's own board members, is the underlying economics — the original above-market prices that made those returns possible were tied to 20-year provincial contracts from the Green Energy Act era, and those contracts are now maturing. The co-op is in the middle of figuring out what its next chapter pays.

On the City side, Toronto Hydro is beating its own targets. By the end of 2024 it had hit 154% of its 2023-2025 solar-connection goal and 148% of its storage-connection goal. It also has a real, quantified success story most people haven't heard: a demand-response program at one transformer station alone deferred approximately $30 million in capital upgrades between 2015 and 2019, and the utility is now scaling that same approach — paying large commercial and institutional customers to cut demand at peak times instead of building new poles and wires — across six stations, expecting another approximately $10 million in avoided costs.

The gap that's still open

Ontario made a real move in July 2025: a new regulation let large industrial and commercial electricity users sign long-term contracts with renewable generators anywhere in the province, cutting their bills. That's genuinely useful — but it's for big players, not small ones. The City's own April 2026 report says the thing SolarShare-style community projects actually need — "a community solar regulatory framework" — still doesn't exist in Ontario, and the City is actively lobbying the province to create one. The July 2025 fix and the still-missing community framework are two different problems. Don't let the first one get mistaken for solving the second.

Where the two honest sides of the argument actually are

For what's already working: this isn't aspirational. SolarShare exists, has a real track record, and real members. Toronto Hydro's own numbers on solar, storage, and demand-response are already ahead of target and already saving real money. None of this needs to be invented.

For staying honest about the ceiling: Toronto Hydro says this plainly in its own words — it "cannot act unilaterally" and "does not have the authority to establish or override provincial plans for large-scale generation." Everything the City is doing well, it's doing inside a lane the province still controls. The specific policy lever that would matter most for community-scale solar — the regulatory framework — is the one thing nobody in Toronto can just decide to build.

Both of these are true at once. Keep running and expanding what the City and Toronto Hydro already control — rooftop solar on City buildings, the demand-response program, streamlined connection times — and keep pressing the one ask that actually needs Queen's Park to move.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't confirm the exact current interest rate SolarShare pays its bondholders — the figure "up to 4.0%" showed up in search results but wasn't pulled from a freshly-fetched version of the co-op's own bond page. It doesn't confirm the precise legal text of the July 2025 regulation — everything here about it comes from law-firm summaries that agree with each other, not from the regulation itself. It doesn't explain where the City's own claim that clean-electrification spending generates an "estimated $4-8 in economic activity" per dollar actually comes from — that number is stated in the City's report without a visible source note in what was reviewed here. And it doesn't say when, or whether, Ontario will actually build the community-solar framework it's being asked for — there's no timeline anywhere in what's been found.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Keep the "community solar framework" ask visible and separate from the July 2025 large-consumer reform, so nobody mistakes a partial fix for a complete one (a recommendation card). 2. Make Toronto Hydro's demand-response savings a standing, annual public number, not a one-time mention in a single report — the program already works, so show the receipts every year (a recommendation card).

Neither move invents a new program. Both ask for the City to keep doing, visibly and consistently, what it's already started.
