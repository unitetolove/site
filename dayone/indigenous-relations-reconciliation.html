Toronto's Reconciliation Action Plan — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/indigenous-relations-reconciliation.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/indigenous-relations-reconciliation.md, research/cards/indigenous-relations-reconciliation.md, research/briefs/indigenous-relations-reconciliation_toronto.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Honest limitation, stated up front per research/indigenous/DESIGN.md §7: this piece is written by a non-Indigenous-curated research project. It is not an Indigenous perspective, and no Indigenous reader has reviewed it. Where it states what an Indigenous person or organization says, it is quoting them directly, by name, in their own words — never speaking for them. Curation is not consultation.

In 2022, Toronto's Council voted unanimously to spend the next ten years making 28 specific promises about how the city treats Indigenous people, land, and history. Three and a half years in, the city's own numbers say most of those promises are moving. The city's own words, in the same report, also say the plan "has not been Indigenous-led." Both of those things are true at once, and this piece doesn't pretend otherwise.

What the city actually did

Toronto's Reconciliation Action Plan (RAP) runs 2022 to 2032. It has 28 headline actions and, underneath those, 141 more specific sub-actions that City staff are supposed to actually deliver. As of the city's most recent public accounting, from November 2025: 103 of those 141 are started and moving, 3 are finished, and 18 haven't been touched yet. By the city's own count, it has put real money behind it — more than $120 million so far into Indigenous housing providers alone, $21 million into a Residential Schools memorial called the Spirit Garden that opened on the 2024 National Day for Truth and Reconciliation, and tens of millions more spread across shelters, an Indigenous entrepreneurship centre, and crisis-response services. The number of city departments actually doing something toward this plan grew from 13 to 21 over three years.

None of that is invented or rounded up generously — it's the city's own tally, in the city's own progress report.

What the city admits it got wrong

Here's the part that doesn't fit a simple good-news framing, and it comes from the same report, not from an outside critic. The city's own words: the plan "has not been Indigenous-led and important concerns remain." It names a "lack of a meaningful community advisory structure for validation, feedback, updates, or the development of required accountability measures" — meaning there's no standing, empowered body that gets to check the city's homework on this plan the way, say, a board reviews an audit. It also names "limited co-creation" and says flatly that "some actions have been undertaken without the engagement of the Indigenous community."

TASSC — the Toronto Aboriginal Support Services Council, which by the city's own description represents every Indigenous-led social service agency in the city and serves two-thirds of Toronto's Indigenous population — gets a one-time $350,000 top-up in this same report, explicitly to address what the city calls TASSC's own "longstanding underfunding." One grant, once, for a gap the city admits has been there a long time.

What that means at ground level: land acknowledgments

You've heard a land acknowledgment before a council meeting or a school assembly. Hayden King has thought about this more carefully than almost anyone — he's Anishinaabe, from Beausoleil First Nation, runs the Yellowhead Institute, and literally helped write one of the first university land acknowledgments in Canada back in 2012. He's since said, specifically about Toronto, that acknowledgments here are sometimes "simply inaccurate," repeat the same nation's name under different terms, or flatten a real relationship — the Dish With One Spoon covenant, which isn't even a treaty with government, it's older than that — into what he calls a "multicultural utopia" metaphor that doesn't hold up.

His own words on what should come after the acknowledgment: "A land acknowledgment should be an obligation... Do the internal work before you decide to step out into the world with your reconciliation mime. Do something different, actually work with Indigenous communities in a meaningful way."

That's not this project's opinion. That's a named person, on the record, in his own words, about this specific city.

The bigger picture nobody's numbers quite settle

Toronto's own report puts 73% of its sub-actions "started and in progress" — a figure that sounds like real momentum. Compare it, carefully, to what Eva Jewell found nationally. She co-authored five straight years of independent tracking of the country's 94 Truth and Reconciliation Calls to Action for the Yellowhead Institute. Her own conclusion: only five were fully completed in five years of watching, and she stopped counting because, in her words, "there is a limit to how many times we can analyze nothing." She's also named a specific pattern she doesn't think counts as progress: "the rise of consultants offering Indigenous 101 workshops, the symbolic raising of Indigenous flags, orange donuts" — activity that looks like reconciliation without being it.

"Started and in progress" and "fully completed" aren't the same yardstick, and this piece isn't claiming Toronto is quietly beating the national record. It's naming that the two pictures — a city with a lot of activity, a country with almost nothing finished — sit next to each other without a clean way to compare them yet.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Give the plan the accountability structure the city itself says is missing (RC-IR-01). The city already runs a body with real teeth in a related area — the Toronto Indigenous Community Advisory Board has actual final say over a piece of homelessness policy, not just an advisory voice. The city's own report says the RAP as a whole doesn't have anything like that. This card proposes funding and establishing one, at a scale roughly comparable to the one-time TASSC grant the city already made — not inventing a new funding mechanism, just extending what's already been tried once into something standing.

2. Publish a straight line from the eight national priorities to the actual work (RC-IR-02). Back in 2015, Toronto's Council picked eight of the national Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 94 Calls to Action as the city's own priorities. Nearly ten years later, the city tracks 141 detailed sub-actions — but doesn't publish, in the document this pass reviewed, a direct map showing which sub-actions are actually advancing which of those eight original priorities. This card asks the city to publish that crosswalk using data it already collects.

What this doesn't settle

Neither card decides whether the RAP's underlying 28 actions are the right 28, or whether $120 million in housing investment is the right amount — those are judgments for the people the plan is actually about, not for a research brief. Neither card resolves whether Toronto's 73% activity rate represents real progress or an activity measure that doesn't capture what actually matters — that's a live, honestly unresolved tension this piece states rather than papers over. What both cards do is close a specific, nameable gap: right now, there's no standing structure for Indigenous partners to formally check the city's work on this plan, and no public document showing whether the city's original eight stated priorities are actually the ones getting delivered. Closing those two gaps doesn't finish reconciliation. It makes the next stage of it checkable, by the people the city's own report says haven't had enough of a say in it so far.
