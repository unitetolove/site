Car-Free Zones & Pedestrianization — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/car-free-zones-pedestrianization.md

Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Status: DRAFT · Layer: L-PLATFORM Built from: research/backgrounders/car-free-zones-pedestrianization.md (L4) + research/cards/car-free-zones-pedestrianization.md (RC-CFZ-01, RC-CFZ-02). No claim below is freehand — every figure traces to a card or the backgrounder it draws from.

---

Toronto already ran the experiment on itself. Two programs, same city, same few years — one made permanent, one left to expire quietly. The results tell you everything about what actually decides whether a street-space program survives.

King Street got made permanent by Council back in 2019. Since then, it's kept getting investment: traffic agents posted at the busiest intersections since December 2023, a measurable speed improvement when they're there, and a few million dollars committed to physical upgrades through the City's own capital plan. It's not perfect — the corridor still loses its edge on evenings and weekends when nobody's enforcing it — but it's alive, funded, and improving.

OpenStreetsTO and ActiveTO never got that same permanence. They ran during the pandemic, people loved them, and then they just... stopped. Nothing has run since 2022. Nothing is scheduled for 2026. And it's not because people stopped wanting it — a campaign that asked the people who actually showed up found more than nine in ten of them want it back. The problem the City itself points to isn't evidence or demand. It's coordination: getting Transportation Services and Toronto Police Service to commit together needs someone at City Hall to actually push for it, and so far nobody has.

One: finish what King Street started. Close the enforcement gap. The City has been saying automated cameras are coming to cover evenings and weekends since Q3 2025 — get a public update on where that actually stands, and a real date if it hasn't shipped yet (RC-CFZ-02).

Two: give OpenStreetsTO and ActiveTO what King Street already has — a permanent by-law, not an annual maybe. Name the departments. Commit the calendar. Fund it like a program, not a pilot that keeps almost happening. The demand is already proven. The only thing missing is the decision (RC-CFZ-01).

What we don't know, said out loud. We checked the King Street enforcement figure — streetcars running 28 to 44 minutes faster with agents present — directly against the City's own staff report: it's real, and it describes a different thing than an earlier, smaller travel-time number for the same corridor (that older number was the 2017 pilot's original effect; this one is what Traffic Agents specifically added once congestion had gotten much worse by 2023). We don't know if King Street's camera enforcement has already launched since the last update we could find. We don't know exactly why City Hall hasn't pushed the inter-departmental coordination OpenStreetsTO needs, only that it hasn't. And the biggest open question in this whole file isn't ours to answer: a court case over the province's power to rip out Toronto bike lanes was argued back in January and still hasn't been decided as of this writing — whatever that ruling says will shape how much of any of this Toronto actually gets to decide for itself.

The throughline: Toronto doesn't need more evidence that car-restriction programs work here. It has its own proof, running in both directions at once, on two streets a few blocks apart. What it needs is the same thing King Street already got — a permanent decision — extended to the program its own residents are already asking for back.
