Vacant Home Tax Effectiveness — Day-One Paper

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/vacant-home-tax-effectiveness.md

Status: DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from research/cards/vacant-home-tax-effectiveness.md (RC-vht-01–02) and research/backgrounders/vacant-home-tax-effectiveness.md; every claim below traces to one of those two documents.

What a mayor does in week one on this file

Toronto's Vacant Home Tax is a real, working revenue tool that had a genuinely bad year in 2024 — and week one is about finishing the fix, not re-litigating whether the tax should exist.

1. Finish the declaration-model redesign — properly this time. The 2024 cycle's default-vacant design wrongly flagged one in five Toronto homes as vacant. The City has already responded with process fixes (earlier portal, extended deadlines, a help team), but the underlying default-vacant logic is, per the best available evidence, the actual decisive variable in whether this kind of program succeeds or fails. Week one: direct Revenue Services to move toward a presumed-occupied or verified-data model using information the City already holds, rather than relying on faster complaint-handling to paper over a structurally error-prone default (RC-vht-01).

2. Ring-fence the money, and say publicly where it's going. Vancouver's comparable tax has raised roughly $194 million since 2017 with about $142 million explicitly earmarked for affordable housing — a clean, legible commitment. No source available to this office establishes what Toronto's own $50–56 million a year is currently funding. Week one: direct staff to statutorily earmark Toronto's VHT revenue for non-market housing and report the dollar figure annually, following Vancouver's model (RC-vht-02).

3. Stop implying the VHT will move rents. It won't, on the best evidence available. The same independent study that proves the tax cuts vacancy also proves it doesn't touch average rents — the honest reason is scale: even a real, validated vacancy reduction is a rounding error against total rental stock. Week one: brief communications staff to stop selling the VHT as an affordability fix in public messaging, and start selling it as what the evidence actually supports — a vacancy-and-revenue tool that funds the housing programs that do the affordability work.

What this office does not control, and won't pretend to

Whether flagged-vacant homes actually get rented out, as opposed to merely being re-declared to dodge the tax, is something the City's own revenue services say they cannot currently confirm. No amount of declaration-model redesign fixes that measurement gap by itself — it requires a separate outcome-tracking commitment this paper does not claim RC-vht-01 alone solves.

The honest bottom line

The VHT works exactly as well as the best evidence says it works: it cuts vacancy, it raises real money, and it does not move rents. Toronto's 2024 failure was an execution problem, not proof the tool doesn't work — but fixing execution means finishing the declaration-model redesign, not just handling complaints faster next time. Week one is about doing that, being honest about what the tax can't do, and making the money's destination legible to the public.

Uncertainties this paper inherits from its cards

Neither card quantifies the redesign's exact cost or confirms its legal feasibility in Ontario — both are named as open in the backgrounder. Whether short-term rentals are being used to dodge the VHT is flagged by this leaf's own spine source as a real risk with thin evidence, not resolved by anything in this paper. Several figures this paper leans on (the $105M/year 3%-rate revenue projection, Toronto's own administration cost) carry explicit [confirm] flags inherited from the spine document and are not independently verified here.
