Citizens' Assemblies and Sortition — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-13 ·

DRAFT v1.1 — L-PLATFORM layer · Date: 2026-07-13 Built only from: our research file for that page and that page's recommendation cards. No claim in this document goes beyond what those two files already establish. Provenance: earlier research plus this review, as carried from the backgrounder/cards; see those documents for full citations.

Toronto already paid for the answers — this work reads it all, integrates it, and shows the work, including the uncertainties.

The case

A citizens' assembly gathers a randomly selected, demographically representative group of residents, gives them balanced evidence over multiple days or weeks, and asks them to deliberate and recommend — democracy by lottery rather than election. The OECD has catalogued nearly 300 such cases across 34 countries and found the process reliably produces thoughtful, trusted, common-ground recommendations, especially on the values-laden, long-term, trade-off-heavy questions that elected politics handles worst [backgrounder, "The evidence that the deliberation itself works"]. Ireland is the proof it can move the immovable: citizens' assemblies broke decades of political deadlock on same-sex marriage and abortion, with their recommendations carried into successful national referendums [backgrounder, "The Irish precedent"].

The honest catch

Deliberation is not decision. Assemblies almost always only recommend, and a high-quality recommendation changes nothing if the surrounding politics won't carry it. France's government promised to pass its 2020 climate convention's 149 proposals "without filter," then dropped or diluted most of the boldest ones — participants graded the government 3/10 [backgrounder, "The decisive weakness"]. Canada's own excellent assemblies died the other way: BC's 2004 assembly recommended a new voting system, and the 2005 referendum got 57.7% Yes against a government-set 60% threshold; Ontario's 2007 assembly's recommendation was rejected 63% by voters [backgrounder, "The decisive weakness"]. A fair reading holds both sides: it may be a coupling-and-design failure, or simply voters legitimately saying no — this file does not pick one [backgrounder, "Key tensions / tradeoffs"]. Ireland's own success also rested on conditions — a referendum culture, sovereign convening power, a specific historical moment — that this page’s evidence flags as possibly not transferring to a large, fragmented city like Toronto [backgrounder, "The Irish precedent, and why it may not transfer directly"].

Two live 2025 developments sharpen this picture. British Columbia's electoral-reform question — the closest Canadian precedent there is — is back on the table: an all-party legislative committee has formally recommended the government consider a new peoples' assembly on the same question its 2004 assembly already tackled once [backgrounder, "The decisive weakness"]. And the Netherlands ran one of the largest citizens' assemblies yet, on climate policy, with a formal difference from France's failure: the government committed, in writing, to respond to all 23 proposals within six months, naming what it adopts, rejects, and why [backgrounder, "A new, larger, and differently-coupled 2025 precedent"]. ⚠️ UPDATE (live-checked 2026-07-13): that response has now happened — the Dutch cabinet delivered it on schedule, 29 May 2026, adopting roughly half the recommendations (largely by pointing to plans already underway), still weighing 18, and declining 23 outright. The deadline held; whether "half adopted, mostly via pre-existing plans" counts as genuinely honouring the assembly's work is a fair question this document does not resolve — Parliament debates the response in September 2026, with a final Parliament/Assembly review due no later than December 2026.

Two places to start

Make it standing, not one-off, with a legal duty to respond. Create a recurring Toronto civic assembly on a fixed cycle, paired with a council rule that Council must publicly respond to every recommendation within a set window and either act or explain why not. This is modeled on Belgium's Ostbelgien permanent-council design and the Dutch six-month response mechanism just observed in 2025 — and it requires no other government's permission, since it binds Council to itself [card a recommendation card]. Cost is anchored to real comparators: BC's single 2004 assembly ran about $5.5 million; the Dutch 2025 assembly sent 70,000 invitations, of which 4,070 residents said they were willing to take part, with 175 members ultimately selected from that pool — a useful upper-bound scale reference [card a recommendation card].

Build a referral valve for genuinely deadlocked questions. Let a defined council supermajority or citizen-petition threshold send a specific stuck, values-laden question to an ad hoc assembly under the same standing infrastructure — but require the referring motion to state, in advance, exactly what Council will do with the result. That single design choice is what France got wrong and what this card is built to avoid: deciding the decision pathway before convening, not after [card a recommendation card]. Where the question touches something Toronto doesn't control — housing supply, transit funding — the assembly's output becomes an advocacy case carried upward with the weight of a representative citizen body, not a promise the City can't keep [card a recommendation card].

What's not yet settled

Neither card resolves the deepest open question here: what decision pathway a municipality without referendum machinery can actually commit to before convening an assembly at all. The right supermajority or petition threshold for a referral trigger isn't addressed anywhere in the source evidence. The Dutch government's six-month response has since arrived — on schedule, 29 May 2026 — adopting roughly half its 23-proposal report's recommendations (largely via pre-existing plans), still weighing 18, and declining 23; whether that counts as honouring the assembly's work or a softer version of France's broken promise is a fair question, not yet settled, and Parliament's September 2026 debate plus a final review due no later than December 2026 are the next points to check. The accountability question also cuts both ways: sortition members face no election to answer to, which is the whole point, but that also means no correction loop if an assembly gets something wrong [backgrounder, "Key tensions / tradeoffs"]. Neither recommendation above is costed at Toronto scale or has a confirmed funding source; both name what is and isn't settled rather than presenting an aspirational design as decided.
