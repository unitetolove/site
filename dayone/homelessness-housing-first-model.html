Housing First — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-13 ·

DRAFT v1.1 — L-PLATFORM layer · Date: 2026-07-13 Built only from: our research file for that page and that page's recommendation cards. No claim in this document goes beyond what those two files already establish. Provenance: earlier research plus this review, as carried from the backgrounder/cards; see those documents for full citations.

Toronto already paid for the answers — this work reads it all, integrates it, and shows the work, including the uncertainties.

The case

Toronto ran part of the world's largest randomized controlled trial of Housing First — give people permanent housing immediately and unconditionally, then wrap voluntary support around them, instead of making housing a reward for sobriety or treatment progress first. Among the trial's highest-needs participants, 73% ended up stably housed versus 31% under the old system — more than double, and this is trial evidence, not a hopeful pilot [backgrounder, "The decisive Canadian trial"; confirm flag inherited from source]. Toronto's own Auditor General separately found that emergency shelters cost 3 to 10 times more than supportive or subsidized housing. The city has adopted Housing First as its stated philosophy. It has not funded it as the system's default: 78% of shelter users meet the City's own chronic-homelessness threshold, and the system moved fewer than 4,000 people a year into housing against a nightly count above 12,000 [backgrounder, "Toronto's own adoption record"].

What works, proven twice more

Houston cut its homeless population 63% since 2011 without new municipal spending, by coordinating access and prioritizing the highest-need cases first. Medicine Hat, Alberta — a city of about 63,300 — became the first in Canada to reach "functional zero" chronic homelessness in June 2021 using the same tools: a real-time list of every homeless individual by name, updated at every service contact, and every provider meeting weekly to review every case. Finland took the model national, cutting shelter use 76% between 2008 and 2017. Toronto already belongs to the same national network (Built for Zero Canada) these Canadian results come from, and already has the assessment tool that network's coordinated-access work is built on [backgrounder, "City-scale precedent: Houston/Medicine Hat"].

The honest catch

Both Finland and Medicine Hat later slipped. Finland's homelessness rose for two straight years — up 20% in 2025 alone, the largest single-year increase since it started tracking the number — after cuts to housing-advice budgets and income supports, even as the underlying model kept running [NEW discovery, 2026-07-13, LegalClarity/Y-Säätiö]. Medicine Hat lost its functional-zero status within a year, as rising rents pushed already-housed people back into homelessness faster than the system could re-house them. Neither case is read here as Housing First failing — both are read, by the sources themselves, as proof the model needs continuous housing and income investment to hold, not a one-time fix [backgrounder, "Key tensions / tradeoffs"]. Toronto's own October 2025 count showed a 21% drop — real, but driven almost entirely by fewer refugee claimants in the shelter system, not by improvement in chronic, long-term homelessness, which has not gotten better on the same measure [backgrounder, "Toronto's own adoption record"]. The City's own November 2025 release says plainly that even this year's gains are "at risk without stable funding agreements between all three orders of government."

Three places to start

Unlock TCHC's own empty units first. Toronto's Auditor General already found that better vacancy management at Toronto Community Housing would house about 2,200 more people and recover roughly $7 million a year now being wasted — with no new construction, no new program, and no other government's permission required. This is the cheapest, least contestable first move in the entire file [card a recommendation card].

Fund Housing First as the default, not the exception. Shift shelter-system spending toward rent supplements and supportive housing, prioritizing expiring hotel-lease conversion and modular construction over costlier shelter-bed construction where the two can substitute. Toronto's own capital strategy for new shelters costs 36% more per space than its modular housing program for a worse (partitioned, not private) result — a real, City-sourced number. This depends on the Province not repeating its 2024 suspension of the Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit, which coincided with a 27% drop in housing exits that year [card a recommendation card].

Build the by-name list at Toronto's scale. Medicine Hat and Houston both show that knowing every homeless individual by name, tracked in real time, and reviewing every case weekly across every provider, is itself part of what makes housing placement work — not just having units available. Toronto has the pieces (Built for Zero Canada membership, an existing assessment tool); it has not run this at the scale a city its size requires. Start with the highest-cost, highest-need individuals, not the whole system at once [card a recommendation card].

What's not yet settled

The exact size and cost of the supportive-housing pipeline Toronto needs is not answered anywhere in this file — an open question in the evidence, not something hidden. The headline trial figures (73% vs. 31%) carry their own [confirm against the primary study] flag from the source material. Whether Toronto's count decline holds if refugee-system pressures shift again is unknown. None of the three recommendations above is fully costed or funding-secured; each names what is and isn't confirmed rather than presenting an aspirational path as settled.
