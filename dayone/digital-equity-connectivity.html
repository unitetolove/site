Digital Equity and Connectivity — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto already has an answer to "does the City have a Wi-Fi program" — yes, and it's bigger than it was last year. What it doesn't have, despite an earlier briefing saying otherwise, is a broadband subsidy for people on Ontario Works or ODSP. That's not a small correction. It's the difference between "the City is building something" and "the City is pointing people toward programs it doesn't run."

What ConnectTO actually is, right now

Free public Wi-Fi at more than 250 City-owned locations — community centres, TCHC buildings, civic centres, public squares, even a handful of shelters — plus all 100 library branches. That's up from 100 sites total (all libraries) back in 2021. By the City's own count, 89 percent of those sites sit in neighbourhoods the City itself has flagged as needing the most support. Real usage: about 7,600 connections a day in 2024, with seven in ten of those being people coming back to the same spot more than once — not a one-time convenience, a routine.

What the City said it was exploring, and what it's actually doing

An earlier briefing said Toronto was "exploring" a broadband subsidy for people on OW or ODSP. Checking the City's own most recent program update — July 2025, the most current one available — that's not what's happening. There's no City-funded subsidy in the works. What the City is actually doing is pointing OW and ODSP recipients toward two programs it doesn't run and doesn't pay for: the federal Connecting Families program ($20 a month, 200 GB, since 2022) and Rogers' own Connected for Success program, which is open to ODSP, OW, and GIS recipients. The City's own words for its 2025 activity: "updated the Human Services website with new links" and "developing a strategy" to mention these programs when people contact the City for other reasons. That's outreach, not a subsidy. Worth being plain about that, because a resident reading the earlier framing would reasonably expect a City cheque or a City-negotiated rate that doesn't exist.

How big is the problem, actually

Here's an honest complication: the City's own numbers don't agree with each other. One figure, from the City's own commissioned research, says about 58,000 Torontonians — roughly 2 percent of the city — lack affordable home internet. A different, older framing says something closer to 40 percent of the lowest-income households don't have it. Those aren't the same question (one's a citywide headcount, the other's a rate within one income group), and nothing found in this review reconciles them. Rather than pick the more dramatic number or the more comfortable one, both get stated here, honestly unresolved.

The federal side: real movement, but not aimed at this

The CRTC has been busy. In February 2025 it let smaller internet providers start using the big phone companies' fibre networks to sell service — meaning more competition, in theory, for up to 8.5 million households. In April 2026 it locked in the final prices those smaller providers pay for that access, plus cracked down on switching fees and surprise contract renewals. All real, all dated, all decided. None of it, though, is aimed at the specific population this page cares about — people on social assistance who can't afford a plan at any price. The federal subsidy for that population, Connecting Families, hasn't been expanded or changed in this window as far as this review could find. And the underlying market problem — most homes only really have two choices, the phone company or the cable company — hasn't gone away either: the small, independent providers who used to offer real price competition saw their market share cut in half between 2020 and 2024, from 8.4 percent down to 4.2. Canada's own Competition Bureau flagged this exact two-network problem in its own market study, in almost exactly those words.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Measure whether the referral strategy is actually working (a recommendation card). The City already runs a funded awareness campaign for its Wi-Fi program. This asks for the same thing — planned, funded, measured — applied specifically to getting OW/ODSP recipients enrolled in subsidies they already qualify for. Worth doing because the same kind of referral-only approach, tried in the UK for a structurally similar program, reaches fewer than one in ten eligible households, mostly because seven in ten people never even know the option exists. Toronto has no way of knowing right now whether it's heading toward that same outcome, because nobody's measuring.

2. Publish an actual device-gap number, not just a Wi-Fi-site count (a recommendation card). Toronto tracks Wi-Fi locations and daily connections in detail. It doesn't publish a number for how many residents have access to Wi-Fi but no device to use it — a laptop, a tablet, anything beyond a phone. San Jose does exactly this: a published, tracked figure (25,500 households) alongside its connectivity numbers. Toronto already funds the research relationship that could add this. It just hasn't asked the question yet.

What this doesn't settle

Neither card touches the actual affordability problem, because that's a national telecom-market and federal-regulation question the City can influence only by asking, not by acting. Neither card creates a City subsidy — that would be a bigger, different proposal than either of these, and this page isn't making it. What both cards do is close a smaller, more honest gap: the gap between what the City says it's doing and what it can actually show it's doing, and the gap between having a Wi-Fi program and knowing whether the people who need it most are actually being reached by it.
