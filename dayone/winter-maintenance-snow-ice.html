Winter maintenance (snow and ice) — Day-One Brief

DRAFT File: research/dayone/winter-maintenance-snow-ice.md

File: research/dayone/winter-maintenance-snow-ice.md · Status: DRAFT · v1.0 · 2026-07-14 Issue slug: winter-maintenance-snow-ice · Built from: research/backgrounders/winter-maintenance-snow-ice.md + research/cards/winter-maintenance-snow-ice.md only — no new claims introduced here. Trust class: NEW (inherited from the backgrounder/cards, no CL-#### rows minted for this leaf).

Toronto has already paid for a plan to fix this. It just hasn't fully acted on its own prior plans yet.

What's actually going on

Toronto spends $143.8 million a year clearing snow and ice from 14,700 lane-km of roads, 7,900 km of sidewalks, and 487 lane-km of cycling infrastructure — mostly through 11 zone-based contracts worth nearly $1.5 billion over their full ten-year term (2022 to 2032). The City publishes a real, specific service standard: expressways cleared to bare pavement within 2 hours of 2.5 cm of snow, arterial roads within 6 hours, sidewalks made "safe and passable" within 12 hours, and driveway windrow openings cleared within 2 hours of the road being plowed.

That standard held up better on major roads than on sidewalks and local streets during the storm that actually tested it. In February 2025, a storm big enough to be the first event of its scale since 2022 hit the city, and — in the City's own commissioned review's words — "the February 2025 storms revealed both the dedication of City crews and significant shortcomings in sidewalk clearing, communications, and readiness for more extreme weather. While major roads were kept functional, pedestrian accessibility and local street service fell far below expectations." Residents filed more than 25,000 winter-related 311 requests. Cleanup cost about $20 million and took over 18 days.

The part that isn't a surprise

Here's what makes this not simply a story about one bad storm: the City's own Auditor General had already been flagging this program's contract-oversight gaps for five years before February 2025 hit. A 2020 audit made 22 recommendations. A 2021 audit added 4 more. By 2023, only 10 of those 22 were "fully implemented," and City Council had to adopt four brand-new recommendations — on things as basic as verifying equipment shows up when contracted, applying financial penalties (liquidated damages) when contractors miss targets, and pricing substitute equipment fairly — for the first season for which those very same recommendations were meant to apply.

Then came February 2025. The Auditor General went back in, at Council's own request, specifically to check whether the prior recommendations had actually been implemented before that storm hit. The answer: 9 of 30 cumulative recommendations were still not fully in place. Three more new recommendations followed.

This isn't unique to Toronto's own contracting choices, either. The Ontario government ran into a strikingly similar pattern with its own provincial highway winter contracts roughly a decade earlier: after switching to "performance-based" contracts that paid for outcomes instead of prescribing equipment levels, the province's own Auditor General found contractors "aggressively minimized their winter equipment fleets" to win lowest-bid contracts, and documented one case where choosing the cheaper, less-equipped bidder over a marginally pricier, better-equipped one ended up costing an extra $1.7 million a year once the winning contractor failed to meet service targets. Same contract philosophy, same kind of gap between price and delivered service, one level of government up.

What the City itself says would fix it — and what it would cost

The City's 2025 consultant review didn't just diagnose the problem — it costed five different ways to fix it, from a bare-bones $24.0 million-a-season option to a maximum-readiness $130.3 million-a-season option requiring close to 1,000 pieces of standby equipment. The recommended option — $24.0 million a season — is, per the review's own words, something that "can primarily be accommodated within the City's current winter maintenance budget." In other words: the City's own consultants say the fix is affordable inside money the City is already spending.

The review also names the specific mechanical reason sidewalks and driveways get stuck behind schedule: parked cars. "Even when parked in accordance with regulations, on-street parking during or after a snowfall prevented plows and removal equipment from operating effectively" — vehicles get buried, windrows harden around them, and clearing crews have to work around obstacles instead of through them. During the February 2025 event, police issued 3,633 parking tickets but towed only 74 vehicles, because — per City staff's own account — "ticketing was prioritized over towing, due to operational constraints."

What the evidence doesn't yet say

Update: this session's own research found what the previous draft flagged as missing. Two companies — A & F Di Carlo Construction Inc. and Infrastructure Maintenance Inc. — and their joint venture, launched days before the December 2021 award, ended up with 88% of the $1.471-billion total contract value, per the City's own Auditor General and contemporaneous CBC News reporting on the award. The remaining two zones went to Maple Crete Inc. (Willowdale) and Emcon Services Inc. (the Gardiner and Don Valley Parkway). A rival bidder went on the record at the time to question how one group of companies came to dominate so completely. What's still not independently confirmed in full: a subsequent forensic audit of that same procurement, which — per news reporting this pass could not fetch directly in full — reportedly found no outright wrongdoing but did flag risk areas, including a reported financial link between one of the winning companies and its own subcontractors. That last piece is flagged, not asserted, until the actual audit report itself gets a direct read.

Also unconfirmed: the exact wording of the Auditor General's three newest 2025 recommendations (only a summary of them exists in what was checked this pass), and roughly the back half of the City's own 142-page 2025 consultant review — including the section specifically about improving sidewalk-clearing equipment and route planning, which is almost certainly where the densest sidewalk-specific findings live.

Two things worth doing, since the City's own numbers already make the case

Fund the $24 million plan the City already priced, and report on it the way the Auditor General already knows how to report. The consultant review's own recommended path costs $24.0 million a season and fits mostly inside the existing budget. Pairing that funding decision with a standing public report — using the same recommendation-by-recommendation status format the Auditor General's own follow-ups already use — would make it possible to see, every season, whether this round of fixes actually stuck, instead of finding out five years and two storms later that they didn't.

Put windrow and sidewalk clearing times on a public map, not just an internal one. The City is already building a GPS dashboard to track contractor performance — the Auditor General's 2025 report says it's in "final phases of user acceptance testing." Right now that's an internal tool. Making the same data public, broken down by area, would let residents (and Council) see for themselves whether the 2-hour windrow standard and 12-hour sidewalk standard are actually being met near them, instead of relying on a 311 complaint spike to find out only after a storm that they weren't.

Where this comes from, and where it doesn't yet reach

Every figure above traces to City of Toronto and Auditor General documents fetched live on 2026-07-14: the City's own "Salting & Plowing Roads" service-standard page, the 2025 winter storm consultant review, the Auditor General's 2025 Annual Report, the Auditor General's June 2023 follow-up report and procurement-award audit, City Council's own 2025 agenda records on the forensic audit, contemporaneous CBC News reporting on the December 2021 contract award, and — for the provincial comparator — the Auditor General of Ontario's 2016 Annual Report on Ministry of Transportation highway winter maintenance. No comparator city's own program (Montreal, Ottawa, and others are named as candidates in the backgrounder but not yet independently checked), and no advocacy position is drawn on here; the forensic-audit interconnection finding is flagged as coming from a secondary summary, not a directly fetched primary article.
