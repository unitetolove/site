Gender Equity and Women's Safety — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/gender-equity-womens-safety.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/gender-equity-womens-safety.md, research/cards/gender-equity-womens-safety.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto declared this a crisis in 2023. This brief checks what's happened since, using the most current tracking data and international review findings available, rather than repeating the declaration as if it were the whole story.

What's actually happening

City Council declared gender-based and intimate-partner violence an epidemic in 2023, joining more than 95 other Ontario municipalities. The province still hasn't. A bill that would have made the province say the same thing — Bill 173 — passed a first procedural hurdle in 2024, then got sent to committee, and in November 2024 the government refused to pass it, saying it needed more study first. Nothing found in this research shows that's changed since.

Meanwhile, the group that actually tracks femicides in Ontario month by month — the Ontario Association of Interval and Transition Houses — counted 16 confirmed femicides in the province over the twelve months ending in May 2026. That's about flat compared to the year before. Their own report doesn't sugarcoat it: they say inquest after inquest keeps recommending the same fixes, and it's still unclear whether those recommendations are actually being implemented.

The strategy nobody could confirm the status of

Here's an honest gap in this research, not a resolved fact. Toronto has had a Gender Equity Unit since 2019, and reporting from a few years back said the City was putting together its first Gender Equity Strategy, with an action plan meant to run 2023 through 2026. That window is closing this year. This research could not find a public report saying the strategy was formally adopted, or what it's actually delivered so far. Two things could be true: the City has a real record of this that just wasn't findable in this pass, or a multi-year equity strategy is running out its own stated clock without a public accounting. This brief doesn't guess which — it says plainly that the gap exists, and that it's worth someone checking directly with the City rather than assuming either answer.

What the UN said about Canada, recently

In October 2024, the United Nations committee that reviews countries on women's rights looked at Canada's record. Their findings were published that December. One of their specific concerns, in their own words: refugee, asylum-seeking, and undocumented migrant women and girls face compounded discrimination and a higher risk of gender-based violence, including from trafficking. Canada has to report back to that committee within two years — so sometime around October 2026, there should be an update on what's changed. That's a real, dated, checkable moment worth watching for.

That specific finding matters here because it names a population this issue doesn't always focus on by default. This brief doesn't treat "women's safety" as one undifferentiated group — the UN's own current review says some women face substantially more risk than others, and that should shape where funding and outreach actually go.

What Toronto already built, and could fund again

In 1989, a Toronto organization called METRAC invented something called the Women's Safety Audit — a way for residents to walk through a space and flag what makes it unsafe: bad lighting, no sightlines, nowhere to signal for help. Toronto used it on the TTC in 1994. Other cities around the world picked it up after that. It's a genuinely Toronto-made tool, sitting mostly unused at scale right now. Reviving it, with the UN's own newest finding about migrant and refugee women built into who gets prioritized, is a concrete, low-cost, already-proven idea — not a new invention.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't prove the City is failing on gender equity, and it doesn't prove it's succeeding. The declared emergency is real. The tracking data on femicide is real, current, and comes from the people who actually count it every month, not from this project. What's genuinely missing is a clear public account of what Toronto's own flagship equity strategy has delivered — and until that exists, nobody outside City Hall can actually judge whether the declaration from 2023 turned into follow-through or stayed a declaration.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Make the City show its own work (RC-GEWS-01) — require a public status report on what the Gender Equity Strategy has actually delivered against its own 2023-2026 plan, before deciding what comes next. This costs almost nothing and fixes a transparency gap this research ran into directly. 2. Fund Toronto's own invention again, aimed at who needs it most right now (RC-GEWS-02) — revive the Women's Safety Audit at citywide scale, in partnership with METRAC, with specific outreach to refugee and newcomer women, following the UN's own most current finding about who faces the highest risk.

Neither move claims to fix gender-based violence on its own — the deepest levers here are provincial and federal, and this brief says so plainly. Both are things the City can do on its own authority, right now, with tools it has already either built or funded once before.
