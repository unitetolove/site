One Percent: What Toronto's Own Complaint Numbers Actually Show — Day-One Paper

DRAFT

Status: DRAFT · v1.0 · 2026-07-14 · Draws only from: our research file for that page and that page's recommendation cards Layer: L-PLATFORM (opinionated synthesis; every factual premise traces to the backgrounder/cards above)

Only 1% of public complaints against Toronto police officers over a five-year period led to a disciplinary hearing. That number came from an individual journalist combing through raw data — not from any government body publishing it as a standing, tracked metric. Ontario's newest police oversight body has just launched its biggest public action yet, an 18-month anti-corruption review, in response to a case where more than 170 charges have been laid and none tested in court. Both facts point the same direction: the tools that exist to answer "is this working" aren't yet built to answer it on their own.

Where things stand

Ontario rebuilt its police oversight architecture in April 2024, renaming the old public-complaints body (OIPRD) into the Law Enforcement Complaints Agency and creating a brand-new Inspectorate of Policing to handle policy and service complaints separately from individual officer conduct. Two years earlier, the Ontario Human Rights Commission had already finished a multi-year inquiry — three separate reports over five years — concluding that the Toronto Police Service and its civilian board are responsible for systemic anti-Black racial discrimination and profiling, with over 100 specific recommendations attached. And this year, a corruption investigation that started with a thwarted plot against a corrections officer grew into something much larger: more than 400 investigators across four agencies, more than 170 charges, seven active Toronto officers among those charged. None of it has been tested in court. All of it was serious enough that Ontario's Inspector General of Policing launched a province-wide, 18-month review of how police services screen, supervise, and monitor their own officers.

Here's what connects all three threads: none of the bodies responsible for catching these problems can currently show, on a standing basis, whether they're catching them. The 1%-of-complaints figure that got public attention wasn't published by the complaints agency itself — a journalist derived it from raw data. The Human Rights Commission's 100-plus recommendations exist; whether they've actually been implemented isn't tracked anywhere this research found. And the new Inspectorate, for all its just-launched anti-corruption review, closed its first nine months of operation with 505 complaints received and zero formal enforcement actions taken — not necessarily a bad sign for a body that new, but not evidence of anything either, in the absence of a published track record to compare it against.

Two things worth doing, at minimal cost

First: make the discipline-conversion rate a standing, published number, not a one-time investigative find. Require the complaints agency and the Toronto Police Services Board to jointly publish, every year, what share of complaints reach a hearing, what share are substantiated, and why the rest don't proceed. This costs almost nothing — the data already exists inside the complaints process; it just isn't packaged for public accountability. It doesn't settle whether 1% is the right number or a broken system — reasonable people can read that fact differently, and there's a real due-process argument (the same "clear and convincing evidence" standard used in criminal trials) that deserves to be taken seriously, not waved away. But nobody should have to wait for the next journalist with the time to do this analysis by hand.

Second: get a commitment, now, that the anti-corruption inspection's findings get published in full. The Inspectorate has already committed to producing a Findings Report from an externally appointed inspector. What it hasn't committed to, as far as this research could confirm, is publishing that report itself rather than just a summary of whatever compliance directions come out of it. Given that this same Inspectorate closed its first operating year with zero enforcement actions and no established public track record either way, the substance of this review — not just its bottom line — is the only real window the public will get into whether the response to a serious integrity concern was rigorous. This costs nothing beyond a publication-format decision on work already underway and already funded.

What this doesn't settle

This paper takes no position on what caused the 1% discipline rate — a broken system, a demanding evidentiary standard, or some mix neither this research nor the underlying reporting fully disentangles. It takes no position on any individual charge connected to the ongoing corruption investigation; nobody is named here, and nothing alleged is treated as proven, because none of it has been tested in court yet. And it doesn't resolve whether Ontario's new Inspectorate of Policing is an effective oversight body or a fledgling one still finding its footing — that's a genuinely open question this paper flags rather than answers, because the record needed to answer it doesn't exist yet either. What's proposed here is the minimum precondition for anyone — journalist, councillor, resident — to answer these questions without having to reconstruct the data from scratch each time.
