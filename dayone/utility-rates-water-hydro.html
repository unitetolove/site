Municipal Water and Hydro Rates — Day-One Paper

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from that page's recommendation cards (RC-utility-01–02) and our research file for that page; every claim below traces to one of those two documents.

What a mayor does in week one on this file

Toronto residents pay two structurally different utility bills, governed by two different accountable bodies, and week one is about making that structure legible rather than pretending the City controls more than it does.

1. Be honest that the City sets water rates directly, and hydro rates are not the City's call. Toronto Water's rate rose 3.75% for 2025 and again (interim) for 2026 — City Council's own decision, adding roughly $40/year to the average household's bill. Toronto Hydro's distribution rate, despite Toronto Hydro being wholly City-owned, is set by the Ontario Energy Board under an approved 2025–2029 plan, not by Council (RC-utility-02, backgrounder). Week one: stop letting "hydro rates" and "water rates" be discussed as if the City has equal control over both — it doesn't, and saying so plainly is more useful to residents than vague reassurance.

2. Publish the dividend-versus-ratepayer-impact disclosure. Toronto Hydro paid the City $60 million in dividends in 2025 while its ratepayers faced OEB-approved distribution-rate increases justified by capital investment need. This is not evidence of wrongdoing — it's a structural fact the City can make visible at negligible cost, using data it already has, alongside the water-rate disclosure it already publishes annually (RC-utility-02). Week one: direct staff to add this table to the next Budget cycle's public materials.

3. Find out whether the existing relief programs are actually reaching people. Both the Water Rebate Program and the Ontario Electricity Support Program exist and are real, documented mechanisms — but no source in this file's evidence base establishes what share of eligible low-income households actually receive either one (RC-utility-01, backgrounder). Before proposing any new affordability mechanism, week one starts by asking Revenue Services and the OEB's own OESP administration for actual enrollment-versus-eligible-population data. A program that exists on paper but isn't reaching people is a different problem than a program that doesn't exist.

4. Study, don't yet commit to, a different affordability-program design. Philadelphia's Tiered Assistance Program caps water bills at a fixed percentage of income rather than Toronto's current flat-rebate-plus-consumption-cap model — a genuinely different mechanism worth examining, but the Philadelphia figures cited here are themselves unconfirmed beyond a search summary (RC-utility-01). Week one: commission the fact-check and a Toronto-scale cost estimate before treating this as more than a plausible model worth investigating.

What this office does not control, and won't pretend to

Toronto Hydro's distribution rates are an OEB function; the City cannot unilaterally change them regardless of how loudly residents (rightly) feel their hydro bill. The commodity/generation and Global Adjustment portion of an Ontario electricity bill — often the more politically salient part historically — sits with IESO and provincial policy, further outside municipal reach (backgrounder). Naming this clearly is not an excuse; it's the honest boundary of what a week-one municipal action can and cannot touch.

The honest bottom line

Both utility cost streams are rising at a similar pace (roughly 3.75%/year on the water side; a comparable multi-year glide path on the hydro side), both have relief programs whose real-world reach is unverified, and one of the two (hydro) is a rate the City does not set even though it owns the utility and profits from its dividends. Week one is about disclosure, honest jurisdictional framing, and finding out whether the existing safety net actually works — not announcing a rate cut the City cannot deliver on the hydro side, or a new program before checking whether the current one is reaching anyone.

Uncertainties this paper inherits from its cards

The Toronto Hydro dividend figures ($60M/year, 2025) and the Toronto Water capital-plan figures ($9.284B/10-year allocation) were both drawn from search-result summaries rather than this page’s own full-text read of the primary documents, and should be independently re-confirmed before any public use (RC-utility-01, RC-utility-02, backgrounder). Program uptake data for both relief programs does not exist anywhere in this file's evidence base. The Philadelphia TAP comparator's own figures are flagged ⚠️ still being checked in the backgrounder. None of these gaps are resolved here — they are named so this paper isn't read as more certain than its own sources are.
