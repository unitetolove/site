Anti-Black Racism and Investment in Black Communities — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/anti-black-racism-investment.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/anti-black-racism-investment.md, research/cards/anti-black-racism-investment.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto's own numbers on this one cut both ways, and this brief keeps both. The City has a real, documented anti-Black-racism plan, with a real, documented delivery rate. The City's own most recent report also says, in its own words, that the problems the plan exists to fix haven't gone away. Both things are true at once, and neither is smoothed over here.

What's actually happening

In 2017, Toronto adopted Canada's first municipal strategy dedicated to confronting anti-Black racism. The City's own April 2026 report on the plan's final stretch (2018-2024) confirms it delivered 96 percent of its planned actions — training thousands of staff, funding Black-led organizations, building partnerships that didn't exist before. That's a real, checkable number, not a talking point.

Here's the part that doesn't get said as often: the same 2026 report states plainly that Black Torontonians "continue to experience disproportionate and persistent outcomes" in housing stability, food security, employment, and how the city responds to them in a crisis. Black residents remain significantly overrepresented among people experiencing homelessness. That's not this document's own reading of the numbers — it's the City's own language, in the City's own report on the plan's own results.

Delivered isn't the same as closed

Ninety-six percent of actions delivered and disparities that "remain... disproportionate and persistent" are both, simultaneously, in the same report. The City's own explanation for the gap isn't defensive — it says the outcomes "reflect structural and systemic conditions that cannot be addressed through time-limited initiatives or business as usual service delivery alone." In plainer language: doing the planned things was not the same project as fixing the underlying problem, and the City is saying so itself rather than waiting to be told.

A renewed 10-year plan is supposed to close that gap — moving, in the City's own words, "toward deeper, measurable systems change." That plan doesn't exist yet. It isn't scheduled to reach City Council until the second quarter of 2027, close to a year from now. Anyone who thinks outcome-level accountability on this file has already arrived is ahead of where the City itself actually is.

The money, and where the numbers disagree

Two City-sourced figures for how much the Action Plan has spent don't match each other, and this brief isn't going to pretend one of them is right. An earlier document says $25.8 million and change invested, over 9,000 staff trained. A directly-checked City news release from 2023 says $33.3 million cumulative, 27,303 staff trained since 2018. Both numbers are real, sourced documents; they just don't agree, and nothing found in this research explains why. That's stated here as an open question, not quietly resolved in either direction.

Federally, the picture is clearer: the Black Entrepreneurship Program has backed more than 24,000 Black entrepreneurs nationally and approved over $70 million across more than 800 loans, with a $189 million renewal confirmed as of February 2026. The federal minister's own statement names an intersectional barrier directly — Black women and 2SLGBTQI+ entrepreneurs face compounded difficulty accessing capital, in her own words, not an outside inference.

Toronto's own targeted piece — the Black-Mandated Funding Framework, which funds organizations actually governed by Black communities rather than organizations that just serve Black clients — is small and real: 18 organizations, $1.6 million, in its pilot phase. That's the specific design worth other cities copying, not the scale of it yet.

Little Jamaica: still not finished

The Eglinton Crosstown construction hollowed out a historic Black business corridor. The City has been working on a formal Cultural District Plan to protect what's left since roughly 2020-2021. As of the study page's own most recent update, that plan is still listed as "to be developed" — not adopted, not final, years into the process. Real federal money has moved in the meantime: $19 million from the federal government for small retail-based businesses generally, plus $1 million specifically for Little Jamaica's revitalization, delivered in partnership with the Black Business and Professional Association. But the protective planning mechanism itself — the thing meant to stop the next round of displacement before it happens — isn't built yet.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't prove the Action Plan failed — a 96 percent delivery rate on a five-issue-area, 80-action strategy, developed with 800-plus Black residents, is a genuine institutional achievement, and the City's own honest self-assessment doesn't walk that back. It also doesn't prove the plan succeeded at its deeper goal, since the City's own report is explicit that it hasn't closed the disparities yet. Nothing found in this research points to a specific company or contractor profiting from anti-Black racism or underinvestment persisting in Toronto — this looks like an underfunding and follow-through problem, not an extraction problem, and this brief says that plainly rather than inventing a villain to make the story sharper.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Make the next plan measure what matters, not just what got done (RC-ABR-01) — require the renewed 10-year plan, when it reaches Council in 2027, to track the specific outcomes the City itself has already named as still unresolved: homelessness overrepresentation, food insecurity, youth unemployment. Not a new program — a design requirement for reporting that already gets written and published every year. 2. Scale the piece that's already working, on its own proven terms (RC-ABR-02) — grow the Black-Mandated Funding Framework past its 18-organization pilot, using the same eligibility design (community-governed, not just community-serving), sized through the renewed plan's own budget process rather than picked out of the air here.

Neither move claims to have found a shortcut. Both start from what the City has already published, including the parts of what it published that don't flatter the City.
