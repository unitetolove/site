Worker Co-operatives and Community-Wealth Building — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from: our research file for that page and that page's recommendation cards. No claim in this document goes beyond what those two files already establish. Provenance: earlier research plus this review, as carried from the backgrounder/cards; see those documents for full citations.

The model is proven and under-used; the honest puzzle is financing and founding, not performance — and this page’s own strongest piece of evidence turns out to be a procurement-and-wages result more than a worker-ownership result, a genuine finding worth stating plainly.

The case

Worker co-operatives — businesses owned and democratically run by the people who work in them — survive as well or better than conventional firms and deliver markedly better job stability and pay equity. Mondragón, the world's largest worker-co-op federation, kept 100 of the 103 co-ops it founded between 1956 and 1986 running for three decades. In Italy's Emilia-Romagna region, co-operatives generate roughly 30% of the regional economy in one of Europe's richest, most equal places. If the model works this well, the real question is why it's rare — and the honest answer, backed by the evidence, is a financing gap: banks won't lend to a business with no outside equity owner, and raising growth capital usually means selling the control that makes a co-op a co-op in the first place [backgrounder, "Worker co-ops survive as well or better," "Emilia-Romagna"].

The honest catch

This review went and fetched the actual academic study behind the most-cited proof that this whole strategy works — the Preston Model in England — rather than relying on the city's own self-evaluation. The 2025 peer-reviewed study is real and rigorous: a controlled, difference-in-differences design on individual-level national survey data found employment rates in Preston rose 4% relative to what would have been expected, with dramatically larger gains for disabled residents (up to 22 percentage points) and minority ethnic groups. That's a genuinely strong result [backgrounder, "Preston," directly fetched 2026-07-14].

But the same study says something the Preston Model's advocates don't lead with: Preston's own worker-co-op development has been "modest." The employment gains the study measures come mainly from redirecting anchor-institution (hospital, university, council) spending toward local suppliers and pushing those institutions to pay a real living wage — not from building a worker-co-op sector specifically. That's an important correction. The strongest evidence in this whole research area isn't really proof that worker co-ops move the needle on their own; it's proof that procurement and wages move the needle, with co-op development as a smaller, slower-building piece alongside it.

Mondragón's record has its own honest complication too: its largest company, Fagor, collapsed outright in 2013, and Mondragón runs factories abroad staffed by regular employees, not co-op members — the same "grow big or stay small" tension that makes worker co-ops hard to scale everywhere. And Emilia-Romagna's headline 30%-of-GDP figure covers every kind of co-op — farming, retail, credit unions, housing — not just worker co-ops, and it took over a century of specific Italian law and politics to build. Neither is a template Toronto can fast-track [backgrounder, "Mondragón," "Emilia-Romagna"].

Two places to start

Redirect existing procurement before building anything new. Toronto already has a Social Procurement Policy and real anchor institutions — hospitals, universities, the City itself. Adding a modest scoring weight for community-owned and worker-owned suppliers in competitive bids costs close to nothing in new budget; it's the same lever Preston used to grow local spend from £38 million to £112 million in four years, and it's the lever the actual evidence supports most strongly [card a recommendation card].

Build small, not big — Preston's own worker-co-op piece was modest, and that's the honest scale to start at. A small development organization to help new co-ops and retiring-owner conversions get off the ground, backed by a modest loan-guarantee arrangement with a credit union, sized to what the best-evidenced comparator actually achieved rather than to Mondragón- or Emilia-Romagna-scale ambition this page’s own evidence says isn't realistically transferable on a short timeline [card a recommendation card].

What's not yet settled

How many worker co-ops actually operate in Toronto right now is genuinely unclear — this review found one clearly confirmed example (Hypha, a tech worker co-op) and no citywide count. Cleveland's Evergreen Cooperatives, the other flagship model, is still running but doesn't have anything like the rigorous outside evaluation this review found for Preston — a real gap in what we can honestly compare. And Ontario's co-op law saw a partial update recently, but it's narrower than the full modernization advocates are asking for, and this review couldn't confirm the exact legislative text or status.
