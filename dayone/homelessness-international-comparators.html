What the Rest of the World Actually Shows About Ending Homelessness — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Every homelessness debate eventually reaches for a comparator: "Finland solved it." "Houston solved it." "Look at Rockford." This project checked all of them, directly, against their own primary sources — and the honest picture is more useful than the slogan version, because it shows what actually sustains progress and what quietly undoes it.

The pattern: every success story has a documented complication

Houston is real — a HUD-mandated coordinated-entry overhaul that genuinely moved the needle. It's also true that Houston's unsheltered count rose 15.8% in 2025, and in July 2025 the city expanded a 24-hour sidewalk-obstruction ban downtown; citations went from about 3 a month to over 100 a month, and the mayor said publicly that "those homeless are not being cited," a claim the city's own citation data directly contradicts.

Finland is real — eleven straight years of falling homelessness, a national Housing First program credited across the world as the model. It's also true that Finland's own national housing agency recorded the trend reversing in 2024, and Toronto's companion backgrounder on the Housing First model tracks the 2025 numbers getting worse, not better.

Denver's own program says it moved nearly 8,000 people into housing. Denver's own City Auditor, independently reconstructing the numbers, found actual spending ran $20 million higher than the city had publicly reported, and concluded flatly that the city "does not own" the data behind its own headline statistics — meaning it can't verify the very numbers it's been publicizing.

And Rockford, Illinois — a city of 350,000 that has sustained functional zero homelessness for veterans since 2015 and for the chronically homeless since 2017, one of only three U.S. communities to do either — was, as of November 2025, facing a federal funding rule change that its own program coordinator said could shrink its permanent-housing capacity from 308 people to 105. Checking back on this for the current pass: a federal court struck down that specific rule change on June 29, 2026 for violating federal procedural law, but the court refused to permanently block the government from trying something similar again — and it already has, with a new version issued June 1, 2026 that keeps much of the same direction. So the story isn't "resolved" so much as "still going," six months later than the original snapshot. What actually happened to Rockford's own funding specifically wasn't confirmed either way in this review. It's a live example, happening right now, of how a real, sustained, nearly decade-long municipal success can be undone by a funding-design decision made somewhere else entirely — and of how "the threat was blocked" and "the threat is over" are not the same thing.

The one program that actually proved it, rigorously

Most of what gets called "evidence" in this space is a before-and-after number from the program's own dashboard. The UK's Rough Sleeping Initiative is the exception: a government-commissioned, peer-review-adjacent evaluation using a real causal-inference method (Difference-in-Differences) found the program produced a 32% reduction in rough sleeping specifically attributable to the initiative itself — not just correlated with it. Even that program's own government later published a self-critical evaluation finding less than 1% of its funding went to prevention. That's what an honest, working accountability system looks like: real results, and a government willing to say where it's still falling short.

What Toronto's own comparison to other Canadian cities actually shows

Vancouver, Calgary, Ottawa, and Montreal all saw their homelessness counts rise in their most recent count, same as Toronto. Calgary's raw number went up while its per-person rate actually fell, because the city grew faster than its homeless population did — a reminder that a single number, without context, can tell the wrong story either way. And a genuinely useful, less-discussed finding: Toronto's own open data — a public dataset showing shelter occupancy day by day — is better than what several comparator cities publish. Montreal's own city data portal has no equivalent dataset at all. But even Toronto's own "current" data turned out to be six to seven months old when checked directly in July 2026 — the same problem showed up in Calgary's dataset too. "Open data" and "live data" are not the same thing, and this project checked the difference directly rather than assuming it away.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't resolve whether Rockford keeps its federal funding — the broader federal fight it sits inside is confirmed still active as of July 2026, but Rockford's own specific outcome wasn't found in this review. It doesn't resolve whether the UK's rough-sleeping numbers kept falling after 2021; a widely repeated claim of a later reversal couldn't be confirmed against a primary government source, so it isn't repeated here as fact. And it doesn't try to build a per-dollar or per-capita comparison between any of these cities and Toronto — the programs, populations, and measurement methods differ too much for that kind of number to mean what it would look like it means.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Give Toronto's own metrics the same rigor the UK gave its flagship program (a recommendation card) — right now, Toronto tracks trend lines, not attributable causal estimates; the UK's own evaluation methodology is a specific, adoptable template, not just an inspiring result. 2. Keep watching what happens to Rockford (a recommendation card) — the most-cited North American functional-zero success story is currently a live test of whether a sustained municipal win can survive a senior government changing the funding rules underneath it. That's directly relevant to Toronto's own dependence on provincial and federal transfers, tracked in this project's companion funding-architecture research.

None of this requires taking Finland's, Houston's, or Rockford's word for it. It requires reading what each of them, and the governments auditing them, actually published — including the parts that don't fit the slogan.
