"Functional Zero" Was Real — and the Organization That Coined It Just Retired the Term — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Medicine Hat and London get cited constantly in Canadian homelessness debate — proof, depending on who's citing them, either that ending homelessness is achievable or that "functional zero" announcements don't mean much. This project checked both cities' full record, and checked what happened to the standard itself. Both things turned out to be true, and neither cancels the other out.

What actually happened in Medicine Hat and London

Medicine Hat became the first Canadian city to hit "functional zero" chronic homelessness in June 2021. By September 2022, that had reversed — 19 people chronically homeless, roughly 150 cycling in and out of the count. This project already had that much. What we didn't have, until checking this week, is what happened after: Medicine Hat's most recent count, from October 2025, found 136 people homeless on the count night. That's not a clean continuation of the 2022 reversal — the count actually dropped to 104 in 2024 before rising again to 136 in 2025. It went down, then up. Anyone citing a single year from this record, in either direction, is telling you part of the story.

And it's worth being precise about what "136" means: only 33 of those people were counted outdoors. The rest were in emergency shelters, transitional housing, or in custody at the local remand centre. A headline that says "136 living rough" — which is literally what ran locally — overstates street homelessness by a factor of four. We're not repeating that framing.

London's story has its own honest wrinkle already on the record: the city hit functional zero for veteran homelessness in February 2021, then exceeded the threshold for six months in 2022 — not because more veterans actually became homeless, but because the system got better at finding and counting veterans who already were. That's a real, useful distinction, and London fixed it and got back under threshold by October 2022. What happened after that, we don't know — nothing in this review found a more current update, and we're saying so rather than assuming either way.

The bigger finding: the standard itself just changed

Here's what wasn't on this project's radar until this week's check: Built for Zero — the US-based organization that invented "functional zero" as a measurable target — retired it. Not quietly walked it back. Retired it, on their own website, with a plain explanation of why: in big cities, a single numeric threshold was hard to hit and could make a system obsess over the last few hardest cases instead of overall performance. And in places where a city hit functional zero for one group, residents could still see encampments elsewhere in the city — which made the achievement feel fake even when it wasn't.

Built for Zero's own words are worth quoting directly: "The achievements were real." They're not disowning what Medicine Hat, London, or anyone else did. They're saying the yardstick wasn't built for the job at the scale some cities needed. In its place: a broader framework asking whether homelessness is becoming rare, brief, non-recurring, safe, and believable — and a new verification process, adopted in late 2025, that requires a community to say explicitly whether a reported improvement is a real change on the ground or just better data collection catching people who were already there. That second part is, almost word for word, the same lesson London's own 2022 episode already taught — it's just now built into the methodology everywhere, not just learned the hard way once.

For the record: out of every community that ever chased this target, only three ever hit it for both veterans and the chronically homeless — Rockford, Illinois; Bergen County, New Jersey; and Abilene, Texas. Rockford's own current situation — a live federal funding fight that could cut its permanent-housing capacity by two-thirds — is covered in this project's companion research on international comparators.

What this means going forward

If you see "functional zero" used as a current target in a Toronto policy conversation, ask what standard it's actually measuring against — the organization that coined the phrase doesn't use it that way anymore. This project's own future writing will do the same: cite "functional zero" as a real, historical milestone when describing what Medicine Hat and London achieved, and use the current language — rare, brief, non-recurring, safe, believable, verified — for anything forward-looking.

None of this required taking anyone's word for it. It required checking whether the standard both cities were measured against was still the standard anyone uses.
