Gig Economy & Precarious-Work Labour Standards — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-13 · File: research/dayone/gig-economy-precarious-work.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.1 · Date: 2026-07-13 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/gig-economy-precarious-work.md, research/cards/gig-economy-precarious-work.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

A driver logged into the app, waiting for the next ride between fares, isn't being paid for that time — not because of a loophole, but because Ontario's own law defines "work" as the assignment itself, not the waiting. Nationally, roughly 667,000 Canadians did that kind of paid platform work in the twelve months to December 2025; delivery and rideshare are the two biggest slices of it. The province wrote the law, the province set the penalties, and Statistics Canada already counted the workers. This brief reads what's already public, states plainly what the City can and cannot touch, and doesn't dress up a licensing tweak as a labour-standards fix.

What's actually in force

Ontario's Digital Platform Workers' Rights Act came into force July 1, 2025, so 2026 is its first full year on the books. It requires minimum wage — but only for each work assignment, not for the time a driver or courier spends logged in and waiting between jobs. It requires a written reason and two weeks' notice before a platform can cut someone off for 24 hours or more. It sets real penalties: up to $1,000 per offence for an individual and up to $50,000 for a corporation from a compliance officer alone — and beyond that, quasi-criminal offence penalties running as high as $500,000 for a company that keeps breaking it, and, for an individual, a fine of up to $50,000 and up to 12 months in jail. This law carries real criminal-adjacent exposure for a person, not just a corporate fine schedule. None of this depends on whether the worker counts as an employee under the regular Employment Standards Act — the platforms are bound either way.

What isn't there: no EI, no CPP, no WSIB. And nobody has published a single number yet on how the law's first year has actually gone — how many complaints were filed, how many penalties were levied, whether the gap between "paid for the ride" and "paid for the whole shift" is showing up in real paycheques. That's not a criticism dressed as a fact; it's simply what wasn't found.

The scale of it

Nationally, roughly 667,000 Canadians did some kind of paid platform work in the twelve months to December 2025 — barely changed from roughly 671,000 the year before. Delivery work and personal transport are the two biggest categories, at 272,000 and 184,000 people respectively. Ontario's own count is older: 80,000-plus active Uber and Lyft drivers as of December 2024, the most recent figure anyone could locate. Those two numbers use different rulers — one is national and covers all platform work, the other is Ontario rideshare specifically — so they don't add up to a single clean total, and this brief isn't going to pretend they do.

What the City actually controls, and what it doesn't

This is the part worth saying plainly: Toronto does not set minimum wage, does not define what counts as paid time, and cannot add EI or CPP coverage for a single driver. All of that is Queen's Park and Ottawa. What the City does control is who gets to hold a Private Transportation Company licence to drive Uber or Lyft in this city — the training they take, the criminal-record and driving-record checks they pass, the vehicle standards they meet, and the fee they pay every year ($18.32 plus an $8.65 accessibility fee, collected through the company, not the driver directly). That licensing relationship already exists. It's real, it's active, and it's the one lever this brief is going to use — not pretend into something bigger than it is.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. A compliance disclosure, added to the licence Toronto already issues (RC-GIG-01). Every company holding a PTC permit already reports driver-level data to the City every year. This adds one more thing to that same report: what share of their Toronto drivers are actually being paid at or above minimum wage for the time they're logged in, not just the time they're on a job, and how many complaints have been filed against them here. The City isn't policing the law — the province still does that. It's just making sure Toronto isn't flying blind on a law that governs tens of thousands of people working in this city, when nobody nationally has published the numbers yet either. ⚠️ Untested: whether a municipal licence can require wage-compliance disclosure specifically — rather than the safety/training/vehicle data the City's licence already covers — without reaching into labour-standards territory that belongs to the province has not been legally tested or confirmed; that's a real open question this idea would need to clear, not a settled point.

2. Rest stops and washrooms where couriers and drivers actually wait (RC-GIG-02). Unpaid waiting time is baked into the law as written — that's not a loophole, it's the design. A courier or driver logged in and waiting for the next order doesn't get paid for it and doesn't have anywhere to go. This is a public-realm fix, not a labour-standards fix: a handful of washroom and rest points at the delivery and pickup hubs where these workers actually spend that unpaid time, using park and public-space budgets the City already runs.

What this doesn't settle

Neither card touches the actual gap — unpaid waiting time not counting toward minimum wage — because that's written into provincial law and only the province can rewrite it. Neither card adds EI, CPP, or WSIB, because those are federal and provincial systems the City has no authority over. And this brief can't tell you whether the DPWRA's first year is working, because as of today nobody has published that data — not the province, not this pass's search of the public record. What Toronto can actually do here is small next to what only Queen's Park and Ottawa can do. That's a fact about jurisdiction, not a failure of ambition, and it's worth saying that way rather than pretending a licensing form solves a wage law.
