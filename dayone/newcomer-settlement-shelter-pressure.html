Refugee Shelter Costs: A Federal Bill the City Is Increasingly Paying Alone — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/newcomer-settlement-shelter-pressure.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/newcomer-settlement-shelter-pressure.md, research/cards/newcomer-settlement-shelter-pressure.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Immigration and refugee policy is federal jurisdiction. Sheltering the people that policy produces has, in practice, become Toronto's bill — and the federal government's own numbers show it's paying less of that bill each year, on purpose.

The scale of what happened

Refugee claimants in Toronto's shelter system grew more than elevenfold in three years — from 530 people a night in September 2021 to around 6,490 at the August 2024 peak. That's already come down, to 4,597 by March 2025, but the population that remains has changed shape: in 2022, a third of refugee claimants had been in shelter more than six months. By 2024, that was 83%. This isn't a caseload that's passing through quickly anymore.

The City didn't sit still. It stood up a dedicated Refugee Response Unit in 2023, opened eight refugee-specific houses and small shelters, reimbursed over $5 million to Black-led churches and community organizations who stepped in when the system didn't have room, transferred 4,675 people into federal IRCC-run hotels to free up municipal beds, and diverted an estimated 1,600 more away from the shelter system entirely through direct-housing programs. That's not a city that failed to respond. That's a city that built an entire second shelter system on short notice.

The federal government paid 95% of that. Now it's paying less.

Since 2018, federal Interim Housing Assistance Program funding to Toronto has totalled $652.7 million, at a historical 95% reimbursement rate. In December 2024, that changed. New federal directives split refugee-response spending into two buckets: "sustainable and cost-effective" activities (permanent reception centres, purpose-built shelters) still get up to 95% federal cost-sharing in 2025, dropping to up to 75% in 2026. Everything else — including the hotel operations and general shelter capacity the City had no choice but to stand up on short notice during the surge — drops to up to 75% in 2025, then up to 50% in 2026.

The City said this plainly, in its own words, to its own Council: the new formula "penalize[s] municipalities such as Toronto, that had no other choice than to establish emergency programs that can be put in place on short notice, which are typically not cost-effective." Six months later, the number came in: Toronto is set to receive funding for only 26% of what it actually spent on refugee shelter in 2025. The gap, per the Mayor's own public statement: $107 million.

It's not the only cut landing at the same time

The Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit — the program the Mayor calls "the single most effective tool we have for freeing up beds in our shelter system," because it moves people out of shelter into permanent housing — is being cut too, on a different but overlapping timeline: $38 million in 2024/25, $19.75 million in 2025/26, a projected $7.95 million in 2026/27. That's not refugee-specific funding, but it directly affects how fast anyone — refugee claimant or not — can exit the shelter system the refugee caseload is also straining. The City already had to front $4.815 million of its own money earlier this year just to keep 570 households moving into housing before the provincial funding caught up.

The Mayor's own framing of what's left: stop sheltering refugee claimants and put them on the street, undoing the City's progress on encampments, or raise property taxes to cover a federal jurisdiction's bill. "Neither is fair."

What we couldn't confirm

There's a genuine gap between people arriving in Toronto and formally filing a refugee claim — most Ontario claims are filed inland, not at the border, and IHAP only covers people who've already filed. The City has asked Ottawa to close that gap. We don't know if anyone answered. We looked for confirmation that the federal government has responded to any of the City's four formal requests — reverting the cost-share cut, closing the inland-filing gap, creating a portable refugee housing benefit, building a coordinated national reception system — and found none. We also couldn't independently confirm, from a primary source, the specific 2023 episodes of claimants sleeping outside federal immigration offices that got wide media coverage at the time; we could confirm the adjacent, City-documented fact that community organizations stepped in with overflow capacity and got reimbursed for it.

The three moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Revert the 2025-2027 cost-sharing cut for the emergency programs Toronto had no real alternative but to build (RC-NS-01) — the City's own already-stated ask, with a specific, dated $107 million number behind it. 2. Close the inland-claim funding gap so the City stops absorbing the cost of a federal processing delay it doesn't control (RC-NS-02). 3. Restore the Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit allocation to $54 million, the level the Mayor herself has already asked for, to keep the one program everyone agrees frees up shelter beds actually funded (RC-NS-03).

None of these are new asks. They're the City's own requests, already on the record, with the numbers behind them made explicit.
