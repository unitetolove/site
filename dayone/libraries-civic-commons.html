Libraries and the Civic Commons — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/libraries-civic-commons.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/libraries-civic-commons.md, research/cards/libraries-civic-commons.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto's library system just ran a real-world experiment on itself, and the results are strong. The number everyone quotes about what the library is actually worth, though, is thirteen years old.

What's actually happening

Starting this July, all 100 Toronto Public Library branches will be open seven days a week, year-round. That's the finish line of a hours-expansion push the City's been running since 2024, and the results along the way are genuinely striking: when Sunday hours rolled out at every branch in 2025, Sunday visits jumped 50 percent compared to the year before, and Sunday borrowing jumped 54 percent. When the library expanded hours in 2024, the branches involved saw a 44 percent jump in visits. Those aren't estimates or surveys — they're the library's own measured numbers, and they say the same thing three different ways: when the library is open more, people show up.

The number that hasn't kept up

Here's the mismatch. Whenever someone wants to make the case that funding the library pays off, the figure that gets quoted is that Torontonians get $5.63 of value for every dollar spent — about a billion dollars in total impact. That number comes from a 2013 study. It's now well over a decade old, and this research found no sign anyone has redone it since — not this pass, and not the earlier research this project inherited, which already flagged the same gap. So the library's case for "we're worth funding" is stuck using data from before some of today's librarians were hired, while the library's case for "people actually use us, more and more" just got three brand-new, real numbers behind it. One side of the argument is getting stronger. The other is just getting older.

What this doesn't cover

This brief deliberately doesn't get into the library's day-to-day operations, its detailed budget, or the crisis-support and social-work program it runs for vulnerable patrons — that's a whole separate piece of research elsewhere in this project, and duplicating it here would just create two versions of the same facts that could drift apart over time. What this brief is about is the bigger-picture question: is the library core civic infrastructure worth protecting and funding as such, or a nice-to-have that gets cut when budgets tighten. On that question, this pass also didn't find any sign of a specific person or company benefiting from the library being underfunded — unlike some other City services, there's no obvious "someone's profiting from this" story here. It looks more like plain old budget competition than anything else.

What this doesn't settle

Nobody has actually redone the library's own math on what it's worth, so the honest answer to "how much value does the library create today" is: probably still a lot, but nobody's checked recently. This also doesn't settle whether Toronto's library really is the busiest system in the world, as it's often described — that's a hard claim to check without comparing dozens of cities directly, and this pass didn't attempt that comparison.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Get a current number for what the library is actually worth (RC-LCC-01) — commission an updated version of the 2013 study, and use the library's own fresh 2025-2026 hours-expansion data as real, measured evidence rather than starting from scratch. It's a small cost against a budget in the hundreds of millions, and it replaces a stale talking point with a current one — for better or worse, whatever the real number turns out to be. 2. Get the library and the recreation department talking to each other (RC-LCC-02) — right now, nothing found in this research suggests the library and Parks, Forestry and Recreation are formally coordinating on which neighbourhoods most need better access. A light coordination mechanism, not a merger, so the City's two biggest "free space for everyone" systems aren't planning their amenity-gap fixes in isolation from each other.

Neither move is expensive. Both are aimed at the same thing: making sure the library's genuinely strong current performance is backed by evidence just as current, instead of leaning on a headline number that predates the branch's own recent renovation in some cases.
