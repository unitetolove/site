Mobility, Congestion & Transit — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-13 ·

Version: v1.1 · Date: 2026-07-13 · Status: DRAFT · Layer: L-PLATFORM Built from: our research file for that page (L4) + that page's recommendation cards (a recommendation card, a recommendation card, a recommendation card, a recommendation card). No claim below is freehand — every figure traces to a card or the backgrounder it draws from.

---

Most people stuck in Toronto traffic, or waiting for a bus that's stuck in it too, aren't wondering whether the City has studied the problem. It has. Toronto already paid for the answers here — including the uncertainties. The City just passed a transit budget that adds nearly 200,000 service hours and freezes fares for a third straight year. It named its first Chief Congestion Officer and put a $299.4 million number on a real, five-part plan to fix gridlock. It knows exactly how many people ride the TTC, how much the province spends per rider, and how a $6 billion estimate, an $11 billion estimate, and a $44.7 billion estimate for the same underlying problem can all be true at once because they're measuring different things at different scales. (A fourth figure, ~$10 billion, shows up in 2026 coverage of the City's own congestion plan; it's named here rather than quietly dropped, but its underlying study wasn't independently traced this review, so it's excluded from the reconciliation above rather than folded in as confirmed.) The work is done. What's missing is follow-through on the pieces the City controls, and honesty about the two big pieces it doesn't.

One: make bus and streetcar priority a funded, tracked program, not a yearly location count. The Congestion Management Plan already claims real wins this year — streetcars and buses moving faster at 72 locations, a smarter signal network spreading to 244 locations and 356 intersections, a claimed 12% faster trip through downtown. That's the City's own highest-leverage, fully-within-its-authority lever, and it's the one thing that helps the majority of transit riders directly, not just people who drive. Turn it into a multi-year target with an annual public count of lane-kilometres actually delivered, not a one-year list of locations that resets every budget cycle (a recommendation card).

Two: close the One Fare funding cliff and push back on Bill 212 before either one bites. Ontario's One Fare program already eliminated the double fare for anyone transferring between the TTC and GO Transit or four neighbouring systems — a real, popular benefit riders now count on. The province's own fiscal watchdog says the funding for it is only committed through this fiscal year. Nobody has confirmed what happens next. At the same time, a provincial law gives Queen's Park the power to rip out three named Toronto bike lanes over the City's objection — and the province's own internal analysis (CIMA's engineering report, disclosed through litigation and reported by CBC and TorontoToday) found doing so wouldn't even speed up commutes, while it could raise collision risk by more than half — a figure that reporting confirms as accurate, even though the underlying litigation and Bill 212's own legal status were last confirmed in June 2025 and may have moved since. Toronto should put both asks in one resolution: confirm the fare funding before it lapses (a recommendation card), and either repeal the bike-lane rule or release the analysis that undercuts its own rationale before acting on it (a recommendation card).

Three: don't grade our own homework on the congestion plan. Here's the honest part. Every number the City has put out about its own congestion plan working — the 12% faster downtown trips, the shorter construction closures, the growing team of traffic agents — comes from the same news conference. No independent audit has checked any of it yet. That doesn't mean it's wrong. It means a $299.4 million program shouldn't run for three years on the strength of its own press release. Get the Auditor General or an outside data firm to check the claimed numbers against the real traffic data at the plan's midpoint, in 2027, not at the end when it's too late to course-correct (a recommendation card).

What we don't know, said out loud. We don't know if TTC ridership is actually back to 81% of 2019 levels or something close to it — that figure came from news coverage of the City's own budget planning, not the City's own budget document, and even the 2019 baseline it's compared against doesn't quite match the number in the province's own official transit-finance report. We don't know if the province has quietly renewed One Fare funding since the fiscal watchdog's last public report — if it has, good, but nobody's said so plainly. We don't know Bill 212's legal status right now — the most recent update we could confirm is over a year old. And we don't actually know if the congestion plan's headline numbers hold up, because nobody outside City Hall has checked. We also don't know exactly what the ~$10 billion congestion-cost figure in 2026 coverage of the City's own plan is measuring or which study it traces to — it's named here rather than silently excluded, but it's not folded into the $6B/$11B/$44.7B figures above as though it were confirmed. A platform built on "Toronto already paid for the answers" only means something if it says plainly which answers are still open.

The throughline across all three: Toronto has real, funded, working pieces — a transit budget that adds service instead of cutting it, a named official accountable for gridlock, a fare program riders already depend on. The job now is locking in what's working, demanding clarity from Queen's Park on what isn't the City's to fix alone, and checking our own work before we ask anyone else to trust it.
