Mental Wellbeing and Flourishing — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

A mental-health system that only responds to crises will never build a well city. This file is about the other half — not the emergency room, but the parks, libraries, friendships, and sense of purpose that keep people well in the first place. And for once, the biggest number in this file — the one this project had flagged months ago as needing a real check — turned out to hold up.

The number we couldn't confirm before, now confirmed

Earlier work on this topic cited a World Health Organization estimate that loneliness contributes to roughly 871,000 deaths a year worldwide, but flagged it specifically as the one figure most worth double-checking before anyone repeated it publicly. That check is done now. In June 2025, the WHO's own Commission on Social Connection published its first global report, and the number holds: about 100 deaths every hour, more than 871,000 a year, tied to loneliness and isolation. One in six people worldwide are affected. People who are lonely are twice as likely to become depressed. This isn't a rumour anymore — it's a named, dated, published WHO report, and it came with real institutional weight behind it: the World Health Assembly, representing every WHO member country, passed its first-ever resolution on social connection two months earlier, in May 2025.

What Toronto actually knows about itself — and how old that knowledge is

Toronto has real data on this, but it's from 2022, not last year. The Toronto Foundation and the Environics Institute ran a survey of over 4,000 Torontonians that November and found something worth sitting with: 300,000 people in this city said they had no one to turn to. Civic engagement — donating, volunteering, showing up — had dropped 12 percent since the last time they checked, in 2018, working out to about $180 million in lost donations and 36 million lost volunteer hours. Only a quarter of Torontonians said they always have something to look forward to, down from 40 percent four years earlier.

But the same report pushed back against reading this as pure doom: most people still have family and friends to lean on, still trust their neighbours, still feel safe. It's not a city falling apart. It's a city that lost some ground during the pandemic years and hasn't yet been re-measured to see what's happened since. That's the honest gap here — this survey has only run twice, in 2018 and 2022. Nobody has run it a third time. We genuinely don't know if things have gotten better, worse, or stayed the same since 2022.

The fix everyone talks about is still mostly a research project here

"Social prescribing" — where a doctor or link worker connects you to a walking group, a choir, a community garden, or a volunteer role instead of just a pill — keeps coming up as the bridge between the health system and the kind of community life that actually keeps people well. It's real, and it has real evidence behind it. But in Canada, it hasn't been built into the health system anywhere yet. The University of Toronto is running what's being called the first social-prescribing pilot on any Canadian university campus, starting in 2025. That's genuinely exciting — Toronto has real expertise here, housed at the Canadian Institute for Social Prescribing at U of T. But "the first pilot on one campus" and "a citywide program" are very different things, and nothing found in this review shows Toronto has the latter.

What Toronto already has, sitting unnamed

Here's the part that should be encouraging rather than discouraging: the WHO's own report names the exact tools a city needs — "parks, libraries, cafés" — as one of its core recommended interventions. Toronto already has all of those, in abundance. Ravines, library branches, recreation centres, community arts programming. What Toronto doesn't have, as far as this review could find, is any City program, budget line, or plan that names these things as a coordinated "mental wellbeing system" rather than a scattering of separate departments doing separate things. The raw materials are already paid for. Nobody's connected them yet.

What this doesn't settle

It doesn't tell us how Toronto is doing right now — the last real check-in was 2022. It doesn't confirm the exact math behind the WHO's 871,000-deaths figure; the number and its source are real, but this review didn't independently re-derive the calculation itself. It doesn't find a working, citywide social-prescribing program anywhere in Canada, let alone Toronto specifically. And it doesn't find any City of Toronto program that has actually named and coordinated its own wellbeing infrastructure the way this file argues it should.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Run the Toronto Social Capital Study again, on a regular schedule, and call it what it is (a recommendation card) — the City joining the existing 17-partner funding group that already pays for this survey, so Toronto isn't flying blind on its own wellbeing data for years at a stretch. 2. Map what the City already owns and name it as one system (a recommendation card) — parks, libraries, recreation centres, all in one place, publicly, connected to the University of Toronto's own social-prescribing research so any future pilot has a ready-made list of places to refer people to.

Neither one requires new infrastructure or a big new program. Both start from something that already exists — a survey partnership, a pile of City-owned parks and libraries — and ask the City to either keep measuring it or finally name it.
