Building Retrofits and Climate Mitigation — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/building-retrofits-climate-mitigation.md

Version: v1.1 (2026-07-14 adversarial-audit fix: Bill 98/Toronto Green Standard finding added) · Date: 2026-07-14 · Status: DRAFT · Layer: L-PLATFORM Built from: research/backgrounders/building-retrofits-climate-mitigation.md (L4) + research/cards/building-retrofits-climate-mitigation.md (RC-BRC-01, RC-BRC-02, RC-BRC-03). No claim below is freehand — every figure traces to a card or the backgrounder it draws from.

---

Toronto's own numbers say buildings are its single biggest climate problem — about 58% of the city's community-wide emissions, by the City's own count. Toronto's own numbers also say the City has a real plan and real programs already running to fix it. Both of those things are true at once, and so is a third: an independent 2022 assessment found the city off-track for its 2030 target, a big chunk of the federal money the whole plan leaned on has quietly run out, and — most recently — the Province has just stripped the mandatory teeth out of the City's own new-construction green building rulebook.

One: the good news is real — Toronto has a program that actually solves the hardest problem in retrofit policy. Most cities that try to retrofit old rental buildings run into the same wall: the landlord pays for the upgrade, the tenant gets the lower energy bill, so nobody moves. Toronto's Taking Action on Tower Renewal program (TATR) solves that directly — $11.7 million in financing plus $1.7 million in grants for pre-1990 rental apartment buildings, with a condition attached that actually has teeth: no rent increases, no renovictions, tied to the retrofit work. That's not a talking point, it's a documented City program with real dollar figures behind it. It should be bigger (RC-BRC-01).

Two: the federal money this plan counted on has mostly dried up, right when it's needed most. Three separate federal and CMHC programs cited in this file are now closed or closing to new applicants: the Canada Greener Homes Grant stopped taking new applications back in early 2024, with only a paperwork deadline (December 31, 2025) left for people who already got in; CMHC's Greener Affordable Housing Program has fully committed its budget and shut its portal; and the Oil to Heat Pump Affordability Program's application window closes July 31, 2026 — a matter of weeks from now. That leaves one federal channel still genuinely open, the Green Municipal Fund's GHG Impact Retrofit program (up to $10 million per project) — and no source found in this pass confirms Toronto has ever used it. If the City wants to keep its retrofit pace up, either Ottawa needs to announce something new, or Toronto needs to lean harder on its own tools (RC-BRC-01).

Three: the scale of what's actually been done so far is still small. As of the most recent independent count available, one City retrofit program had reached 15 buildings, and another had completed 187 projects over five years. Toronto has thousands of buildings. This isn't a criticism of the programs' design — TATR's tenant protections in particular are genuinely good design — it's a plain statement that the volume moved so far is a small fraction of what the City's own 2030 and 2040 targets require, and an independent 2022 peer-reviewed assessment said exactly that: Toronto beat its 2020 target (mostly because Ontario's grid got cleaner, not because of city action) but is not on track for 2030.

Four: one City rulebook just lost its teeth, and another program still needs a basic status check. The Toronto Green Standard — the rulebook new buildings have to meet — is still numbered Version 4, the same version that's been in effect since May 2022. But as of this year, that numbering is close to beside the point: Ontario's Bill 98, passed June 2, 2026, stripped the mandatory "sustainable design" requirement out of provincial planning law, which the City's own planning department and outside reporting both describe as ending the Standard's enforceable, mandatory floor. Toronto's own chief planner said on the record the change limits the City's ability to advance its climate goals — and the City says it will comply anyway. That's a bigger and more urgent finding than "what version number is it," and it changes what this leaf's own card on this topic should be asking for (RC-BRC-02, now revised). The Better Buildings Partnership, a real City program that shows up in the City's own program list, still could not be confirmed as active or funded in this pass — not because it's gone, but because nobody has checked recently enough to say for sure (RC-BRC-03).

What we don't know, said out loud. We don't know if there's a successor to the Deep Retrofit Challenge, which officially wrapped up over a year ago. We don't know if Toronto has ever applied to the Green Municipal Fund's larger retrofit-financing stream. We don't know the current, real-world status of the Better Buildings Partnership. And every single figure in this file — all sixteen of them — comes from a source that was directly checked once, but not yet independently double-checked by a second pass, which is the normal next step before anything here gets repeated as settled fact.

The throughline: Toronto has the right tools for its biggest climate problem, and at least one of them (TATR) is genuinely well-designed, not just well-intentioned. But the scale hasn't caught up to the target, the federal funding floor is disappearing under the plan as this is being written, and at least one named City program needs someone to go check whether it's actually still there.

Sources: every figure above traces to research/cards/building-retrofits-climate-mitigation.md (RC-BRC-01/02) and the backgrounder's cited ledger claims (CL-80328 through CL-80341, CL-120006) and live-discovery findings (NEW-1, NEW-2) it draws from — see those documents for exact quotes, dates accessed, and the full set of open questions this paper inherits rather than resolves.
