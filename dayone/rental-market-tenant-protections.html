Rental Market & Tenant Protections — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-13 ·

Version: v1.1 · Date: 2026-07-13 · Status: DRAFT · Layer: L-PLATFORM Built from: our research file for that page (L4) + that page's recommendation cards (a recommendation card, a recommendation card, a recommendation card). No claim below is freehand — every figure traces to a card or the backgrounder it draws from.

---

Toronto already paid for the answers here — including the uncertainties. The City spent the last two years measuring exactly what's broken in its own rental market: a vacancy rate below the healthy 3% line, vacant units renting for $512 to $1,089 more a month than occupied ones of the same size, a Landlord and Tenant Board that cut its backlog by a quarter but still runs three times slower than it did in 2018, and a renoviction bylaw finally in force after years of asking. The work is done. What's missing isn't more research — it's follow-through on what the City already knows, and honesty about which piece of it isn't the City's to fix.

One: make the renoviction bylaw prove itself now, not in 2027. Toronto's Rental Renovation Licence Bylaw has been law since July 31, 2025 — a real, in-force municipal tool requiring landlords to get a City licence before evicting a tenant for renovation. But Council gave itself until 2027 to check whether it's working, which means two years of a bad-faith landlord's incentive to game the licence process going unmeasured in public. Fix that gap this term: publish licence outcomes — approved, denied, fined — on a quarterly public dashboard, and fund the inspection capacity to back it up, sized against what RentSafeTO already spends enforcing building standards on comparable Toronto rental stock (a recommendation card). This is entirely within City authority. No one has to grant permission for this one.

Two: the eviction-prevention programs are already over-subscribed — say so, and fund past it. The City's own words, not ours: EPIC and the Rent Bank are "already over-subscribed." The 2026 budget nearly doubled Rent Bank funding since 2023 and added support for 1,100 more households through EPIC and rent subsidies — real money, helping close to 4,500 households stay housed. It is still not enough, by the City's own account, and a provincial bill working its way through Queen's Park in the second half of 2025 would shrink tenant compensation and shorten eviction timelines in ways that will push more people toward these same programs. Scale Rent Bank and EPIC funding to match what that bill actually does to eviction filings once it's in force for a full year — don't wait for the next budget cycle to notice the demand already arrived (a recommendation card).

Three: stop treating the provincial ask as a formality, and start treating it as the headline. This is the honest part, and it's the part a platform document has to say plainly instead of quietly: the two biggest levers in this entire file — the rent-control exemption on everything built after November 2018, and a Landlord and Tenant Board that needs more than just more staff — are the Province's to pull, not the City's. Toronto has asked three times in two years, in three separate Council votes, and been told no each time by inaction. That's not a City failure. It's a City that did its job and a Province that hasn't done its. The honest platform position is to stop burying that ask in a staff report footnote and put it where voters can see it: a standing, annually renewed, publicly tracked demand — restore rent control on post-2018 units, and actually fund LTB capacity in a way that doesn't just add adjudicators who, on the Province's own numbers, are resolving fewer than half as many cases each as they did in 2018 (a recommendation card).

What we don't know, said out loud. We don't know if the renoviction bylaw is actually stopping bad-faith evictions — nobody does yet, including the City. Bill 60 itself is no longer in doubt — it passed and received Royal Assent on November 27, 2025, over Council's own formal objection — but we don't yet know whether every specific provision Council opposed made it into the final enacted text unchanged, or when each of the bill's individual provisions actually comes into force versus waits on a future proclamation. We don't have a real cost estimate for scaling Rent Bank to match Bill 60's actual impact, because a full fiscal year of the law actually being in effect hasn't happened yet to measure. And there's a live question mark over some of the Landlord and Tenant Board's own published performance numbers — an independent tribunal watchdog says the annual report's figures were quietly changed after they were tabled, with no explanation of who had the authority to do that. We're not going to pretend that away. A platform built on "Toronto already paid for the answers" only means something if it's honest that some of those answers are still incomplete.

The throughline across all three: Toronto has already built the muscle — a real bylaw, a funded prevention program, a documented paper trail of provincial asks. The job now is enforcement, scale, and volume on the ask that was never the City's to grant itself.
