AI's Labour-Market and Income-Replacement Effects — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Here's the honest, unsatisfying answer to "is AI taking Canadian jobs yet": the best available evidence says no, not measurably, not yet. And here's the second honest, equally unsatisfying answer: Canadian youth unemployment just hit its worst level since 2010, outside the pandemic. Both of those sentences are true at the same time, and anyone offering you a clean story where only one of them matters is skipping half the evidence.

What the rigorous evidence actually says

Yale's Budget Lab published new research in May 2026 using a serious statistical method — comparing AI-exposed jobs to similar non-exposed jobs, correcting for the fact that AI-exposed jobs were already different before AI existed (more educated, more often held by women, less sensitive to recessions). Their finding: no measurable effect on employment or wages that they can distinguish from zero, statistically. Not "small effect" — genuinely can't tell the difference from no effect at all. They also checked whether people are switching occupations more than usual, the kind of thing you'd expect if AI were pushing people out of old jobs into new ones. No unusual increase there either.

The researchers themselves don't conclude AI is harmless. Their own words: "AI seems quite likely to eventually leave its mark on the labor market, even if it has not already." That's a genuinely careful, non-alarmist finding — not a declaration of victory for either side of the debate.

What the youth numbers say

Statistics Canada counted it plainly: 14.7% youth unemployment in September 2025, the worst since 2010 excluding the pandemic years. For teenagers specifically (ages 15-19), 20.8% — that figure is a summer-into-fall quarterly average (July through September), not a September-only snapshot, so it's not directly comparable month-to-month with the 14.7% figure, though both point the same direction. Separately, industry surveys suggest three in ten HR leaders in Canada and the US say they're deliberately hiring fewer entry-level workers in favor of people with more experience — though that specific number hasn't been independently checked yet, and should be treated as reported, not confirmed.

Here's the part that matters most for honesty's sake: Statistics Canada's own analysts, looking at their own data, said they can't tell whether this is AI, or the post-pandemic labour market settling, or immigration policy changes, or trade tensions with the US, or some mix of all of it. They said so explicitly. That's not this project being cagey — that's the government's own statisticians declining to blame AI for something they can't yet prove AI caused.

The gap nobody's talking about enough

Here's the fact that should worry anyone paying attention regardless of which side of the "is AI disrupting jobs yet" debate they're on: Canada doesn't currently have a working income-replacement safety net ready if it turns out AI-driven displacement does arrive. Ontario ran a basic income pilot from 2017 to 2019 — a real program, roughly 4,000 people enrolled, real money, up to $1,415 a month for a single person. A new government cancelled it before it finished. At the federal level, there was a serious bill in the Senate — Bill S-233 — working its way toward becoming Canada's first real guaranteed-income framework. It died when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025, the same event that killed the country's one serious attempt at AI regulation.

So if the Yale Budget Lab's caution turns out to be right — that AI's mark on the labour market just hasn't landed yet — Canada currently has no active safety net specifically built for when it does.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Build the local early-warning system now, before it's needed (a recommendation card). Toronto's own employment services already track who's using its programs. This card adds one more layer: cross-reference that against Statistics Canada's own AI-exposure categories, so if a Toronto-specific study — one is already underway — confirms an AI-specific driver of youth unemployment here, the City isn't starting from zero. It's a data plumbing fix, not a new program.

2. A narrow, time-limited bridge fund for the highest-risk group (a recommendation card). Not a basic income — the City can't create that, only Queen's Park and Ottawa can, and right now neither has one running. This is smaller: short-term transition support through Toronto's existing employment services, specifically for people whose last job falls into StatCan's highest-risk category, to bridge the gap between losing work and finding the next thing, while the province and feds figure out whether they're bringing back a real income floor.

What this doesn't settle

Nothing here fixes the actual gap: Ontario cancelled its pilot, and the federal bill died procedurally, not because anyone concluded it was a bad idea. Only the province and the federal government can bring either back. This brief isn't pretending a City data project or a small bridge fund is a substitute for that. What Toronto can do, while it waits, is not be caught flat-footed — build the monitoring and the narrow safety net piece it actually controls, instead of waiting for someone else to act first.
