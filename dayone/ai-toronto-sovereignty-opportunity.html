Toronto's AI-Sector Economic Opportunity and Sovereignty — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto is winning at AI, by the numbers. It's also losing its best people, by the numbers. Both of those things are true at the same time, and a real assessment of Toronto's AI opportunity has to hold both without flinching from either.

The winning part

Ontario pulled in somewhere between $42 and $52 billion of GDP from AI-related jobs between 2019 and 2024 — that's roughly half of everything AI added to Canada's economy in that stretch, captured by one province. The Greater Toronto Area is projected to keep getting the biggest share of new AI jobs going forward, nearly 9,000 a year, more than anywhere else in the country. Cohere, the Toronto-based AI company, just merged with a German AI firm and is headed toward a roughly $20 billion combined valuation once the round closes, on top of up to $240 million in federal money committed to its Cambridge, Ontario compute facility, on top of a further $220 million private deal to bring next-generation chips onto Canadian soil. This isn't a speculative story about potential. It's already happened, and the trendlines say more of it is coming, concentrated here.

The losing part

A Toronto Dominion Bank economics report from May 2026 said it plainly: Canada's problem isn't attracting talent, it's keeping it. Tech workers in the US get paid 46% more, before you even account for the exchange rate or the stock packages American firms hand out. And it's not the average graduate who leaves — it's the best ones. Top Canadian-born students leave the country at roughly double the rate of students at the bottom of the class. Top international students leave even faster. TD's own conclusion wasn't "cut taxes and it'll fix itself" — they said plainly that tax policy alone won't solve it. The actual problem is that Canada doesn't have enough companies in the middle — not tiny startups, not the handful of giants, but the mid-sized, fast-growing firms that give someone a reason to build a career here instead of moving south.

Why this matters together, not separately

Here's the thing worth sitting with: the growth numbers and the brain-drain numbers aren't actually in tension. They're the same story from two angles. Toronto has built something real — research strength, capital, a company like Cohere reaching an estimated $20 billion valuation. But building it and keeping the people who built it are different problems, and right now Canada is solving the first one faster than the second. A strategy that only counts the GDP numbers and ignores the retention numbers is telling half the story. So is a strategy that only worries about brain drain and doesn't notice how much has already been built here to retain people for.

There's also a loose thread worth naming honestly: Cohere just merged with a German company. Some of the up to $240 million in federal money that went into Cohere was explicitly framed as building Canadian sovereign AI capacity — capacity the country wouldn't be dependent on foreign firms for. Nobody has sorted out yet what a merger with a European AI company does to that framing. This brief doesn't pretend to have the answer. It's flagging that the question exists and hasn't been asked publicly yet, as far as this review could find.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Turn the City's own AI pilots into a paid talent pipeline (a recommendation card). The City is already running AI pilots — Public Health's documentation tool, the food-safety prediction model. Right now those are just staff assignments. This card proposes formalizing some of those roles into a named fellowship, marketed directly at Toronto AI and computer science graduates, so working on real public-interest AI problems in this city becomes a visible, credentialed career option — not a consolation prize next to a US tech job.

2. Give Toronto's own AI-adoption firms a directory and a small leg up in City contracts (a recommendation card). Toronto reportedly has more AI startups per capita than any other Canadian city. But there's no City-run list of the local AI-adoption and consulting firms, and no procurement preference helping them win City work. This card proposes both — not an exclusive set-aside, just a modest scoring edge, paired with visibility.

What this doesn't settle

Neither card touches immigration policy, tax policy, or the deeper "missing middle" of scaling companies TD's report says is the real structural problem — those are federal and provincial levers, not municipal ones. And neither card resolves the Cohere-merger question above. What Toronto can do at the municipal level is build the visible, local, public-facing pieces of a talent and adoption ecosystem — not the whole fix, but a real piece of it, sized honestly to what a city government actually controls.
