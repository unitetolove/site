Active Transportation and Cycling — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Status: DRAFT · Layer: L-PLATFORM Built from: our research file for that page (L4) + that page's recommendation cards (a recommendation card, a recommendation card). No claim below is freehand — every figure traces to a card or the backgrounder it draws from.

---

Toronto has built almost half of the cycling network it planned — 230 of 500 kilometres — and has 100 more committed over the next two years. Independent research on Toronto's own streets finds protected bike lanes make cyclists measurably safer, not just on the lane itself but on the blocks around it. And in a court case that put the province's own internal paperwork on the record, Ontario's own Ministry of Transportation admitted, in writing, that it had no data to support tearing out three of Toronto's most-used bike lanes. That's not an advocate's talking point. That's the government's own file.

One: settle the argument with a real number, not another anecdote. Right now both sides of the bike lane fight are arguing without a full accounting. The province couldn't produce data to support removal — a court said so, based on the province's own briefing. But Toronto doesn't have a complete cost-benefit study either, just a $50 million estimate of what tearing the lanes out would cost and a patchwork of per-project numbers. Commission the real study — modelled on Copenhagen, which claims close to $800 million in value for every $300 million it spends on cycling — and put a Toronto-specific number on the table instead of importing someone else's (a recommendation card).

Two: say what happens next, before the court tells us. The Court of Appeal could rule any day. Toronto should say now, on the record, what it does either way: if the lanes stay, keep building at the current pace. If the province wins and forces removal, the ask is simple — the province ordered it, the province pays the estimated $50 million, not Toronto taxpayers (a recommendation card).

What we don't know, said out loud. We don't know how the Court of Appeal will rule, or when — it's been under advisement since January. We don't know if the province will accept a loss or reach for the notwithstanding clause instead; the Premier's own words after the first ruling — "they elect parties to make decisions, they don't elect judges" — leave that door open. We don't know why one Toronto bike lane proposal costs roughly $1.5 million a kilometre and another roughly $3.75 million — more than double, for reasons nobody has explained publicly. And we don't know if the University Avenue bike lanes are actually slowing down ambulances, because the people who'd know — the paramedics themselves — haven't said they are, and the City's own watchdog points at hospital wait times and understaffing instead.

The throughline: Toronto isn't guessing here. It has real safety data on its own streets, a court record that undercuts the case for tearing lanes out, and a genuine, specific cost if it happens anyway. The job now is turning that into a real study and a real position — not waiting to react to whatever the courts or Queen's Park do next.
