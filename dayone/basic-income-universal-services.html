Basic Income and Universal Basic Services — Day-One Paper

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from that page's recommendation cards (a recommendation card, a recommendation card) and our research file for that page; every claim below traces to one of those two documents.

What a mayor does in week one on this file

Ontario already ran the experiment. Roughly 4,000 people in Hamilton, Brantford, Thunder Bay, and Lindsay got unconditional cash for a while, and 79 percent of them said their health got better — most kept working, some moved to better jobs (a recommendation card). Then the province cancelled it early anyway, for stated political reasons, not because it failed. That's the evidence this file starts from. Toronto is already a UBS city in pieces — libraries, recreation, public health, the Fair Pass discount — without ever having planned it that way. The week-one job on this file is doing the two things that are actually available: building a real plan for the half of this agenda the City controls, and being precise about what it's asking senior governments for on the half it doesn't.

1. Commission the costed UBS plan that doesn't exist yet. The City already runs a partial universal-basic-services model, but no one has ever added it up, sequenced it, or costed what expanding it coherently would take (a recommendation card). Week one directs staff to produce exactly that: an inventory of existing UBS-adjacent programs, a list of proposed expansions already under discussion elsewhere in this research, and a real multi-year sequencing and funding plan for each piece. This costs nothing to commission and commits no funding — it's the analytical step that has to happen before any bigger ask can be made credibly (a recommendation card).

2. Send a specific, evidence-backed resolution to Queen's Park and Ottawa — not a vague appeal. Four independent pilots (Finland, Stockton, Kenya, and Ontario's own 2017-19 pilot) all found the same thing: people receiving unconditional or near-unconditional cash didn't stop working, and in some cases did better at it (a recommendation card). Week one passes a resolution asking the Province and the federal government to study and pursue extending Canada's already-proven Child Benefit/Guaranteed Income Supplement model to working-age adults, citing Toronto's own frontline poverty data and the pilot's own findings (a recommendation card).

What this office does not control, and won't pretend to

The City cannot run a basic income program. That's not a political choice this office is making — it's a fiscal and constitutional fact about which level of government controls income transfers in Canada (backgrounder). Nothing in this paper proposes the City fund or administer a basic income itself, and residents deserve that stated plainly rather than implied away. What the City can do is build the UBS half of this agenda for real, and use its evidence and its voice on the cash half.

The honest bottom line

The evidence on giving people money without conditions is about as consistent as social-policy evidence gets: four different pilots, four different countries, the same basic finding. What's missing in Toronto isn't proof this works — it's a plan for the piece the City can actually build, and a clear, specific ask for the piece it can't. Week one produces the plan and sends the ask. Neither promises a result the City can't deliver on its own.

Uncertainties this paper inherits from its cards

No source in the evidence base costs a complete Toronto UBS package — that's exactly the gap the costed-plan action is meant to close, not a figure this paper can cite yet (a recommendation card). The pilot evidence behind the advocacy resolution is strong on individual-level outcomes but does not resolve what a permanent, universal, full-population program would do to wages, prices, or the broader labour market — this paper does not claim that question is settled (a recommendation card). The current status of the Ontario Basic Income Pilot's own $200 million cancellation-related class action was not confirmed in this review.
