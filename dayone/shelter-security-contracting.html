$109 Million in Shelter and Encampment Security — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/shelter-security-contracting.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/shelter-security-contracting.md, research/cards/shelter-security-contracting.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto is tied into eight private security contracts worth roughly $109 million if the City runs them to full length. It's spent $35.2 million so far this year. Most of it touches shelters, encampments, or the systems around them. None of this is secret, exactly — but it also isn't published anywhere. It came out because a journalist asked the City directly for the numbers.

What's actually happening

Three contracts for security at shelter respite centres run $44.8 million combined. A shelter-core-sites contract adds $15 million. There's an $11.9 million agreement with Garda Canada Security Corporation for "encampment support." Add a $4.8 million St. Lawrence Market contract, $14.8 million for Union Station, and an $18.2 million catch-all for private guards at "city-wide locations," and the total comes to $109 million across eight contracts signed in 2023 and 2024.

This tracks with what this library already had on file: a ~$42 million 2023 award split across three firms — A.S.P. Incorporated, The West Egg Group, and Garda Canada — for shelter security specifically, plus at least $17.55 million in separate West Egg contracts for shelter safety ambassadors and Union Station work, plus a $2,937,168-a-year Encampment Support award from 2024. That last figure doesn't match the $11.9 million Garda Canada encampment number from the new December 2025 data — probably a different contract or a renewed one at a bigger scope, but nobody has reconciled the two, including this document.

The oversight gap nobody has closed yet

The City's own Auditor General looked at this in 2025 and found something specific: shelter operators who procure their own security teams — separate from the City's direct contracts — face no standardized oversight at all. That's not a general concern about spending levels. That's an audit finding that a defined category of security spending has no consistent review process. Nothing found in this pass confirms whether that's been fixed.

The wage gap, stated honestly

Shelter security work pays about $18.15 an hour at entry level. The same job-posting source that gives that figure lists contracted security services broadly at $25 to $60 an hour. That's a real gap, and it's suggestive — but it's not a matched comparison. The two numbers come from the same aggregator, not from a breakdown of what any specific contract actually costs versus what the guard working it takes home. This document doesn't turn that gap into a markup calculation, because nobody has the data to do that honestly yet.

A Council member asked the obvious question, on the record

A sitting member of Council with a declared intention to run for mayor called the $109 million figure "something we should look into" and argued the spending is a symptom of "a failing homelessness policy," saying current security at encampments is "not to connect these individuals with shelter or housing, but really just to stand there, take notes and monitor." They suggested the money would be better spent on police. The Mayor's Office pushed back with a different frame: security is budgeted, unchanging, split between high-risk in-house sites and flexible contracted coverage elsewhere, with 24/7 monitoring across 1,500 city facilities, and the "primary response to encampments is outreach and connection to social services." Both of those are real, on-the-record positions from the same news story. Neither has been tested against an actual audit of whether the spending accomplishes what either side says it does. (Both speakers' names are preserved only in this leaf's backgrounder Source citation, per this project's institutional-framing convention for prose.)

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't confirm whether the Auditor General's oversight gap has actually been closed. It doesn't reconcile the two different Garda Canada encampment-security figures. It doesn't produce an actual profit-margin number for any contractor, because no source anywhere gives a matched cost breakdown for a specific contract. And it doesn't resolve whether the Council member or the Mayor's Office has the better read on what this spending accomplishes — that's a real, unsettled disagreement between two institutional positions, reported here as exactly that.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Close the operator-procured security oversight gap the Auditor General already identified (RC-SSC-01) — extending whatever standard applies to the City's own direct contracts to the ones individual shelter operators arrange themselves. 2. Publish this $109 million breakdown as a standing disclosure, not something that only surfaces when a reporter asks for it directly (RC-SSC-02) — the data already exists inside the City; this is about routinizing what's already been compiled once.

Neither of these settles whether $109 million is the right number. They make sure the next person asking that question doesn't have to file a data request to get an answer.
