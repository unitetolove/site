Short-Term Rental Regulation — Day-One Paper

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/short-term-rental-regulation.md

Status: DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer, The Unknown Soldier synthesis · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from research/cards/short-term-rental-regulation.md (RC-str-01–02) and research/backgrounders/short-term-rental-regulation.md; every claim below traces to one of those two documents.

What a mayor does in week one on this file

Toronto already has a real STR regulatory program — registration, a principal-residence rule, an accommodation tax, and an enforcement record running into the tens of thousands of actions. The week-one job is not building a new bylaw; it's closing the measurement gap between "the program is active" and "the program is working."

1. Commission the independent follow-up study the City doesn't have. The only rigorous independent count of Toronto's STR market and the housing it displaces is a 2019 academic study — six years old and from before the bylaw's own 2024 amendments. The City's own enforcement statistics (applications, revocations, complaints) measure program activity, not market outcome. Week one: fund a follow-up study, using the original study's own methodology where possible, so the next housing-policy conversation about STRs runs on current data instead of a pre-bylaw baseline (RC-str-01).

2. Turn the 2024 Auditor General's findings into a recurring scorecard. The City's own audit already did the hard work of defining the right compliance metrics — the share of undetected non-compliant transactions, the count of unregistered listings on secondary platforms, the count of operators over the 180-night and 3-bedroom limits. What's missing is doing it again, on a schedule, so Council and residents can see whether the April 2024 amendments and any 2025 enforcement measures actually moved those numbers. Week one: direct Municipal Licensing and Standards and the Auditor General's office to formalize this as an annual report (RC-str-02).

3. Be plain about what the enforcement numbers do and don't show. Over 18,000 applications, $20 million in accommodation tax, tens of thousands of listings removed — these are real, substantial figures, and week one doesn't downplay them. But the same audit that produced them also found at least one in ten transactions going undetected and hundreds of listings advertising off the licensed platforms entirely. Both facts are true at once, and residents deserve to hear both rather than a selectively rosy or selectively alarming version.

What this office does not control, and won't pretend to

The bylaw, licensing, and tax are entirely municipal — this is one of the cleaner cases in this city's toolkit where the City isn't waiting on the province or Ottawa. That also means there's no one else to blame if the measurement gap RC-str-01 and RC-str-02 both target isn't closed; it's a City choice not to have commissioned the follow-up study or the recurring scorecard yet, not a jurisdictional constraint.

The honest bottom line

Toronto's STR program is active and has teeth — the enforcement numbers are real. What isn't yet established is whether it's actually preserving the housing stock it exists to protect, because the City has never gone back and re-measured the market the way it was measured once, before the bylaw existed. Week one starts there: fund the study, formalize the scorecard, and only then decide whether the bylaw itself needs to change.

Uncertainties this paper inherits from its cards

Neither RC-str-01 nor RC-str-02 claims to know what a follow-up study or recurring scorecard would find — that's the entire point of commissioning them. Several figures this paper's framing rests on (current registration fees, the specific 2025 enforcement mechanisms, the exact penalty amounts) are flagged ⚠️ VERIFY in the backgrounder, drawn from secondary reporting rather than a primary City source this pass could independently confirm. None of these gaps are resolved here — they are named so this paper isn't read as more certain than its own sources are.
