Aging Well: Home Care and Aging-in-Place — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.1 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Most seniors want to grow old at home. Toronto and Ontario both say they agree. The gap between that agreement and what's actually funded and measured is the story here.

What's actually happening

The National Institute on Ageing's 2024 national survey found 80 percent of older Canadians want to age in their own homes. But that number hides something important: 89 percent of homeowners feel that way, versus only 64 percent of renters. Renters want to age in place at a meaningfully lower rate — probably because they have far less control over whether their home actually can support them as they age. Nobody in the research this project reviewed has specifically studied why that gap exists or what would close it.

Toronto has been doing real work here for over a decade. Its first Seniors Strategy (2013-2017) completed 90 of 91 recommendations. Its second (2018-2022) completed 24 of 27, with the rest substantially underway. A third strategy is now being built: the City ran a large 2025 consultation — nearly 5,100 residents surveyed, workshops, focus groups specifically reaching Black, Indigenous, 2SLGBTQ+, low-income, unhoused, and newcomer seniors — but the City's own page says the actual report won't reach Council until 2027. The good work of listening is done; the actual new commitments are still to come.

A program worth knowing about

Toronto already runs a small but genuinely promising program: Community Paramedic-Led Clinics, embedded directly inside apartment buildings with high concentrations of frail or homebound seniors. It's currently in 16 buildings, with an enhanced pilot in 9 of them done in partnership with a hospital-based NORC Innovation Centre. The City has identified 21 neighbourhoods citywide with the highest concentrations of seniors — meaning the current program covers a fraction of where the need has already been mapped. This is the kind of program that's cheap to describe as a win and easy to leave under-scaled, because expanding it doesn't require new legislation, just money and paramedic staffing capacity the City is already stretching thin elsewhere.

The money story doesn't fully add up yet

Ontario has committed real new dollars to home care: $1.1 billion in new funding announced in the 2025 Economic Outlook, and another $1.1 billion in the 2026 Budget — $2.2 billion combined. That's a genuine fiscal commitment, not a rounding error. But the province's own independent Financial Accountability Office found that the actual number of nursing and personal-care hours each senior receives has been basically flat since 2019-2020. Either the new money hasn't had time to show up in the numbers yet, or it's being absorbed by rising costs somewhere else in the system. Nobody found in this review has explained which.

An old warning that still matters

In 2015, Ontario's Auditor General found that of $2.4 billion in public home-care funding flowing through the old Community Care Access Centre system, 62 percent went to private contracted companies — and that as much as 18 percent of that contracted money, roughly $267.8 million a year, was being retained as profit and administrative overhead rather than reaching actual care. The Auditor General couldn't even independently verify the companies' own numbers, because the province had no legal power to audit their books. That specific figure is now over a decade old, and the system has since been restructured (a 2023 law wound down the old Local Health Integration Networks and created Ontario Health atHome). But the underlying structural worry — that routing public home-care money through layers of contracted, financially-interested providers means less of it reaches the person who actually needs care — hasn't gone away. A legal clinic representing seniors raised almost exactly the same concern about the 2023 restructuring, warning it could "create conflict between the financial interests of service providers and the health care needs of the home-care clients they serve." Nobody has re-run the Auditor General's kind of rigorous check under the new system yet. That's a real gap, not a solved problem from a decade ago.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't say why renters want to age in place less than owners, or what would fix it — that's a genuine, undocumented gap. It doesn't say why home-care hours have stayed flat despite new funding. It doesn't confirm the exact, current size of Ontario's long-term-care waitlist — the widely repeated "~50,000" figure wasn't traced to one clear, dated, primary source in this review. And it doesn't know whether today's for-profit home-care contracting looks anything like the 2015 audit's numbers, because nobody has checked recently.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Expand the Community Paramedic-Led Clinic program into more of the 21 neighbourhoods the City has already identified as highest-need (a recommendation card) — using the existing pilot's own data to guide where it goes next, rather than starting from scratch. 2. Ask the Province to actually study the renter/owner aging-in-place gap (a recommendation card) — not to propose a fix nobody has evidence for yet, but to get the evidence that would let anyone propose one honestly.

Neither move invents a new number. Both start from what the City and the NIA have already published, including the parts — the renter gap, the flat funding-to-hours ratio, the unanswered contracting question — that don't make for a clean success story.
