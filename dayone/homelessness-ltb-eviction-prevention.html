The Eviction Tribunal Backlog and Homelessness — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Ontario's Landlord and Tenant Board is, by law, the only door through which a landlord can actually evict a tenant — there is no lockout without an order, and no order without the Board. That makes the Board's own backlog and wait times a direct measure of how much runway a tenant facing eviction actually has before they lose housing. This brief checks what that runway looks like right now, and finds a genuinely improving system sitting on top of an unresolved integrity problem in its own official numbers.

The good news is real

The backlog fell from just over 53,000 cases to about 41,500 through 2024-25. That happened because the province actually funded it — three separate rounds of money between late 2022 and 2024, and the adjudicator roster grew from 51 people to 133. That's not a talking point; it's independently confirmed straight from the tribunal-watchdog group's own analysis of the official numbers.

The bad news is also real, and it's not just "still too slow"

Here's the part that should worry anyone relying on the Board's own official annual report: the report that got tabled in the Legislature and released to the public in late 2025 was quietly changed sometime after that, with no public notice that anything had been altered. The change wasn't cosmetic. The original report said each adjudicator resolved about 382 cases a year through a hearing — a number that, compared to the 950 cases each adjudicator resolved back in 2018, made the current tribunal look dramatically less efficient despite having more than double the staff. The revised, undisclosed version says 528 cases per adjudicator instead. That's a big difference, and nobody at Tribunals Ontario has explained why it changed, or under what authority they get to edit an official government report after it's already been given to the Legislature. The watchdog group asked, in writing, in May. As of their public statement in June, nobody had answered.

That matters for a simple reason: every number in this brief, and every number anyone else uses to argue about whether the LTB is fixed or still broken, traces back to that same report. If the report's own numbers can be silently changed without a public trail, that's a problem bigger than any single statistic.

Bill 60 is not fully in force yet — even though a lot of coverage treats it as done

The legislation meant to speed up the LTB got royal assent back in November 2025, and for seven months, nothing happened — none of it was actually in force. That changed July 1 of this year: a first batch of changes is now law — shorter deadlines to challenge an order, a mandatory paperwork form for payment plans, bigger fines for landlords who break the rules. But the two changes that most directly affect how much notice a tenant gets before an eviction case can even be filed — cutting the arrears-notice window from 14 days to 7, and a rule letting landlords skip compensation on some personal-use evictions — don't take effect until September 21. Anyone using those specific numbers as if they're already the law right now is wrong, as of this week.

Prevention that works, if it reaches people in time

Two other places have tried a different approach: intervene before a case ever reaches a hearing. British Columbia funds a rent bank that gave out no-interest loans and, in one year, estimated $27.5 million in combined savings and more than 600 people kept from becoming homeless. Wales went further — it's not a program tenants have to know about and apply for, it's a legal duty: once someone is likely to become homeless within 56 days, the local authority has to act. Toronto has its own version of this, the EPIC program, and it's worked — 849 households kept housed between 2017 and 2020, confirmed cost-effective by an outside evaluator. But it's voluntary, not a legal guarantee the way Wales's is. As the LTB's own notice periods get shorter under Bill 60, a program that depends on someone finding out about it and applying in time is a weaker safety net than a legal duty triggered automatically.

What this doesn't settle

Nobody has explained the data change in the official report, and this brief doesn't pretend to know why it happened. Toronto's own Rent Bank doesn't have a published cost-effectiveness number, despite a real search for one. And a real estate board report that's supposed to have useful backlog-reduction recommendations is cited here only in general terms — the actual report wasn't tracked down and read in full.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. A statutory prevention trigger, modeled on Wales (a recommendation card) — move Ontario's prevention programs from "you have to find out about this and apply" to "the system finds you automatically once you're at risk," using the same 56-day-style design Wales already runs. 2. A formal demand that Tribunals Ontario explain and never repeat the silent data change (a recommendation card) — a transparency ask, not a spending ask: if an official report gets revised after it's public, say so, in the report itself.

Both are aimed at the same underlying goal: making sure the numbers this whole debate runs on can actually be trusted, and making sure the help that exists reaches people before the clock runs out, not after.
