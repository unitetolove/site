A Court Just Changed the Law — And the Change Is Still Live — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/homelessness-legal-rights-frameworks.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/homelessness-legal-rights-frameworks.md, research/cards/homelessness-legal-rights-frameworks.md only — no fresh citations introduced here. Not legal advice.

Two months ago, an Ontario court did something no Canadian court had done before: it ruled that being homeless is, in itself, a protected characteristic under the Charter's equality guarantee — the same legal category as race, sex, or disability. That's not a small thing. It's also not settled. This brief explains what actually happened, and why the most important sentence in it might be "under appeal."

What the court actually said

The case was about a specific piece of land in Kitchener — 100 Victoria Street North — where people have been living in an encampment since 2021, and which the Region of Waterloo wanted cleared to build a transit hub. The Region tried a new, narrower bylaw after an earlier, broader one had already been struck down for the same site. The court struck this one down too, on two separate grounds.

First: the numbers. 2,371 people without housing in the region, against 377 shelter spaces — and even that undercounts the real gap, because many of those beds don't actually work for the specific people who'd need them: gender-restricted beds, congregate settings that don't work for someone with certain mental health conditions. The court found that closing this specific site, with nowhere else in the region where it would even be legal to put up a tent, put people's lives and safety at genuine risk. That's an application of a principle Canadian courts have recognized since 2009 — you can't make it illegal to shelter yourself if there's truly nowhere else to go.

Second, and this is the actually new part: the court found that homelessness itself qualifies as a protected ground for equality-rights purposes — not just that this particular bylaw affected homeless people badly, but that homelessness is now, in this court's reasoning, in the same legal category as being a woman, or Indigenous, or having a disability. Practically, that mattered here because the same bylaw was also found to hit Indigenous residents (17% of the area's homeless population, against under 2% of the general population), people with disabilities, and women and gender-diverse people especially hard.

Why "under appeal" is doing a lot of work in that last sentence

The Region of Waterloo isn't accepting this. They filed a formal appeal in June, arguing the judge got it wrong on multiple points. The Ontario government joined them — officially, to protect the transit hub project, but also publicly floating something much bigger: using the Charter's notwithstanding clause, the constitutional escape hatch that lets a legislature override certain court rulings entirely. They haven't pulled that lever. But they've said out loud that they're thinking about it.

So here's the honest state of play: a real, significant legal precedent exists. It might not survive the next twelve months in its current form. Anyone treating this as settled law is getting ahead of the actual facts.

What this means, and doesn't mean, for Toronto

Toronto's own approach to clearing encampments is different from what got struck down in Waterloo. Toronto offers people shelter placements — contested by advocates as inadequate, but offered — before enforcement happens. The Waterloo Region's own position was blunter: once that site closed, they weren't going to allow tents anywhere on their property, period. That's a real, material difference in the facts, and this research found no case, no legal opinion, nothing on the record testing whether Toronto's specific model would survive the same kind of challenge. Nobody knows. That's not a gap in this research — it's a gap in the actual legal record.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't establish a general right to housing in Canada — the courts have already rejected that broader claim once, in a 2013-2014 case, and the Waterloo line of cases works specifically because it stays narrow: a specific site, a specific shelter-capacity gap, not a sweeping constitutional entitlement. It doesn't resolve whether procedural fairness — proper notice, a real chance to be heard — is its own independent Charter ground in encampment cases; the court seemed ready to decide that question and then didn't. And it doesn't tell us what the Court of Appeal will do, or whether the province will actually reach for the notwithstanding clause if it loses there too.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Get an honest, independent answer on whether this applies to Toronto (RC-HLR-01) — not from the City itself, whose policy is what's being assessed, but from a law school or legal clinic capable of publishing the kind of rigorous case analysis this whole brief leans on. 2. Actually watch the appeal (RC-HLR-02) — a small, almost administrative step: make sure this project notices when the Court of Appeal rules, rather than finding out by accident months later.

Neither of these tells anyone what to believe about whether Toronto's policy is fair. They make sure that whatever gets believed is based on what the law actually says — including the part where it might still change.
