Parking Policy and Management — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Status: DRAFT · Layer: L-PLATFORM Built from: our research file for that page (L4) + that page's recommendation cards (a recommendation card, a recommendation card). No claim below is freehand — every figure traces to a card or the backgrounder it draws from.

---

The Toronto Parking Authority makes real money — $44.7 million net income last year, a record $177.3 million in revenue budgeted for this one. It's one of the few city agencies that pays the City rather than the other way around. And Toronto has already, quietly, cut the amount of parking required for new buildings by more than two-thirds since 2016, with the province's own laws helping rather than hindering that shift. That's the good version of this story. Here's the part that doesn't fit as neatly: the agency making all that money has had its entire board thrown out by Council twice — once in 2017 after nearly losing $2.5 million on a land deal built on a lobbyist's numbers instead of an independent appraisal, and again in November, this time for budget reasons. A governance review is underway. We don't know yet what it will find.

One: don't let a good balance sheet buy a pass on governance. TPA's finances are genuinely strong, and that's exactly why the governance question is easy to let slide — the agency isn't costing anyone money, on paper. But the City's own watchdog just found, in May, that one of seven recommendations from years-old audits still isn't fully implemented. When the governance review lands, make its findings public in full and put a real deadline on fixing what it finds — not another report that sits on a shelf (a recommendation card).

Two: measure what we've already done before deciding whether to do more. Toronto has cut new-building parking by two-thirds in eight years, mostly by getting out of the way of a shift that was already happening. Studies from UCLA and the OECD say parking minimums can add well over half again to a building's construction cost where they actually bind. Toronto has the project-by-project data to check whether that's true here — and hasn't published that number yet. Do the math before the next round of the debate happens without it (a recommendation card).

What we don't know, said out loud. We don't know what the TPA governance review will find, or when it lands — it's due sometime before this Council term ends, and that's the only deadline that exists right now. We don't know if TPA's vendor contracts have the same kind of red flags its land deals once did — nobody has checked, as far as we could find. We don't know how much of Toronto's parking-minimum reduction actually lowered housing costs here, specifically, as opposed to just being a number that looks good in a report. And we don't know if Paris's roughly-10,000-space, referendum-approved parking removal — a figure we found via search and haven't traced back to a primary Paris source, and the most aggressive comparator we found — would even be legal or politically survivable here; we cited it as a comparator, not a template.

The throughline: this is a well-run agency with a governance problem it hasn't solved twice in a row, sitting on top of a genuinely good policy shift — less mandatory parking, more housing room — that the City hasn't bothered to prove worked. Fix the oversight. Show the math. Both are cheaper than finding out the hard way that neither was actually solid.
