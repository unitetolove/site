Data Privacy and Municipal Information Governance — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.1 (2026-07-14 adversarial-audit fix: corrected "no privacy-assessment checklist exists" framing) · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

The province just rewrote the rules for how the City handles your personal information, and the association representing the people who'll have to follow those rules is already saying the deadline doesn't work. Meanwhile the City's own AI policy is more specific than you'd expect, and its last big data project is still an open argument about who told the truth.

What just changed, and when

Ontario passed Bill 97 this spring — Royal Assent April 24, 2026 — and it rewrites part of the law governing how municipalities like Toronto handle your personal information. Starting this July and fully in force by January 2027, the City will have to do a formal privacy risk assessment before it collects your personal information, not after something goes wrong. If there's a breach — your data stolen, lost, or improperly shared — the City now has to tell you and tell the provincial privacy watchdog, not just deal with it quietly. The province's privacy office also gets new power to go in and audit how an institution actually handles information, not just take its word for it.

Here's the catch, and it's not us saying it — it's AMCTO, the association of municipal clerks and administrators that spent years lobbying for exactly this kind of reform. Now that they got it, they're on record saying the January 2027 deadline for the privacy assessments "is too short a timeframe for municipalities to address new requirements and seek new resources." The people who asked for the law are warning the law's own timeline might not work.

One more thing worth knowing: this is a different law from the cybersecurity one the province passed in 2024. That one — Bill 194 — still doesn't apply to Toronto or any other municipality directly. As of today, it only covers hospitals, colleges, universities, school boards, and children's aid societies. If you assumed "the province's new digital security law" covers the City, it doesn't, not yet.

What the City already built on its own

Since June 2025, the City has had a real, specific policy on AI tools — not a vague statement of principles. Staff can use exactly one AI chat tool, Microsoft's, built into the software the City already runs. Nothing else is allowed for City work. And the policy spells out, in plain language, how something that looks anonymous can stop being anonymous: if you feed an AI tool a spreadsheet of service complaints sorted by postal code and date, then ask it to summarize complaints about one specific address, it can piece together who that is — even if you never typed a name. That's a real, specific risk, explained in a real, specific way, in a document the City actually wrote.

The argument that still isn't settled

Toronto tried to build a "smart city" on its waterfront once, with a Google-affiliated company called Sidewalk Labs. It fell apart in 2020. Before it did, the province's top privacy watchdog at the time — a woman who'd been hired by Sidewalk Labs as their own privacy consultant — quit, publicly, because the company wouldn't guarantee that everyone touching the project's data would strip out identifying details the way the company itself had promised to. She wrote, "I imagined us creating a Smart City of Privacy, as opposed to a Smart City of Surveillance."

Separately, Ontario's Auditor General later found that Sidewalk Labs got more information ahead of the official bidding process than the other companies competing for it. Waterfront Toronto — the public body that ran that process — disputed it, hired a retired judge to review the finding, and that judge concluded nobody got anything that wasn't already public. Both of those things are on the record. Nobody has resolved which one is right, and neither should we pretend to here.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Make sure the City is actually using the checklist that already exists for the new privacy assessments (a recommendation card, revised after we double-checked this ourselves). An earlier version of this brief said no such checklist existed anywhere and suggested the City borrow one from Europe's privacy law from scratch. That was wrong — Ontario's own privacy watchdog, the IPC, already publishes a real, detailed privacy-impact-assessment guide that explicitly covers the City. It's being updated right now to match the new rules, but it isn't a blank page. The real question is narrower than "build a checklist": has the City adopted the one that already exists, and will the province finish updating it before the deadline. Europe's own privacy-law checklist is still useful — as a second opinion to check the Ontario one is just as rigorous, not as the only option on the table.

2. Publish which AI tools the City has actually looked at (a recommendation card). The City already evaluates AI tools behind the scenes and can block ones it decides are too risky. Right now, the public only sees the one tool that got approved. Publishing the list — approved, denied, and roughly why — costs almost nothing, because the evaluation work is already happening. It just isn't visible yet.

What this doesn't settle

Neither card resolves whether the City can actually meet the province's January 2027 deadline — that's a resourcing question for the City and the Province to work out, not something a checklist or a published list fixes by itself. Neither card resolves the Sidewalk Labs dispute — that's a factual disagreement between an official audit and a commissioned rebuttal, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. What both cards do is close two specific, real gaps: whether the City has actually adopted the privacy-paperwork standard the province's own watchdog already publishes, and no public visibility into a governance process the City says is already running.
