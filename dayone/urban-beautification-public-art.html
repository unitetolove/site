Urban beautification and public art — Day-One Brief

DRAFT

Status: DRAFT · v1.0 · 2026-07-14 · Built from: our research file for that page + that page's recommendation cards only — no new claims introduced here. Provenance: NEW (inherited from the backgrounder/cards, no formally registered claims minted for this page).

A mural goes up on a blank wall, and the story everyone tells is the same one: it makes the block prettier, it brings people out, and it makes the street safer. Two of those three turn out to be true.

What the research actually found

Toronto pays for public art two very different ways. The bigger one is the Percent for Public Art Program: when a private developer builds something significant, the City works with them to fund public art either on the new site or pooled into a citywide reserve fund, overseen by a citizen panel called the Toronto Public Art Commission. The smaller one is StreetARToronto — StART for short — a family of grant programs that fund murals directly, from a few thousand dollars for a property owner covering paint and supplies up to $70,000 for the largest community-partnership projects. One of StART's own 2026 programs, "Just Us," explicitly builds in geographic fairness: up to six projects, "distributed equitably across the city in Scarborough, Etobicoke, North York and Downtown." The bigger program has no such equity mechanism built in — by its own design, it funds art wherever private development happens to be occurring, not evenly across the city.

That structural difference matters, but it's not the headline. The headline is what this research found when it went looking for the actual study behind the most common argument for murals: that they don't just look nice, they make streets safer.

The claim that didn't hold up

There's a real, peer-reviewed study behind this — a University of Cincinnati research team, led by urban-design professor Hyesun Jeong, studied the city's own several hundred murals using foot-traffic, crime, and demographic data, published in the journal Cities in 2025. Part of what it found is genuinely solid: "even after accounting for demographic and density factors, murals are significantly linked to increased pedestrian activity." Murals really do bring people outside. That part checks out.

But the crime-reduction story — the one built on Jane Jacobs's old "eyes on the street" idea, that more people around means less crime — doesn't survive contact with the same study. The researchers tested it directly, and their own conclusion is unambiguous: "The study found no statistically significant relationship between murals and either violent or nonviolent crime rates." Not a small effect. Not inconclusive. No relationship, in either direction, for either kind of crime. A document that had cited this exact study as evidence that public art fights crime was, on its own terms, citing research that says the opposite of what it claimed.

That's worth sitting with for a second, because it's not a minor footnote — it's the difference between "here's data showing murals make streets safer" and "here's data showing murals bring people outside, and separately, no one has found a safety effect one way or the other." Both can be true statements about murals. Only one of them is what the data supports.

The part that cuts the other way, too

The same Cincinnati study found something else, and it's not flattering to a simple "murals are good, full stop" story either: neighbourhoods with murals saw faster increases in income, rent, and home values between 2010 and 2020 than neighbourhoods without them. The researchers themselves don't dodge this — their own response, per the university's release, is that "as neighborhoods revitalize, issues like rising property values and displacement may follow," and they suggest specific strategies for "balancing cultural development and affordable housing." Whether murals cause that faster appreciation, or just tend to get painted in neighbourhoods that were already gentrifying, isn't something this one study — or this research — can settle. The same release notes murals "cluster near vibrant urban features" and are "rarely found near vacant lots," which cuts toward the second explanation, but doesn't rule out the first. That's an honest, unresolved question, not a verdict either way.

What this research didn't get to

Some real gaps, stated plainly rather than papered over. Nobody has done a Cincinnati-style study of Toronto's own murals — so whether StART actually produces the same foot-traffic bump, whether it has any measurable safety effect, and whether it's nudging property values the same way, is simply unknown here. The City's own promotional language claims StART "reduce[s] overall infrastructure maintenance costs," but that's presented as an aim, not something anyone has actually measured. And there's no clean public number for how much money the bigger program — Percent for Public Art — actually moves in a given year, separate from the broader pool of development-linked community-benefit funding it sits inside; that specific figure wasn't found this review, and is worth someone actually looking for.

Two things worth doing

Fix the record before it spreads further. If any current City messaging repeats the "murals reduce crime" claim, it should be corrected to say what the research actually shows: a real foot-traffic benefit, and no confirmed crime effect either way. This costs nothing beyond a communications team's time, and it's the kind of small, honest correction that protects a program's credibility rather than exposing it — quietly citing a study that says the opposite of what you're claiming is a worse look, found later, than fixing it now.

Actually measure what StART does here, the way Cincinnati measured what its own murals did. The Cincinnati study is a genuinely replicable model: a local university, partly grant-funded, studying an existing mural program with data the city already had access to. Toronto has universities with urban-planning departments and a mural program that's been running since 2012 — nobody has yet put those two things together to find out whether StART's own stated goals are actually happening, or just stated.

Where this comes from, and where it doesn't yet reach

Every figure above traces to sources fetched live on 2026-07-14: the City of Toronto's own Percent for Public Art and StreetARToronto webpages, and the University of Cincinnati's own July 2025 release describing the peer-reviewed Cities journal study — not a summary of the study, the university's own direct account of its findings. What isn't here yet: the full peer-reviewed academic paper itself (only the university's release describing it was fetched), a primary source for StART's individual funding-tier dollar amounts beyond search-engine synthesis, and any Toronto-specific version of the Cincinnati evaluation, which as far as this research could tell, doesn't exist yet.
