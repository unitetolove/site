The Bill Nobody's Actually Added Up — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/homelessness-full-cost-accounting.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/homelessness-full-cost-accounting.md, research/cards/homelessness-full-cost-accounting.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Every shelter budget debate in Toronto happens as if the shelter budget is the whole cost of homelessness. It isn't, and the gap between what shows up in that one budget line and what actually gets spent, across hospitals, courts, correctional facilities, and now-confirmed private-security contracts, is the subject of this brief. So is a second, less comfortable finding: several of the numbers this kind of argument usually leans on are less solid than they look, once you check where they actually came from.

What's real, and newly confirmed

Start with what this week's research could nail down directly. Toronto currently has $109 million in private-security contracts running through 2026 and 2027, concentrated almost entirely on shelters and encampments — $71.7 million of it specifically tied to shelter respite centres, shelter core sites, and one named encampment-support contractor. The city has already spent $35.2 million of it this year. That's not a one-time crisis cost; it's a standing, multi-year commitment, confirmed by the City's own data.

Second: Canada's own federal cost-benefit methodology — the same tool used to decide whether a new safety regulation is worth its cost — puts a dollar figure on a human life: approximately $9.8 million in today's terms. Apply that, honestly, to the 59 shelter-resident deaths Toronto's own system recorded in a single year, and you get $578 million. Not a projection. Not an estimate this brief invented. The government's own number, applied to the government's own count, for one year.

Third: Canada's national health data agency found that a hospital stay for someone experiencing homelessness costs more than double the average — $16,785 versus $7,803 — driven by the fact that 93% of these patients arrive through the emergency room, not a family doctor's office. That's a national figure, confirmed directly this week from the source.

What's less solid than it looks

Here's the part that matters just as much: two of the figures this kind of case usually leans hardest on turned out to be shakier than expected when checked directly.

Ontario's own website for provincial correctional per-diem costs — the number behind claims like "it costs $326 a day to incarcerate someone" — currently says flatly that the data "is not available right now" while the province reviews whether to release it. The $326 figure floating around isn't from that dataset. It's the Canada-wide average, from Statistics Canada, for 2022. Whether Ontario's actual number matches it, runs higher, or runs lower is genuinely unknown right now, because Ontario itself won't say.

And that $16,785 hospitalization figure — real, current, directly sourced — is a national number. Nobody has published the Ontario-specific or Toronto-specific version. It's a reasonable proxy. It is not the same claim as "Toronto's homeless population costs the health system this much," and this brief isn't going to blur that distinction just because the national number is more dramatic.

Why this matters more than it might seem

A case built on inflated precision is a case that collapses the first time someone checks the footnote. The strongest version of this argument doesn't need borrowed precision — the real, confirmed numbers (the $109 million security portfolio, the $578 million mortality cost, the $3.1 billion province-wide social-services funding gap the Financial Accountability Office itself projects by 2027-28) are already large enough to make the point. Padding them with unconfirmed Ontario-specific figures that turn out to be national averages wearing a local label doesn't make the case stronger. It gives a skeptical reader a free win.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't produce a single "true cost of homelessness" grand total — the promoted research this leaf draws from explicitly declines to invent one, and this brief agrees with that discipline. Legal liability exposure (a live $50 million class action, an unresolved lawsuit over a 2021 encampment clearance) is real and documented but isn't summed into anything, because doing so would manufacture a false precision. Education costs and child-welfare costs tied to family homelessness remain completely unquantified in anything found this week — a real gap, stated as one, not papered over with a guess.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Get a real, government-published, jurisdictionally-precise cost picture (RC-HFC-01) — close the exact gap this brief just spent five paragraphs describing, using methodologies (Treasury Board's VSL, CIHI's own coding, the FAO's own projections) that already exist and already belong to government bodies, not this project. 2. Do the mortality math properly (RC-HFC-02) — replace the current illustrative, single-rate calculation with a year-appropriate, discounted one, so the strongest number in this whole leaf stops carrying an asterisk.

Neither of these requires a new argument. They require finishing the math the government's own tools already make possible, and being honest about which parts of that math aren't finished yet.
