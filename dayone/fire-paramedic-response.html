Fire and Paramedic Response Times and Coverage — Day-One Paper

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/fire-paramedic-response.md

DRAFT v1.0 — L-PLATFORM layer · File: research/dayone/fire-paramedic-response.md · Date: 2026-07-14 Built only from: research/backgrounders/fire-paramedic-response.md and research/cards/fire-paramedic-response.md. No claim in this document goes beyond what those two files already establish. Trust classes: NEW, as carried from the backgrounder/cards; see those documents for full citations.

The case

Toronto's own Auditor General found that staffed-ambulance availability collapsed between 2019 and 2023: episodes with zero ambulances available anywhere in the city rose from 29 to over 1,200 — a more than 40-fold increase — and response times for the most life-threatening calls (sudden cardiac arrest, highest-acuity patients) got slower even while overall targets were still met about 80% of the time [backgrounder, "Toronto Paramedic Services: the Auditor General's 2024 findings"]. The primary driver, per the same audit, is hospital offload delay: paramedics spent 60% of their total service time waiting inside hospitals in 2023, with 42% of cases taking over 30 minutes to hand a patient off. Toronto Fire Services shows a parallel pattern on its own self-adopted standard: 90th-percentile travel time worsened from 5:25 to 6:06 between 2020 and 2024, missing its own 4-minute target more often each year [backgrounder, "Toronto Fire Services: response times against the NFPA benchmark"].

The honest catch

Both governments have already responded, and their own numbers do not yet fully agree with each other. The City announced a $52 million, 362-position Multi-Year Staffing Plan in June 2025, cost-shared with the Province, targeting up to 20% better ambulance availability by 2028. The Province separately reports a 65% reduction in offload time province-wide since an October 2022 peak, backed by close to $1 billion in 2025 land-ambulance funding and a Dedicated Offload Nurses Program covering roughly 800,000 hours. But that 65%-improvement claim comes from government communications, not an independently audited dataset — and it sits on a timeline that overlaps with the City's own June 2024 audit, which used full-year 2023 data and still found the offload problem driving declining availability [backgrounder, "The provincial and municipal response"; "Key tensions / tradeoffs"]. This document states both claims rather than picking one: it is possible both are true of different things — a genuine province-wide trend, and one city's specific 2023 snapshot — but nothing in the available sourcing reconciles them directly.

There is also a real data gap on equity. The Auditor General found that response times "varied by geographic area and are not monitored," and where informally checked, were slower for high-priority calls outside downtown Toronto [backgrounder, "Toronto Paramedic Services: the Auditor General's 2024 findings"]. Whether the same pattern holds for fire response specifically was not confirmed either way in this research pass — no comparable independent fire-service coverage audit was located.

Two places to start

Fund the paramedic staffing plan through to 2028, and start reporting response times by neighbourhood. The City has already committed to this — the ask here is completion, not a new program: $52 million and 362 positions (331 paramedics) already approved and partly cost-shared with the Province, targeting up to 20% better ambulance availability. Add to it what the Auditor General already recommended and the City has not yet delivered: routine public reporting of response times broken out by geographic area, so the downtown/non-downtown gap the audit flagged becomes a tracked number instead of a one-time finding [card RC-FP-01].

Give Toronto Fire Services the same independent scrutiny paramedics already got. TFS measures itself against a voluntary NFPA standard it has been missing more each year since 2020, with no equivalent to the Auditor General audit that drove the paramedic staffing plan. Formalize that standard as a Council-adopted target and direct an Auditor General coverage review — including the same geographic breakdown the paramedic audit called for — so any fire-service equity gap gets found and measured rather than assumed either way [card RC-FP-02].

What's not yet settled

Neither card resolves the central open question: whether the paramedic staffing plan and offload-nurse investments, both still mid-implementation, will actually close the gap the 2023 audit data describes, or whether the province's own claimed 65% improvement already means the picture has changed materially since that audit was written. Whether Toronto Fire Services carries the same downtown/non-downtown equity gap the paramedic audit found is a genuinely open, untested question — this document does not assume it does. No accountability-ledger finding connects a specific named entity to profiting from either service's response-time shortfalls; both are directly city-delivered services, and the backgrounder's own Cui Bono search came up empty rather than forcing a beneficiary into the picture [backgrounder, "Cui Bono"].
