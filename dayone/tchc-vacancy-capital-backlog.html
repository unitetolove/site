TCHC's Two Numbers: 98.5% Full, $2.2 Billion Behind — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 · File: research/dayone/tchc-vacancy-capital-backlog.md

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: research/backgrounders/tchc-vacancy-capital-backlog.md, research/cards/tchc-vacancy-capital-backlog.md only — no fresh citations introduced here.

Toronto Community Housing is, on the numbers it just published in its own 2026 budget, doing better at one job and worse at the other. It is filling units faster than its own target requires. It is falling further behind on keeping those buildings in repair, and the federal money that pays for over half that repair work runs out in two years.

The good number: 98.5%

TCHC's own Service Manager sets a 98% occupancy target. As of September 2025, TCHC hit 98.5% — and had been above target for a full year running. That is a genuinely different picture than the one this library's own evidence has carried since 2019, when the City's Auditor General found an average 1,400 rent-geared-to-income units sitting empty system-wide and $7 million a year lost to vacancy. TCHC's own report credits a specific set of fixes: streamlined processes, better tracking of units taken permanently out of circulation, and an improved move-out program to speed up unit turnover.

Here's the catch nobody has checked: no Auditor General follow-up has reviewed those specific 2019 recommendations since they were made. The 2025 follow-up cycle audited two entirely different reports. So the honest claim is narrower than "TCHC fixed the problem the 2019 audit found" — it's "TCHC's occupancy rate is now strong, and nobody has independently confirmed why."

The bad number: $2.2 billion, heading to $4.8 billion

TCHC's capital repair backlog — the cost of bringing its aging buildings up to a state of good repair — is $2.229 billion this year, up $432 million from last year. The City's own 10-year projection has it nearly tripling, to $4.799 billion by 2035, even with steady annual investment running roughly $110-270 million a year. As a share of what TCHC's entire portfolio is worth, the backlog grows from 13% today to nearly 20% by 2035. That's not a projection of decline — it's the City's own stated plan, math it has already run and published.

Layer onto that a hard deadline: over half of TCHC's annual capital repair money — the federal Co-Investment program — is confirmed to stop after 2027. TCHC's own budget document says plainly what happens next: unfunded capital repair starting in 2028. No replacement federal program has been announced.

Why both numbers matter together, not separately

A vacant unit and a unit that needs $50,000 of repair before anyone can move in are not the same problem, but they show up in the same place: an empty apartment that could house someone. TCHC's occupancy figure is genuinely strong. It does not tell you how many of the remaining roughly 1.5% of units are vacant because nobody's been matched to them yet, versus vacant because they're not safe to live in until capital money arrives that, on current federal commitments, may simply not exist after 2027. Nobody has published that breakdown. This brief isn't going to pretend the 98.5% figure answers a question it wasn't built to answer.

What this doesn't settle

This doesn't confirm whether TCHC's occupancy improvement is durable or attributable to the 2019 audit's recommendations specifically — that requires an Auditor General follow-up that, on the evidence gathered here, has never happened. It doesn't reconcile a real discrepancy between two figures for the same George Street Revitalization project ($610.9 million here, $555.8 million in this library's shelter-capacity brief) — both are stated, neither is picked. And it doesn't know whether federal officials are already negotiating a Co-Investment Fund replacement; that wasn't checked this pass, and it's exactly the kind of thing that could make this whole section moot in either direction.

The two moves this leaf's cards propose

1. Ask the federal government now, ahead of the 2027 cliff, to confirm a Co-Investment Fund replacement — sized to at least the 52% share it currently provides, not a smaller renewal that quietly widens the gap (RC-TCHC-01). 2. Ask the Auditor General to actually check the 2019 vacancy recommendations — seven years on, nobody has independently confirmed whether the fixes TCHC credits are the reason occupancy improved, or whether something else is doing the work (RC-TCHC-02).

Neither of these invents a new number or a new program. Both ask someone who already has the authority to check something TCHC's own budget documents have already surfaced, and nobody has yet finished checking.
