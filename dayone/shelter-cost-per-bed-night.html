What a Bed Actually Costs — Day-One Brief

DRAFT Date: 2026-07-14 ·

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Layer: L-PLATFORM Sources: our research file for that page, that page's recommendation cards only — no fresh citations introduced here.

A per-bed-night cost figure sounds like a simple number. It isn't. This brief walks through what Toronto's own numbers actually say, why the obvious comparison (2010 to today) is more complicated than it looks, and where the trail genuinely runs out.

The headline, stated carefully

Toronto's system-wide average per diem rate rose from $52.13 in 2010 to $136 in 2023 — a 161% increase in raw dollars, about 96% after adjusting for inflation. That's a real, defensible, apples-to-apples comparison: both are system-wide averages across all contracted operators, not one shelter's rate dressed up as the whole system's. Two things explain part of that growth honestly: the system got about 114-125% bigger over roughly the same span, and the average length of a shelter stay has been getting longer every year since the City started tracking turnover in 2011. Both are real. Neither is spin.

But here's the part that doesn't get said often enough: even counting both of those, the City's own underlying research says outright that they don't fully explain the growth. There's a residual — real, acknowledged, and not yet broken down into what it actually is. Is it staffing costs rising faster than inflation? Richer wraparound services bundled into a "bed-night" now that weren't there in 2010? Something else? Nobody has done that math yet. That's not a gap this brief is inventing to sound rigorous — it's the City's own research naming its own biggest unanswered question.

The number that actually explains the capital strategy

Cost per bed-night isn't one number — it's at least three, depending on what kind of shelter you're talking about. A base shelter runs about $136 a night. A new, purpose-built shelter runs about $126. A temporary hotel room runs $253 — nearly double. That $127 gap is not incidental; it's the literal, stated math behind the City's decision to spend $674.5 million building 20 new purpose-built shelters instead of leaning on hotels indefinitely. Thirteen of those twenty sites are funded. Seven aren't — a $166.9 million gap sitting on a savings case the City itself already made the arithmetic for.

Two operators, computed independently, same shock

Nobody — not Homes First, not Dixon Hall — publishes a cost-per-bed-night figure directly. This project computed both, combining each organization's own audited statements with the City's own daily occupancy data. Both show the same shape: a COVID-era spike, then a partial recovery. Homes First peaked at $249.65 in 2020; Dixon Hall peaked a year later, at $313.23, consistent with its different (April-to-March) fiscal year catching a later slice of the same disruption. That's not two organizations behaving differently — it's the same shock landing on two different calendars.

What's genuinely different: Homes First's cost kept falling after the spike, down to $104.53 by 2023, before a small uptick. Dixon Hall's fell too, then turned around and climbed again — up to $210.36 by 2024. Why one operator stabilized and the other didn't isn't answered anywhere in the record this project has access to. That's stated as an open question because it is one, not because a firmer answer was inconvenient.

The audit finding that changes how you should read every "reported" figure

The 2025 Auditor General's own numbers for winter shelter programs show something that looks, at first glance, like cost overruns: reported per-bed-night costs consistently higher than what was budgeted — sometimes by a wide margin (a Warming Centre budgeted at $266-323/night reported out at $336-491). But the AG's own table explains why: budgeted cost assumes a site runs at full capacity every night; reported cost is calculated from what actually happened. A site that runs below capacity will always show a higher reported per-bed-night cost, for the boring reason that fixed costs get divided across fewer nights — not because anyone spent more than planned. Anyone reading a "reported cost" figure without checking occupancy is at real risk of mistaking an occupancy problem for a spending problem.

The transparency gap that's actually simple to fix

The City published a full, itemized, shelter-by-shelter rate schedule in 2010 — every operator, every site, down to the dollar. It hasn't published anything that detailed since. That's not this project failing to find a current schedule; a dedicated search came up empty, and the most plausible explanation is that the City simply stopped publishing at this granularity. Unlike the cost-growth residual above, this one has a clean, concrete fix: publish the schedule again, in the same format. The City already has the data — it administers these contracts. The only missing piece is a decision to disclose.

The two moves this page’s cards propose

1. Publish a current, itemized per-operator per diem schedule (a recommendation card) — the same format the City already produced once, in 2010, and hasn't repeated since. 2. Actually disaggregate the unexplained cost-growth residual before the ~96% real-growth figure gets cited again anywhere (a recommendation card) — an internal research-discipline ask, not a City action, aimed at this project's own future honesty.

A third card (a recommendation card) adds this page’s own independently-computed cost evidence to the sibling capacity-strain page’s existing ask to fund the seven remaining purpose-built shelter sites — not a new ask, a second, independent confirmation of the same one.
