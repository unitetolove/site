The Beautify playbook — KM CLEAN

Visible public work is the fastest honest way to demonstrate that you can convene strangers into coordinated action — the core skill of governing. This playbook offers every candidate and team in Ontario’s 2026 municipal elections, identically, a workflow that costs almost nothing, a metric that can’t be faked, and a receipt standard that lets anyone verify it. It’s a suggestion, free to take: your crew, your name, your receipts, your credit — nothing here to join and nothing to submit.

Part of Show Your Work — two ideas for every candidate. This page suggests; it never convenes — every round described here is run by a campaign or crew under its own name.

The headline metric: KM CLEAN

Kilometres of sidewalk cleaned. One number, additive, route-verifiable, comparable across every ward and all 444 municipalities. A kilometre cleaned in Kenora counts exactly like a kilometre cleaned in Scarborough. It reads as what it is: care for a shared commons, one stretch at a time.

Three tiers, so the metric stays honest about what was actually done:

  • CLEANED — litter and cigarette butts off the route. The true near-costless floor: a crew, gloves, tongs or grabbers, and bags. This is the tier every campaign can reach this week.
  • WASHED — the same route power-washed or scrubbed. A labeled bonus tier, not costless: equipment rental, water access, and — read the safety layer below — real municipal rules about what may go into a storm drain. Claim it only when you actually did it.
  • TRANSFORMED — paint, planting, signage repair, an eyesore fixed. Entirely consent-gated: property-owner permission and any required municipal permit come before the brush comes out. See “the one rule” below.

A route can claim more than one tier — report each tier’s kilometres separately. Never add tiers together into one number: 2 km cleaned + 1 km washed is reported as exactly that, not as “3 km.”

The receipt standard — what makes a round count

A round without receipts is a story. A round with receipts is a demonstration. For each round, publish on your own site or page:

  • Date, municipality, and route — start point and end point, named plainly (streets or intersections), and the distance claimed.
  • Photos along the route — before and after, every few hundred metres, so the route is verifiable end to end, not just at its best corner.
  • What came off the route — bags filled, and kilograms if you can weigh them.
  • The butt count — cigarette butts collected, logged per route segment. This is the sub-receipt that feeds the one-year grand challenge below; one walk produces all three numbers (km, kg, butts).
  • Crew size — how many people showed up. Convening is the thing being demonstrated; count it.
  • The tier claimed — cleaned, washed, or transformed, per the definitions above.

Count at the time, on the route — never reconstruct numbers afterward. If a number wasn’t counted, publish “not counted” rather than an estimate dressed as a count. An honest gap outranks a round number. If a photo shows a person under 18, get a parent or guardian’s okay before it goes online — or frame the shot so no child is identifiable.

Repeatability is the real demonstration. One round proves a weekend; a cadence of rounds — same metric, growing crews, receipts every time — proves you can convene strangers into coordinated action again and again. That is the capacity governing actually takes.

The safety and consent layer — non-negotiable

This section is not fine print. A campaign that skips it is not demonstrating capacity; it is demonstrating carelessness with volunteers. Every rule here rides every round.

Sharps — the rule that protects your crew

  • Cigarette butts co-occur with needles. This is Ontario; say it plainly at every crew briefing.
  • Nobody picks up anything by bare hand. Gloves and tongs or grabbers, every person, every round.
  • Needles and other sharps are never collected by your crew’s bags. If your municipality offers a sharps-disposal or 311-type reporting channel, use it; many public health units and some pharmacies accept sharps in approved containers. Know your local channel before the round starts.
  • No minors on any site where sharps are found or expected. If a route turns up sharps, anyone under 18 moves to a different segment, immediately and without exception. Plan family-friendly segments (parks fronting main streets, BIA blocks) apart from segments where sharps are likely.
  • Hi-vis for anyone working near a curb; the crew lead walks the route once before the crew does.

Disposal — finish the job

  • Collected bags go where your municipality says they go — a round that ends at an overflowing public bin has moved litter, not removed it. Ask your municipality’s waste line or 311-equivalent where a community-cleanup’s bags should land; many municipalities run cleanup programs that will collect bags from a named point.

Washing — the tier with real rules

  • Power-washing takes equipment, a water source you have permission to use, and attention to runoff: in many municipalities, sewer-use rules restrict what may enter a storm drain, and wash water carrying detergent can violate them. Default to water-only. Use detergent only after confirming your municipality’s sewer-use by-law allows what you plan to do — ask the municipality, not this page.
  • Washing a public sidewalk in front of a consenting shop is the easy case; washing municipal property at scale may need permission. When unsure, ask first. The answer is a receipt too — publish it.

The one rule everything else is a footnote to

Beautify only what you have the right to beautify. Litter off a public sidewalk needs no one’s permission. Paint on a wall needs the owner’s consent — written is better — and, on public property, the municipality’s. A mural nobody asked for is vandalism with good intentions, and it loses the argument this whole program exists to make. This is the same ethic as our postering rule: post only where you have the right to post — beautify only what you have the right to beautify.

EVERY BUTT — the bigger idea, for whoever wants it

The butt count inside a KM CLEAN receipt is more than a footnote — it’s the seed of a year-scale idea any campaign, community group, or council could pick up: a jurisdiction cleared of cigarette butts within a year, by people and technology working together, measured entirely by receipts. The year-scale number is a target — labeled a target, never claimed as a count — because butts re-deposit daily and honesty about that is the whole point: eradication measured by receipts, never a claim of “done.”

A campaign that starts counting now is building the opening baseline of whatever its community later chooses to run with. No campaign should promise to finish anything by voting day — “every butt by October” is a claim nobody can verify and nobody should make. What a campaign can demonstrate by October is the thing that matters: crews that show up, rounds that repeat, receipts that hold up.

Publish your receipts

Your rounds belong on your own page, under your own name — dated, photographed, counted at the time, standing for the whole term you’re asking to serve. That’s the entire mechanism: receipts your voters and your local press can check with their own eyes. Nothing flows back to us — no submissions, no registry, no scoreboard here. Comparing candidates is the public’s seat, and the public is welcome to it.
