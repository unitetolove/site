So you’re thinking of running

A practical guide for anyone weighing a run for Mayor, City Councillor, or School Board Trustee in Toronto’s 2026 municipal election. Plain language, every fact sourced. This page does not endorse, favour, or recruit for any candidate, party, or side — the information below is identical for everyone who reads it.

The deadline, up top

You have until August 21, 2026, at 2:00 p.m. to file your nomination and become an official candidate for Mayor, City Councillor, or School Board Trustee in Toronto. The window to file opened May 1, 2026, at 8:30 a.m. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”]

After that time, on that day, the door closes. If you’re on the fence, this is the fact that matters most: the clock is real, and it is short.

How to register

Becoming a candidate is a single in-person filing, not a long bureaucratic process. Here is exactly what it takes.

1. Decide your office

You can run for Mayor, City Councillor (one of Toronto’s 25 wards), or School Board Trustee (Toronto District School Board, Toronto Catholic District School Board, Conseil scolaire Viamonde, or Conseil scolaire catholique MonAvenir). Note: as of April 2026, the province cut the number of Toronto District School Board wards from 22 to 12 — check the current trustee ward map before you file. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”]

2. Check you’re eligible

To run for Mayor or Councillor, you must be:

  • A Canadian citizen
  • At least 18 years old
  • A resident of Toronto, OR an owner/tenant of land in Toronto, or the spouse of one
  • Not legally prohibited from voting
  • Not disqualified by legislation from holding municipal office (this rules out sitting judges and sitting provincial/federal legislators who haven’t resigned by close of nominations, among a short list of others)

To run for School Board Trustee, the requirements are the same except residency must be within that school board’s jurisdiction. City of Toronto employees and school board employees must take an unpaid leave of absence before filing. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”]

3. Gather your documents and, for Mayor/Council, your 25 signatures

You’ll need:

  • A completed nomination paper
  • Acceptable identification proving your name, signature, and qualifying Toronto address (a list of accepted ID combinations is on the city’s site)
  • At least 25 original endorsement signatures from eligible Toronto voters — required for Mayor and Councillor candidates only. School Board Trustee candidates do not need endorsement signatures. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”; Ontario’s 2026 Candidates’ Guide confirms the same 25-signature threshold and the trustee exemption]
  • The filing fee (see below)

All signatures must be original — photocopies and scans aren’t accepted.

4. Pay the filing fee

  • $200 to run for Mayor
  • $100 to run for Councillor or School Board Trustee

Paid by cash, credit or debit card, certified cheque, or money order made out to “Treasurer, City of Toronto.” This fee is refunded after you file the required post-election financial statement — it is a deposit, not a permanent cost. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”; Ontario’s 2026 Candidates’ Guide confirms the same $200/$100 figures and the refund-on-filing rule]

5. File in person

Nominations are filed at the Toronto Elections Office, City Hall, 100 Queen St. West, 1st floor (Permit Alley), Monday to Friday, 8:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. (on the final day, August 21, filing hours are 9:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m.). An appointment-booking system is available and recommended to reduce wait times; walk-ins are also accepted (limit four guests per candidate). An agent may file on your behalf with their own ID plus your documents. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”]

You cannot raise or spend any campaign money until your nomination paper is filed. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”]

What running actually costs

The filing fee ($100–$200, refundable) is the smallest part of the honest answer. Here is the fuller picture, as plainly as we can put it:

  • Time. A serious campaign runs for weeks to months of unpaid work — evenings, weekends, and often full days, on top of whatever job or care responsibilities you already carry.
  • Money beyond the filing fee. Printing, lawn signs, transportation, a website, and basic outreach materials all cost real money that the filing fee does not cover. Individual campaign contributions are capped by law — a maximum of $1,200 to a candidate for Councillor or School Board Trustee and $2,500 to a candidate for Mayor, per contributor — and corporations and trade unions cannot contribute at all. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”]
  • Paperwork. Every candidate must open a dedicated campaign bank account (if raising or spending any money), track every contribution and expense, and file a full financial statement after the election — win, lose, or withdraw. Missing that statement’s deadline means forfeiting the filing-fee refund and, in some cases, being placed “in default” and barred from running again until after the following election. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”]
  • Public exposure. Once you file, your name, and the fact of your candidacy, is a public record. Campaigning means putting yourself, and often your family, in front of the public eye for months.

None of this is stated to discourage anyone — it is stated because a decision made with the real costs in view is a stronger decision than one made without them. See the honest paragraph below on why this matters more than it should.

What you get free and identically — from the library

Whatever you decide, one thing is already true: the homework is done, and it’s free, for every candidate in this election identically. No candidate gets a head start and no candidate is charged. What’s available:

  • The backgrounders. Researched, sourced issue briefs across Toronto’s major policy areas — housing, transit, safety, climate, health, cost of living, work and the economy, and more — so you don’t have to build a research department from your kitchen table to understand what you’d be governing.
  • The citizen question bank. A published, sourced set of 66 real questions Torontonians are asking, organized into 12 category banks matching the issues people actually care about. Every candidate faces the same questions, on the same terms — no gotchas, no favourites.
  • Day-one package scaffolding. Worked examples and drafting tools for the single scariest question any candidate faces — what would you actually do in office? — so that publishing a specific, dated first-weeks plan is something any candidate can do, not just the ones with paid staff.

All three are offered to every registrant in every race — incumbent or first-time challenger, whichever office, whichever ward — on identical terms. Nothing here endorses, ranks, or favours anyone.

Why good people don’t run — and what this lowers

Nominations close four weeks from a standing start, and every extraordinary Torontonian who might serve is on that same short clock. It’s worth asking honestly why so many never even consider it. Not because they don’t care. Because the price is real, and everyone can see it:

  • The scrutiny lands on your family, not just on you. A candidate’s spouse, children, and past become public property overnight, and transparency can curdle into harassment of the people running, not just criticism of their ideas.
  • The income cut and the career risk are real. Running seriously can mean months of unpaid work, and losing means explaining a public defeat to every future employer. People with mortgages, caregiving duties, or precarious status — which is to say, most of the city — are structurally filtered out by this alone.
  • Incumbents and established networks hold real advantages. Municipal races are formally non-partisan, but name recognition, donor networks, and organizing machinery give incumbents and well-connected candidates a real head start that a first-time candidate faces alone.
  • Many races look “already decided” before they start. When a handful of races get all the attention, every other potential candidate hears the same question: why bother? — and even people who would make excellent representatives conclude the outcome isn’t really in question.

This kit exists because each of those has an answer. The homework — backgrounders, question bank, day-one scaffolding — is done and free, so no candidate starts from zero. The published, identical question bank replaces “gotcha” moments with the city’s real questions, asked the same way of everyone. None of this changes the real costs of running listed above. It is offered to lower the unnecessary costs — the ones that come from having to build everything alone — so that the decision to run can rest on whether you want to serve, not on whether you can afford a research team.

Where to send someone you trust

You don’t have to be the one who runs for this to matter. If you know someone — a neighbour, a colleague, someone in your community — who you think would serve Toronto well and has never been asked: tell them. Naming someone you trust and helping bring them the resources above (the backgrounders, the question bank, the day-one scaffolding) is one of the most concrete things anyone can do in the four weeks before nominations close.

This library recruits and endorses no one, and serves every candidate identically, regardless of who wins. Its only interest is a wider, better-informed field, chosen by Torontonians.
