So you’re thinking of running

A practical guide for anyone weighing a run for Mayor, Head of Council, Councillor, or School Board Trustee in any of Ontario’s 444 municipalities holding a 2026 election — not just Toronto. Plain language, every fact sourced. This page does not endorse, favour, or recruit for any candidate, party, or side — the information below is identical for everyone who reads it, wherever in Ontario they live.

v1.0 · Ontario-wide · independently verified 2026-08-06 — see the note at the foot of this page.

The deadline, up top — same for all of Ontario

You have until August 21, 2026, at 2:00 p.m. to file your nomination and become an official candidate — in every one of Ontario’s 444 municipalities, under the same provincial law (the Municipal Elections Act, 1996). The window to file opened May 1, 2026, at 8:30 a.m. Whichever municipality you live in, the clock is identical.

After 2:00 p.m. on August 21, the door closes everywhere in the province at once. If you’re on the fence, this is the fact that matters most: the clock is real, it is short, and it does not run on a different schedule depending on where you live.

Ontario-wide rules — what the law says, wherever you live

Source for this section: Ontario’s 2026 Candidates’ Guide (“Campaign finance” and “General information” chapters, ontario.ca), the Municipal Elections Act, 1996 itself, and AMO’s Municipal Elections page — all fetched and independently reconfirmed 2026-08-05. Section numbers refer to the Municipal Elections Act, 1996 (MEA).

Who can run

  • A Canadian citizen, at least 18 years old.
  • A resident of the municipality, OR a non-resident owner or tenant of land there, or the spouse of such an owner/tenant.
  • Not legally prohibited from voting, and not disqualified by other legislation from holding municipal office.
  • For School Board Trustee, residency must be within that board’s jurisdiction instead of the municipality.

What it costs to file

  • $100 to run for Councillor or School Board Trustee.
  • $200 to run for Mayor or other Head of Council.
  • Refunded once you file your required post-election financial statement on time — a deposit, not a permanent cost.

The 25-signature rule — with an exemption most Toronto-focused writing misses

Candidates for Mayor/Head of Council and Councillor need at least 25 original endorsement signatures from eligible voters to file. This applies in any municipality with 4,000 or more electors (using the count from that municipality’s previous regular election’s voters’ list); municipalities with fewer than 4,000 electors are exempted from the signature requirement entirely. [Municipal Elections Act, 1996, s.33(1.4)–(1.5), independently confirmed directly against the Act’s own e-Laws text; the “prescribed number” of 4,000 is set by O. Reg. 101/97, s.1.] School Board Trustee candidates never need endorsement signatures, regardless of municipality size.

Your campaign period and bank account

  • Your campaign period begins the day your municipal clerk receives your nomination — you cannot raise or spend a dollar before that.
  • It ends December 31, 2026 for most candidates (exceptions: if you withdrew, if you were never certified, or if you choose to end it early once you know your finances are settled).
  • You must open a campaign bank account exclusively for the campaign if you accept any money or incur any expense — your personal account can never be used, even for a very small campaign.

Money mechanics — the actual formulas, not just headline numbers

  • Self-funding limit (what you and your spouse together may put into your own campaign): Head of Council — $7,500 + $0.20 per eligible elector; Councillor — $5,000 + $0.20 per eligible elector; capped at $25,000 either way. Your clerk tells you your exact number.
  • Contribution limit from any other individual contributor: $1,200. This is the province-wide number for every office — Mayor, Councillor, Trustee, everywhere. The one named exception: contributions to a Mayoral candidate in the City of Toronto specifically are capped at $2,500, not $1,200. Every other head-of-council candidate in Ontario faces the same $1,200 limit as a councillor. No single contributor may give more than $5,000 in total across all campaigns in the same jurisdiction. Only contributions of $25 or less may be made in cash.
  • General spending limit: Head of Council — $7,500 + $0.85 per eligible elector; Councillor or Trustee — $5,000 + $0.85 per eligible elector. Your clerk gives you an estimate at nomination and a final number on or before September 30, 2026.
  • Corporations and trade unions cannot contribute at all, anywhere in Ontario. Volunteer labour is never counted as a contribution.

Your financial statement — the deadline that actually decides your refund

It is your personal responsibility to file a complete, accurate financial statement on time, even if a bookkeeper prepares it. If you filed a nomination, you must file a statement — including if you withdrew, were never certified, or were acclaimed. See the full calendar below for every date attached to this.

How to register — Toronto, as a worked example

The mechanics below are Toronto’s specifically, kept as a fully worked example so you can see exactly what “register” looks like end to end. If you live somewhere else in Ontario, the shape is your template — see “Find your own municipality” immediately after.

1. Decide your office

In Toronto you can run for Mayor, City Councillor (one of Toronto’s 25 wards), or School Board Trustee (Toronto District School Board, Toronto Catholic District School Board, Conseil scolaire Viamonde, or Conseil scolaire catholique MonAvenir). As of April 2026, the province cut the number of Toronto District School Board wards from 22 to 12 — check the current trustee ward map before you file. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”]

2. Gather your documents and, for Mayor/Council, your 25 signatures

A completed nomination paper, acceptable identification, and — because Toronto has well over 4,000 electors — at least 25 original endorsement signatures for Mayor/Councillor candidates (School Board Trustee candidates are exempt everywhere in Ontario, not just Toronto). All signatures must be original.

3. File in person

Nominations are filed at the Toronto Elections Office, City Hall, 100 Queen St. West, 1st floor (Permit Alley), Monday to Friday, 8:30 a.m.–4:30 p.m. (on August 21 itself, filing hours are 9 a.m.–2 p.m., matching the province-wide Nomination Day rule above). An appointment-booking system is available and recommended. An agent may file on your behalf with their own ID plus your documents. [toronto.ca, “Become a Candidate”]

Find your own municipality

Every Ontario municipal clerk runs nomination filing the same way in substance — in person or by an agent, during set office hours, against the same province-wide deadline above — but the office location, exact hours, and any local forms are set by your own clerk, not by this page. Mechanically generated from this project’s own working election dataset; regenerable any time the source data refreshes; some municipalities currently have zero races on file and their pages say so plainly rather than implying nothing’s happening there.
  • AMO’s own directory of every municipality’s 2026 election webpage — a single list covering all 444 Ontario municipalities: amo.on.ca/2026-municipal-election-webpages.
  • Search “[your municipality’s name] clerk 2026 municipal election.”
  • Contact your municipality’s clerk’s office directly — every municipal government maintains one, and Ontario’s 2026 Candidates’ Guide (linked below) is the same document every clerk works from.

What running actually costs

The filing fee ($100–$200, refundable) is the smallest part of the honest answer, wherever you run. The fuller picture, as plainly as we can put it:

  • Time. A serious campaign runs for weeks to months of unpaid work — evenings, weekends, and often full days, on top of whatever job or care responsibilities you already carry.
  • Money beyond the filing fee. Printing, signs, transportation, a website, and basic outreach materials all cost real money the filing fee doesn’t cover. See “Money mechanics” above for the actual contribution and spending-limit formulas that apply to your race.
  • Paperwork. Every candidate everywhere in Ontario must open a dedicated campaign bank account (if raising or spending any money), track every contribution and expense, and file a full financial statement after the election — win, lose, or withdraw. See the calendar above for exactly what missing that deadline costs.
  • Public exposure. Once you file, your name and candidacy are a public record, in every municipality, not just the large ones.

None of this is stated to discourage anyone — a decision made with the real costs in view is a stronger decision than one made without them.

Why good people don’t run — and what this lowers

Nominations close on the same short clock for every extraordinary Ontarian who might serve. It’s worth asking honestly why so many never even consider it. Not because they don’t care. Because the price is real, and everyone can see it:

  • The scrutiny lands on your family, not just on you. A candidate’s spouse, children, and past become public property overnight, and transparency can curdle into harassment of the people running, not just criticism of their ideas.
  • The income cut and the career risk are real. Running seriously can mean months of unpaid work, and losing means explaining a public defeat to every future employer. People with mortgages, caregiving duties, or precarious status — which is to say, most of any community — are structurally filtered out by this alone.
  • Incumbents and established networks hold real advantages. Municipal races are formally non-partisan, but name recognition, donor networks, and organizing machinery give incumbents and well-connected candidates a real head start that a first-time candidate faces alone.
  • Many races look “already decided” before they start. When a handful of races get all the attention, every other potential candidate hears the same question: why bother? — and even people who would make excellent representatives conclude the outcome isn’t really in question.

This kit exists because each of those has an answer. The homework, the guides shelf, the tools stack, and the team-role explainers are done and free, so no candidate starts from zero. None of this changes the real costs of running listed above. It is offered to lower the unnecessary costs — the ones that come from having to build everything alone — so that the decision to run can rest on whether you want to serve, not on whether you can afford a research team.

Where to send someone you trust

You don’t have to be the one who runs for this to matter. If you know someone — a neighbour, a colleague, someone in your community, anywhere in Ontario — who you think would serve well and has never been asked: tell them. Naming someone you trust and helping bring them the resources above is one of the most concrete things anyone can do before nominations close.

This library recruits and endorses no one, and serves every candidate identically, regardless of who wins. Its only interest is a wider, better-informed field, chosen by voters.
