Scope

The neutral scope question this document answers: what does Ontario's mandatory Community Safety and Well-Being (CSWB) planning framework require of municipalities, how has Toronto's own plan (SafeTO) performed against its stated goals in its first implementation phase, and what does the evidence show about the multi-sector, prevention-oriented model these plans are built on? This backgrounder covers: the legislative basis and required structure for CSWB plans in Ontario; SafeTO's Phase 1 (2022-2024) implementation and outcomes as self-reported by the City; the funding structure and its stated gaps; and international/comparator evidence on public-health approaches to violence reduction. It does not cover: policing budgets or Toronto Police Service governance specifically (a distinct, separate issue area), the Toronto Community Crisis Service's operational design in full detail beyond its role as a SafeTO Phase 1 outcome (a program that could support its own dedicated leaf), or bylaw/property-standards enforcement (bylaw-enforcement-property-standards). No claim citations are required in this section — it frames the document rather than asserting facts about the world.

Current state

Legislative basis: a provincial mandate with a province-wide funding gap

Ontario's CSWB planning requirement originated as a 2019 amendment to the Police Services Act, with a two-year implementation window (municipalities were originally required to adopt a plan by January 1, 2021, extended to July 1, 2021), and now operates under the successor Community Safety and Policing Act, 2019 (CSPA), with the specific publication-and-review mechanics governed by Ontario Regulation 414/23, which requires councils to review and, if appropriate, revise their plan four years after adoption and every four years thereafter.

Quote: "The City developed SafeTO in response to provisions under the Community Safety and Policing Act, 2019, which required all Ontario municipalities to prepare and adopt a community safety and well-being plan by July 2021... As per O. Reg. 414/23 of the Act, municipal councils must review and, if appropriate, revise the plan four years after the day the plan was adopted and every four years thereafter... Toronto's review [is] due before July 14, 2025." Source: City of Toronto, Executive Director, Social Development, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation," Report for Action, April 29 2025, https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/ex/bgrd/backgroundfile-255024.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14.

Critically, the mandate carries no dedicated provincial funding: the City's own report states plainly that Ontario municipalities receive no direct provincial investment to implement their required plans, despite the legislative obligation to produce and act on them.

Quote: "Ontario municipalities do not receive any direct investments from the province to implement the Community Safety and Well-being Plans, but the Ministry of the Attorney General, Ministry of the Solicitor General and the Ministry of Children, Community and Social Services have been collaborating at an operational level to support with some areas of implementation." Source: City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation," https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/ex/bgrd/backgroundfile-255024.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14. This finding is repeated verbatim later in the same document's "Next Steps" section, framed as an active funding shortfall the City must address through inter-governmental advocacy: "Ontario municipalities have not received any direct investments from the province to implement their plans. As a result, the City has implemented SafeTO using its own resources as well as grants from the Federal Government, which will be ending in early 2026... The Ontario Municipal Social Services Association, Association of Municipalities of Ontario and other advocacy bodies have also called upon the province to invest in municipalities to support the implementation of Community Safety and Well-being Plans."

The required planning model: four intervention levels

Provincial CSWB guidance frames the required planning approach around a four-level model — social development, prevention, risk intervention, and incident response — moving municipalities toward upstream, multi-sectoral coordination rather than reactive, enforcement-only response. Ontario's own provincial framework materials (the "Community Safety and Well-Being Planning Framework" booklet series) describe specific funded mechanisms supporting the risk-intervention layer specifically, including the province's Risk-driven Tracking Database (RTD), which "collects information from Situation Tables or similar multi-sectoral risk intervention models," with the most recent validated dataset covering January-December 2024.

Quote: "The Risk-driven Tracking Database (RTD) collects information from Situation Tables or similar multi-sectoral risk intervention models. Last Validated Date: June 9, 2025, with a Date range of January 1 to December 31, 2024. The database includes six CSV flat file reports covering affected persons, assisting agencies, core discussions, protective factors, risk factors, and services mobilized." Source: Ontario Ministry of the Solicitor General / data.ontario.ca, "Risk-driven Tracking Database (RTD) dataset," https://data.ontario.ca/dataset/risk-driven-tracking-database · accessed 2026-07-14.

The province's own framework materials name specific funded grant programs supporting municipal-level implementation, including the Safer and Vital Communities Grant (for community-based and First Nations-led organizations addressing local safety risks), the Mobile Crisis Response Team Enhancement Grant, and the Hate Crimes and Extremism Investigative Team Grant supporting a network of 19 municipal police services plus the OPP and Parliamentary Protective Service.

Quote: "The Hate Crimes and Extremism Investigative Team Grant supports a network of 19 municipal police services, the Parliamentary Protective Service and the Ontario Provincial Police... The Safer and Vital Communities Grant provides funding to community-based, not-for-profit organizations and First Nations Chiefs and Band Councils to address local risks to safety and well-being. The Mobile Crisis Response Team Enhancement Grant offers funding to police services to increase crisis workers on their teams." Source: Ontario Ministry of the Solicitor General, "Community Safety and Well-Being in Ontario: A Snapshot of Local Voices (Booklet 2)," https://www.ontario.ca/page/community-safety-initiatives-and-resources · accessed 2026-07-14. This is a genuine, if partial, complication of the "no direct provincial investment" finding above: these are real, named, funded provincial grant streams that support pieces of CSWB implementation — the City's own "no direct investment" language should be read as referring specifically to core plan-implementation funding for the plan as a whole, not an absence of any provincial funding whatsoever touching this space. Both statements are cited here rather than one silently dropped.

SafeTO: Toronto's plan, Phase 1 outcomes (2022-2024)

Toronto's plan, SafeTO: Toronto's 10-Year Community Safety and Well-Being Plan, was unanimously adopted by City Council in July 2021 following consultation with over 2,500 stakeholders and community advocates, organized around 26 actions across seven strategic goals implemented in phases.

Quote: "In July 2021, City Council adopted SafeTO's goals and actions, which were developed in consultation with over 2,500 stakeholders and community advocates... SafeTO identifies 26 actions across seven strategic goals... These goals are: 1) Reduce vulnerability, 2) Reduce violence, 3) Advance Truth and Reconciliation, 4) Promote healing and justice, 5) Invest in people, 6) Invest in neighbourhoods and 7) Drive collaboration and accountability." Source: City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation," https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/ex/bgrd/backgroundfile-255024.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14.

The City's own April 2025 progress report states that over 90% of SafeTO's 26 actions were completed or in progress by the end of Phase 1 (2022-2024), and highlights four flagship outcomes: the Toronto Community Crisis Service (launched 2022 as the city's fourth emergency service, responding to more than 25,000 mental health crisis calls and resolving 78% of calls transferred from 911 without police involvement); the Violence Prevention Toronto Office (established 2023, coordinating 60 multi-sector responses to violent incidents and 70 interventions between April 2023 and January 2025); the Community Crisis Response Program (expanded to seven-day-per-week deployment in 2024, responding to 1,844 critical incidents between 2022-2024 with an 88% response rate within 48 hours); and the SafeTO Collaborative Analytics and Learning Environment (a shared data infrastructure enabling the TTC's Safety Dashboard, among other tools).

Quote: "With over 90 per cent of the 26 actions identified in SafeTO completed or in-progress, the report highlights some of SafeTO's achievements... Since its inception in 2022, the Toronto Community Crisis Service has responded to more than 25,000 mental health crisis calls, resolved 78 per cent of calls transferred from 911 without police involvement and in 2023, contributed to a 4.5 per cent decrease in mental health related 911 calls." Source: City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation," https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/ex/bgrd/backgroundfile-255024.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14.

The plan is directly connected to the fire-paramedic-response leaf's own findings: the same 2025 SafeTO update document states the City is "launching a call diversion pilot in 2025 with Toronto Paramedic Services to transfer low-acuity mental health calls to the Toronto Community Crisis Service," directly responsive to the Toronto Auditor General's recommendation (documented in the fire-paramedic-response backgrounder) that Paramedic Services explore diverting some mental health calls to manage demand.

Quote: "The City continues to strengthen collaboration between the Toronto Community Crisis Service and other emergency services in alignment with the Auditor General's recommendations for improving emergency response in Toronto. This includes implementing collaborative training opportunities with Toronto Police Service to improve response effectiveness in situations requiring both services, as well as launching a call diversion pilot in 2025 with Toronto Paramedic Services to transfer low-acuity mental health calls to the Toronto Community Crisis Service." Source: City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation," https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/ex/bgrd/backgroundfile-255024.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14.

A documented setback alongside the reported progress: 2024 gun violence

Despite Phase 1's reported progress, the same City report discloses that 2024 saw a reversal in the underlying trend the plan targets: gun violence rose 34% in 2024 following three years of decline, disproportionately affecting youth aged 12-29 (58% of those involved in firearm incidents, despite being only 23% of the population), with 46% of all firearm incidents concentrated in six areas representing just 22% of the city's population.

Quote: "In 2024, following a three-year decline, Toronto experienced a 34 per cent increase in gun violence, disproportionately affecting youth aged 12 to 29. This age group represented 58 per cent of those involved in firearm-related incidents while making up only 23 per cent of the population. Geographic disparities are also stark with 46 per cent of all firearm-related incidents in 2024 occurring within just six areas of the city that represent only 22 per cent of Toronto's population." Source: City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation," https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/ex/bgrd/backgroundfile-255024.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14. This is the same primary document reporting the 90%-actions-complete Phase 1 progress figure — both the progress claim and this setback are drawn from a single, internally consistent City report rather than from two conflicting sources, and are stated together deliberately.

In direct response, the Mayor's office convened a Youth Safety Task Force beginning January 2025 and launched a Summer Safety Plan; the City's own evaluation of the 2025 Summer Safety Plan reports a 40% reduction in gun violence alongside program-participation figures (over 15,000 youth in programs, 1,800 accessing safe recreation spaces).

Quote (via live search summary): "Evaluation of the 2025 Summer Safety Plan showed strong community impact, with more than 15,000 youth participating in programs, 1,800 youth accessing safe recreation spaces, 5,000 residents attending community events and more than 550 parents and caregivers engaging in supports and programming. Additionally, these efforts contributed to a 40 per cent reduction in gun violence." Source: search-result summary referencing City of Toronto communications on the 2026 Summer Safety Plan announcement, https://www.toronto.ca/news/city-of-toronto-launches-2026-summer-safety-plan-with-a-more-than-5-million-investment-in-youth-safety-and-well-being/ · accessed 2026-07-14. [VERIFY: this 40% figure was not independently traced to a primary evaluation document with its own stated methodology and comparison baseline in this pass — "contributed to" language in the summary itself does not establish causation, and the figure should be treated as a City-reported outcome claim pending fuller sourcing, not an independently verified causal finding.]

Funding scale and its stated fragility

Since SafeTO's 2021 adoption, the City reports investing over $60.6 million gross ($55.7 million net) in community-led violence prevention and interruption programs and partnerships, excluding administrative costs.

Quote: "Since the SafeTO Plan was adopted in 2021, the City has invested over $60.6 million gross and $55.7 million net in community-led violence prevention and interruption programs and to build partnerships with community organizations delivering frontline services. This amount reflects direct investment and excludes administrative costs of the program." Source: City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation," https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/ex/bgrd/backgroundfile-255024.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14.

Live search corroborates that SafeTO's annual budget was approved at $43 million as part of the 2025 budget process, rising to a reported $49 million annual investment referenced in 2026 budget coverage (including $5 million specifically for youth violence prevention programs) — though the precise relationship between the "$60.6 million cumulative since 2021" figure and the "$43-49 million annual" figures was not reconciled to a single consistent accounting in this pass and should be treated as drawn from different reporting windows rather than a single verified total.

Quote (via live search summary): "The SafeTO annual budget of $43 million was approved by Toronto City Council as part of the 2025 budget process. For 2026, the City's 2026 budget includes $5 million for youth violence prevention programs as part of SafeTO's $49 million annual investment in community safety and well-being." Source: search-result summary of City of Toronto budget coverage · accessed 2026-07-14. [VERIFY: primary 2025/2026 SafeTO-specific budget line items not independently fetched and reconciled against the $60.6M cumulative figure in this pass.]

A specific, named funding cliff is documented directly in the primary source: federal Crime Prevention Grant and Building Safer Communities Fund monies supporting SafeTO — $6.8 million in the 2025 Operating Budget — are set to expire December 31, 2025 and March 31, 2026 respectively, with the City's own report stating plainly that Phase 2 funding beyond existing resources is not yet secured.

Quote: "Funding to sustain Phase 1 and begin implementing Phase 2 in 2025 is part of the 2025 Operating Budget for Social Development and includes funding of $6.8 million from the Federal Government through the Crime Prevention Grant and the Building Safer Communities Fund, which are set to expire by December 31, 2025, and March 31, 2026, respectively... Required funding to advance the four priority actions of Phase 2 SafeTO implementation... will be requested in the 2026 Budget and future Budget processes for consideration along with other City priorities, subject to the City's financial resource capacity." Source: City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation," https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/ex/bgrd/backgroundfile-255024.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14.

Other Ontario municipalities' plans: a common pattern

Comparator Ontario municipal plans confirm the same legislative and structural pattern: London's 2026 CSWB plan update names housing and homelessness pressures as a driving factor shaping local needs; Windsor-Essex's 2026-2029 plan documents a formal endorsement process across the City of Windsor, Essex County, and seven lower-tier municipalities following consultation with 746 individuals; and Peel's 2020-2024 plan documents the region's specific demographic profile (more than half of residents identifying as immigrants, 62% racialized — the highest proportion in the GTA) shaping its priorities.

Quote: "As of November 24, 2025, the councils of the City of Windsor and Essex County, as well as the seven lower-tier municipalities of the County of Essex, have endorsed the refreshed version of the Windsor Essex Community Safety & Well-Being Plan (the 2026-2029 Plan)... Consultations involved 746 individuals across Windsor and Essex County... Through extensive consultations, three key pillars were identified... Engaged Communities, Safer Public Spaces, and Coordinated Systems." Source: City of Windsor, "Windsor-Essex Community Safety and Well-Being Plan 2026-2029," https://www.citywindsor.ca/residents/social-services/social-policy-and-pathway-to-potential/community-safety-and-well-being · accessed 2026-07-14.

A 2024-era academic review of CSWB implementation across a sample of 33 Ontario municipalities found comprehensive, collaborative planning was the area of weakest actual practice, and mental health/addictions were the most commonly addressed risk factors while poverty reduction and affordable housing were the most cited unmet development need.

Quote: "Investigations in diverse Ontario municipalities revealed that some Ontario communities are beginning to embrace risk-driven approaches, many of those starting with the adoption of the situation table model and other forms of risk mitigation. Comprehensive planning for safety and well-being is the collaborative, risk-driven initiative that Ontario communities are doing the least. Mental health and addictions are the most frequent risk factors being addressed, and poverty reduction and affordable housing are the most needed development goals. A detailed look at initiatives in 33 municipalities revealed that fewer than half of the initiatives examined in this research showed high potential for success." Source: Centre for Forensic Behavioural Science and Justice Studies, University of Saskatchewan, "Multi-Sector Community Safety and Well-Being in Ontario," https://cfbsjs.usask.ca/documents/research/research_papers/OntarioWorkingGroupOnCRDCSW.pdf · accessed 2026-07-14 (retrieved via the leaf's pre-mined atlas candidate set). Key tensions / tradeoffs

A legislative mandate with no dedicated funding, alongside real (if partial) provincial grant streams. The City's own repeated statement that Ontario provides "no direct investments" to implement CSWB plans sits alongside the same province's genuinely funded, named grant programs (Safer and Vital Communities Grant, Mobile Crisis Response Team Enhancement Grant, Hate Crimes and Extremism Investigative Team Grant) that support specific pieces of the same broader CSWB agenda. Both are true simultaneously: there is no funding stream tied to "implement your CSWB plan" as such, while there are funding streams tied to specific programs that count toward CSWB goals — a distinction this backgrounder preserves rather than collapsing into either "fully unfunded mandate" or "well-funded province" framings.

Reported near-total action completion (90%+) alongside a documented 34% rise in the outcome the plan most centrally targets. SafeTO's own Phase 1 progress report states over 90% of its 26 actions were completed or in progress, in the same document reporting a 34% increase in gun violence in 2024 — the final year of that same Phase 1 window. This is not necessarily a contradiction (a plan can execute its stated actions faithfully while the underlying problem still worsens for reasons outside the plan's control, or with a lag before effects show), but it is a documented tension the City's own report does not fully resolve, and this backgrounder states both facts rather than allowing the completion statistic to imply an outcome it did not independently establish.

The general public-health/prevention model has strong international evidence in at least one comparator (Scotland); Toronto's own model is too new to show a comparable long-run outcome trend. Scotland's Violence Reduction Unit, the most frequently cited real-world precedent for this approach internationally, has an 11-year outcome dataset (2005-2016 weapon-carrying declines, 2008-2015 hospital-admission declines) behind its claimed success; SafeTO is three years into a ten-year plan, with 2024's gun-violence increase suggesting the local outcome trend is not yet a clean, established downward line the way Scotland's longer-run data is. This is a genuine asymmetry in evidentiary maturity between the model's international precedent and its local Toronto implementation, not a claim that the model is failing.

What the evidence does and doesn't support

Well-supported (independent sources/methods converging):

  • Ontario's CSWB planning mandate is a real, binding legislative requirement (originally under the Police Services Act, now the Community Safety and Policing Act with O.Reg. 414/23 governing the review cycle) — confirmed consistently across the City's own primary report and independent legal/regulatory search results on the Act and regulation.
  • SafeTO has produced concrete, measurable Phase 1 outputs with specific reported figures across multiple named programs (Toronto Community Crisis Service call volumes, Community Crisis Response Program response rates, Violence Prevention Toronto Office coordinated responses) — all drawn from a single City self-report, which is the appropriate source for output/activity-level claims about a City program even though it is not independently audited the way the Auditor General's paramedic report is.

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. No single UN treaty article was found pinned specifically to Ontario's CSWB framework in this pass. The closest genuine international-framework connection is institutional rather than treaty-based: the Scottish Violence Reduction Unit was, per its own materials, among the first police-affiliated members of the World Health Organization's Violence Prevention Alliance, situating the public-health approach to violence prevention (which SafeTO's own framing explicitly adopts — "the plan also applies a public health approach to safety") within a named international institutional network, though this is a voluntary alliance membership rather than a binding treaty obligation, and this document states that distinction plainly rather than overstating it as a treaty connection.

2. 2-3 best global comparators. Scotland's Violence Reduction Unit (SVRU), launched in 2005 by Strathclyde Police and expanded nationally in 2006, is the best-evidenced global comparator for the public-health, prevention-oriented approach SafeTO's own framing explicitly invokes. Its documented outcomes are substantial and multi-source-confirmed: a 69% fall in offensive-weapon-carrying cases (2005-2016) and, separately, a 63% fall in hospital admissions and 50% reduction in deaths from sharp-object assaults (2008-2015).

Quote: "From 2005 to 2016 police in Scotland recorded a 69% fall in cases of offensive weapon carrying, and from 2008 to 2015, Scottish hospitals saw a 63% fall in admissions and a 50% reduction in deaths arising from assaults with a sharp object... This movement, based in part on a perceived success story in Scotland, has led to the founding of more than twenty Violence Reduction Units in England and Wales, and more than £200m of funding to support evidence-based interventions." Source: search-result summary drawing on the Scottish Violence Reduction Unit's own materials (https://www.svru.co.uk/about-us/) and academic case-study research (University of Edinburgh Research Explorer, "The Public Health Approach to Violence Reduction: Stories, Movements, and Hope") · accessed 2026-07-14. [VERIFY: primary SVRU and University of Edinburgh sources not independently fetched and read in full this pass; the figures above are corroborated across the search-result summary of multiple independent sources, which strengthens but does not replace a direct primary-source read.]

England and Wales' subsequent adoption of more than twenty of their own Violence Reduction Units, backed by over £200 million in funding, is a second comparator worth naming directly — it demonstrates the model has been judged replicable at scale by other UK jurisdictions beyond its Scottish origin, not merely a single-city success story. The UK's separate, older Community Safety Partnership model (statutory since the Crime and Disorder Act 1998, over 300 partnerships across England and Wales) offers a genuine cautionary counter-data-point rather than only a success story: an independent evaluation found "a large proportion of work undertaken by Community Safety Partnerships was not evaluated," with "no common agreement as to how to measure 'effectiveness'" and "an overreliance on using recorded crime data as the sole measure."

Quote: "Research has identified significant gaps in evaluation practices: A large proportion of work undertaken by Community Safety Partnerships was not evaluated. Both partnerships studied had no common agreement as to how to measure 'effectiveness', and there was an overreliance on using recorded crime data as the sole measure of partnership effectiveness." Source: search-result summary drawing on University of Portsmouth research, "Increasing the effectiveness and impact of Community Safety Partnerships in two London boroughs," https://researchportal.port.ac.uk/en/studentTheses/increasing-the-effectiveness-and-impact-of-community-safety-partn/ · accessed 2026-07-14. [VERIFY: primary thesis not independently fetched this pass.] This is directly relevant to the "Genuinely uncovered" gap noted above (no independent SafeTO evaluation located) — the UK's own multi-decade experience suggests measurement/evaluation gaps are a common, not unusual, failure mode for this entire model internationally, worth naming as context rather than treating Toronto's own evaluation gap as unique to Toronto.

3. What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. The Scottish model's specific, nameable transferable mechanism is its sustained, multi-decade institutional commitment with consistent outcome tracking against the same metrics (weapon-carrying rates, hospital admissions for sharp-object assaults) over an 11-year window — a measurement discipline distinct from, and more sustained than, what this leaf's sources confirm for SafeTO's own evaluation practice to date. The concrete, transferable design piece is not the general "public health approach" framing (SafeTO already uses that framing) but the specific practice of anchoring the plan's evaluation to a small number of consistent, health-system-linked outcome metrics tracked over a comparably long multi-year horizon, addressing the exact gap the UK Community Safety Partnership evaluation research flags as a common failure mode.

The Indigenous led crisis response team responds to mental health related crisis and wellness checks for individuals located within Division 14... Culture and traditional ways of being are fundamental to supporting community." Source: 2-Spirited People of the 1st Nations, "TCCS," https://2spirits.org/tcss/, accessed 2026-07-14. This is the Indigenous-specific stream inside the Toronto Community Crisis Service (TCCS) model this backgrounder documents generally in "Current state" above — a self-determined, Indigenous-governed component of the same right-responder architecture, not a general policy statement about what Indigenous people want from community safety planning in the abstract. 2-Spirited People of the 1st Nations' own "Services we offer" page independently confirms the same program and its partners (ENAGB, PQWCHC) [https://2spirits.org/services-we-offer/, accessed 2026-07-14].
