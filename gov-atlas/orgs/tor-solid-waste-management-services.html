Solid Waste Management Services - backgrounder

Backgrounder / GOV-STRAT-EVOL-d1 Wave 1 / 2026-07-30 / registry row: tor-solid-waste-management-services (research/atlas/government_orgs.csv) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

Solid Waste Management Services (SWMS) is a City of Toronto operating division, not a body created by its own enabling statute. Its authority derives from the City of Toronto Act, 2006, S.O. 2006, c. 11, Sched. A, which gives Council broad power to determine the City's administrative structure (s.2(3), s.8(2)) (https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/06c11, verified). Day-to-day service rules are set out in Toronto Municipal Code Chapter 844 (Waste Collection, Residential Properties), Chapter 841 (Commercial Properties), and Chapter 846 (Waste Management Facility), all made under that Act (https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/municode/1184_844.pdf, link resolves, verified). The division itself was not established by a dedicated act — it is an administrative unit of Council's own organizational chart (org_id: tor-solid-waste-management-services).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

SWMS collects, transports, processes, composts, and disposes of municipal and some private-sector solid waste — garbage, recycling, organics, yard waste, electronics, and household hazardous waste (HHW). It operates 4 collection yards, 1 maintenance yard, 7 transfer stations, 6 HHW depots, 2 organics processing facilities, Green Lane Landfill, and 160 former landfill sites, serving roughly 870,000 residential and non-residential properties plus 1,000 special events a year (https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/accountability-operations-customer-service/city-administration/staff-directory-divisions-and-customer-service/solid-waste-management-services/, fetched). Its current governing plan is the Council-adopted Long Term Waste Management Strategy (2016) (org_id row, strategy_current_url).

Governance & reporting line

SWMS reports through the Deputy City Manager, Infrastructure Services (parent_org: tor-deputy-city-manager-infrastructure-services, registry row) up to City Council, with policy oversight exercised through Council's relevant standing committee. The division is led by a General Manager (Matt Keliher, per the division's own staff page, fetched 2026-07-30) and is organized into five business sections: Collections & Litter Operations, Transfer Station & Landfill Operations, Policy Planning & Outreach, Business Services, and Infrastructure & Resource Management.

Budget scale

~$406.5M gross / ~$412.6M revenue / ~$6.2M net operating budget for 2026, plus a 3.75% rate increase effective January 1, 2026 (City of Toronto 2026 Budget Notes – Solid Waste Management Services, toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2026/bu/bgrd/backgroundfile-261782.pdf). The division also manages roughly $600M in physical assets (division staff page, fetched).

Institutional history

Toronto's solid waste functions were consolidated when the City of Toronto Act, 1997, S.O. 1997, c. 2 (https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/97c02, verified) amalgamated six former municipalities into the current City on January 1, 1998, folding their public-works and waste departments into a single Works and Emergency Services structure that was later reorganized into the standalone Solid Waste Management Services division; ⚠️ VERIFY exact year SWMS split out as its own division.

Priority evolution

2014-15 (sr-2014, 2a1b6060d273), Ernst & Young Collection Operations Safety Report (Jan 19, 2015, covering a Feb 2014 engagement): Commissioned to review SWMS collection/operational practices across four business-practice areas — waste collection operations (City and contracted), routing, operator training, vehicles/equipment design — and recommend public-safety improvements. Found SWMS's fleet CVOR rating "in the middle of the participants" and identified no significant unmitigated safety risks, but found "no formal strategic framework to deliver on" public safety as a common goal. Three priority recommendations: (1) build public safety into SWMS's culture/mission/strategy (including a dedicated public-safety mission statement and a "Public Safety Committee"), (2) implement detailed pedestrian-accident root-cause analysis and risk management, (3) launch a public-education outreach campaign on garbage-truck operation safety. This document predates and is independent of the Council-adopted Long Term Waste Management Strategy (2016) named in the backgrounder — it addresses collection-operations safety, not waste diversion/disposal strategy (2a1b6060d273).

2016 (not in this extraction; per backgrounder only) — Long Term Waste Management Strategy adopted by Council (July 12-15, 2016, Item PW14.2), placing "priority on maximizing the life of Green Lane Landfill by minimizing the amount of garbage sent for disposal" and identifying long-term residual-disposal-capacity options; cited retrospectively as decision history in sr-2025 (75def2e8dc94) — this document itself is not in the archived set for this pass, so its content is known only at the level of Council-decision-history framing in the later report, not independently verified here. ⚠️ VERIFY.

2022 (sr-2022, 70f76df3e8f7), 2022 Toronto Litter Audit Report (AET Group Inc., Nov 25, 2022): Eighth in a litter-audit series running back to 2002 (audits also in 2004, 2005, 2006, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2020), auditing 300 fixed sites. Large litter per site rose from 10 (2020) to 13 (2022) — framed against COVID-19-era disruption to pedestrian patterns and the city's post-pandemic "regained position as epicenter for tourism, culture, commerce, and entertainment." Small litter per site fell from 13 (2020) to 10 (2022); cigarette butts remained the top small-litter category across all five most recent audits (2012-2022). The report documents operational capacity growth: litter bins city-wide roughly doubled from 5,630 (2011) to over 11,000 (2022); Litter Operations described as comprising manual collection, mechanical litter-vacuum collection, mechanical bike-lane/laneway sweeping, and "fly squad" rapid-response teams, plus a Multi-Divisional Litter Working Group (SWMS, Transportation Services, Municipal Licensing and Standards, Economic Development and Culture) with a stated focus on cigarette-butt litter. Notes a COVID-era pivot: as of Nov 2022, "over 300 hours committed to assisting in the encampment removal city-wide," alongside other divisions (70f76df3e8f7).

2023 (not in this extraction; per sr-2025 decision history) — Residual Waste Management Work Plan adopted by Council (June 14-15, 2023, Item IE4.1), directing SWMS to conduct community consultation on energy-from-waste (incineration) before entering procurement/negotiation on any new long-term residual-waste option, and setting short-, medium-, and long-term option tracks. This document is not itself in the archived set; known here only via the 2025 update report's decision-history summary (75def2e8dc94). ⚠️ VERIFY.

Nov 2024 (sr-2025-2, 5765b58dbcd6), 2025 Interim SWMS Rates and Fees report: Recommends a 3.75% interim rate increase effective Jan 1, 2025, across all customer groups (multi-residential, single-family/RUAC, bag tags/bins, commercial/institutional), framed as needed "to maintain all current service levels and address future capital needs," projected to generate ~$13.4M in additional revenue. Capital priorities named as drivers: increased organics-processing capacity, landfill-gas utilization, Long-term Residual Waste Disposal options, and the Dufferin Waste Facility Site Improvement project. Notes the rate-setting process now runs on an accelerated Council calendar because "Bill 3 amended the City of Toronto Act to provide for the Mayor to present the City's annual Operating and Capital Budgets to City Council no later than February 1" (5765b58dbcd6).

Sept 2025 (sr-2025, 75def2e8dc94), Update on the City's Long-term Residual Waste Management Work Plan: Reports Green Lane Landfill's expected life at ~10 years (closure anticipated 2035); 2024 residential diversion rate at 51.7%, still short of the Province's Strategy for a Waste Free Ontario 80%-by-2050 target, with SWMS itself concluding that even at 80% diversion ~260,000 tonnes/year of residual waste would remain unmanaged. Reports SWMS "is currently undertaking a review and update of the Long-term Waste Management Strategy" (the 2016 document) — the first indication in this series that the 2016 strategy is being actively revised, not just implemented. Documents two rounds of 2023-2025 public consultation on energy-from-waste (Phase 1, informational, Dec 2024; Phase 2, opinion-gathering via online survey of 11,000+ respondents and independent polling of 1,800+ residents, May-June 2025) showing majority public support (72-79%) for further exploring energy-from-waste as an alternative to landfilling, alongside a competing preference (64-67%) that Toronto manage its waste "within its own borders." Reports a Request for Expression of Interest to 378 Ontario municipalities within 500km found zero interested partners for landfill-sharing, hosting, or co-development. A Green Lane Landfill expansion feasibility study concluded 25-year vertical+horizontal expansion is "technically feasible" pending a 6-7-year Environmental Assessment process. Sets a return-to-Committee date of 2027 for final direction (75def2e8dc94).

Ontario/Toronto relevance

This organization is itself a City of Toronto operating division — full relevance is direct and structural, not indirect. Its statutory basis (City of Toronto Act, 2006) and municipal code chapters are Toronto/Ontario instruments by definition. All facilities and sites named across the archived documents — Green Lane Landfill, the Dufferin Waste Facility, city transfer stations, collection districts (District 1-4), and litter-audit sites — are within Toronto. The 2025 residual-waste consultation explicitly engages Ontario provincial policy: it cites the Province's Strategy for a Waste Free Ontario 80%-by-2050 diversion target, references Ontario's Environmental Assessment Act, 2002 amendments as a driver of the City's disposal-planning timeline, and surveyed 378 Ontario municipalities within 500km of Toronto for landfill-partnership interest (75def2e8dc94). The 2022 litter audit report also references two existing energy-from-waste facilities already operating in the Greater Toronto Area (Brampton, Clarington), used as a reference point in the City's own 2025 public consultation (75def2e8dc94).
