City Planning - backgrounder

Backgrounder / 2026-07-30 / registry row: tor-city-planning (this library's government-document registry) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

City Planning is a division of the City of Toronto rather than a separate corporation; its parent, the City, is continued with broad natural-person powers under the City of Toronto Act, 2006, S.O. 2006, c. 11, Sched. A, ss.6(1) and 7 (https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/06c11, fetched and verified). Municipal land-use planning functions - official plans, zoning by-laws, subdivision and site-plan control that City Planning administers - are delegated to municipalities under the province's Planning Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. P.13 (https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90p13, resolution verified; ⚠️ still being checked - specific section text not retrievable this review due to fetch limits).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

Per the City's own 2026 Program Summary: "City Planning shapes Toronto's future by managing the physical form of the city and the opportunities it provides for growth, change, investment, and development," leading "city-wide and area-based policy development," design excellence, and "innovative policy, zoning, and research initiatives on land use issues" spanning housing, environmental sustainability, community/economic development, urban design, heritage conservation, and transportation (2026 Operating Budget and 2026-2035 Capital Budget and Plan - City Planning, p.1, registry strategy_current_url: https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/8ee7-2026-Public-Book-CP-V1.pdf, fetched). The division implements Toronto's Official Plan, whose vision to 2051 includes "a city of complete communities; a sustainable and resilient city; the most inclusive city in the world" (same source).

Governance & reporting line

City Planning's parent organization is recorded as the Deputy City Manager, Development and Growth Services (registry row tor-city-planning, parent_org field, this library's government-document registry), placing it within the City Manager's senior organizational structure under City Council. ⚠️ still being checked - direct confirmation of this reporting line against a current City organizational chart was not obtained this review.

Budget scale

~$48.9M total gross expenditures approved for 2026 ($48,930 thousand), rising to a $50.982M 2027 Outlook (+5.2%) and further to a 2028 Outlook up 3.0% again, per the City's own figures: "The 2027 Outlook with total gross expenditures of $50.982 million reflects an anticipated $2.510 million or 5.2% increase in gross expenditures above the 2026 Operating Budget" (2026 Operating Budget - City Planning, registry strategy_current_url, fetched). Approved 2026 staff complement is 362.0 positions (21.1 capital, 340.9 operating) (same source).

Toronto City Planning - strategy evolution

2026-08-01 / registry: tor-city-planning / grounded in archived copies (cited document id + sha256) / read through our research file for that body

TL;DR: The biggest priority added since the division's 2013 Strategic Plan is the Expanding Housing Options in Neighbourhoods (EHON) "Missing Middle" initiative, which by 2024 produced the first Official Plan/zoning changes in this series to intensify "Neighbourhoods"-designated land — a reversal from the 2013-2015 materials, which treated such areas only as sites to protect. The biggest quietly-dropped/uncertain item is the 2013-2018 Strategic Plan's five-direction framework itself: last confirmed active in the 2015 annual report, with no 2022-2024 document naming it or a successor plan. The most load-bearing figure is the 2023 Land Needs Assessment's finding of 1,312,040 net residential unit potential — characterized as "over fifty years of potential housing supply" beyond provincial growth forecasts to 2051. One open question the record cannot resolve: what happened to City Planning's own strategic-priority framework between 2016 and 2021, since no annual or subject report survives for those years beyond a one-paragraph 2018 stub.

Backgrounder summary

City Planning is a division of the City of Toronto, not a separate corporation; the City itself operates under broad natural-person powers granted by the City of Toronto Act, 2006. The land-use functions City Planning administers — official plan, zoning, subdivision and site-plan control — are delegated to municipalities under Ontario's Planning Act. Per the City's own 2026 Program Summary, the division "shapes Toronto's future by managing the physical form of the city," leading city-wide and area-based policy development, urban design, heritage conservation, housing, and transportation policy, and implementing the Official Plan (vision to 2051: "a city of complete communities; a sustainable and resilient city; the most inclusive city in the world"). Its parent is recorded as the Deputy City Manager, Development and Growth Services (⚠️ still being checked per backgrounder — reporting line not independently confirmed against a current org chart). Approved 2026 gross expenditures are ~$48.9M with 362.0 approved staff positions. The backgrounder does not establish a specific founding date for the division's current form beyond the 1998 amalgamation baseline (⚠️ still being checked).

Priority evolution

2013 strategic plan, "Planning a Great City, TOgether!" (sp-2013/sp-2018, 3f26ee763b23): Under Chief Planner Jennifer Keesmaat, the division's first Strategic Plan set two "Strategic Objectives" — "A Citywide Agenda for City Building" and "A Divisional Agenda for Establishing and Nurturing Leadership" — delivered through five Strategic Directions (Setting Priorities and Improving Processes; Enhancing and Strengthening the Capacity of the Division; Clear, Consistent and Compelling Communication; Pursuing Deep Collaborations; Measuring Success) and 44 numbered actions. Development-application review consumed an estimated 80% of staff time against a stated 50-60% best-practice benchmark, framing "workplan priority setting" as a core internal reform. The plan's work program was organized around two services — "City Building & Policy Development" and "Development Review, Decision & Implementation" — delivered through four geographic Community Planning districts (Etobicoke York, North York, Toronto & East York, Scarborough) plus city-wide units including a Waterfront Secretariat and Strategic Initiatives, Policy & Analysis. Ontario Municipal Board (OMB) hearing support was named as a distinct priority area requiring a dedicated support team and precedent file (3f26ee763b23).

2014 (ar-2014, e31e6b6703f5): With 350 staff, the division processed 204 new development projects, 6,266 planning/heritage applications, and produced 504 staff reports to Council. Major named priorities: the Port Lands Planning Framework (350-hectare working-port revitalization, joint with Waterfront Toronto), TOcore: Planning Toronto's Downtown (a new city-wide downtown growth-management study), the Relief Line Project Assessment, Midtown in Focus (Yonge-Eglinton public realm plan, Council-approved 2014), and the Six Points Interchange Reconfiguration. "Growing Conversations," a new public-engagement initiative aimed at broadening participation beyond traditional consultation audiences, was launched. The high-profile Mirvish+Gehry King Street West proposal illustrated the division's development-review/heritage-negotiation function, with Council refusing an initial 3-tower submission (2013) before a mediated 2-tower alternative was approved (2014) (e31e6b6703f5).

2015 (ar-2015, 04db5cfb84b1): Staff complement grew to 373. New priority: the Toronto Planning Review Panel, a 28-member civic-lottery resident advisory body — described in the report as "the first of its kind globally" — extending the "Growing Conversations" engagement agenda into a standing participatory-democracy mechanism, alongside a dedicated Youth Engagement Strategy. "Waterfront 2.0," a comprehensive strategic review, set up a second phase of waterfront revitalization funding negotiations with federal and provincial governments (flood protection, waterfront transit, land servicing, public realm). Complete Streets Guidelines were developed jointly with Transportation Services. The St. Lawrence Neighbourhood Heritage Conservation District was designated using new HCD procedures introduced into the Official Plan. The report frames the division's work against the 2013-2018 Strategic Plan's progress, noting two-thirds of the plan's 44 actions were underway at the midpoint (04db5cfb84b1).

2018 (ar-2018, 92380f4f88db): Extraction yielded only a one-paragraph web stub. The recoverable text states the 2018 Annual Report "made significant progress on two areas of focus: housing and mobility" and frames the report as required by the 2013-2018 Strategic Plan's Action 1 (an annual report to Council). No further substantive 2018 content is grounded in this review; ⚠️ still being checked for all specific 2018 priorities beyond this framing (92380f4f88db).

2022, "Future of Toronto Employment Areas and the Office Market" (oth-2022, 92e243740bd1): Extraction quality is poor (see Residuals & gaps), but the surviving content shows a Hemson Consulting/Cushman & Wakefield-informed study examining post-pandemic office and industrial floor-space trends (Ontario employment/unemployment data through May 2022) and recommending specific Employment Area land-use conversions: Core Employment Areas to General Employment Areas (2.3 ha), Employment Areas to Regeneration Areas with a Site and Area Specific Policy ("SASP") (45.5 ha), to Institutional Areas with a SASP (3.4 ha), to Mixed Use Areas with a SASP (11.1 ha), and to Parks with a SASP (0.8 ha) (92e243740bd1).

2023, "Our Plan Toronto: Land Needs Assessment" (oth-2023, 792bead8a78e): This report operationalizes the province-mandated Municipal Comprehensive Review (MCR) of the Official Plan for conformity with "A Place to Grow: the Provincial Growth Plan for the Greater Golden Horseshoe" (2020) — a legislated requirement under the Places to Grow Act and Planning Act, whose City deadline had been extended by the Minister of Municipal Affairs and Housing to July 2023. The Land Needs Assessment found a net residential potential of 1,312,040 units (89% in buildings of 5+ storeys), sufficient to house 2,375,481 more people (84% growth) beyond 2016 levels, and characterized as "over fifty years of potential housing supply" beyond Growth Plan forecasts to 2051. It separately identified a net new housing supply potential of 646,336 units by 2031 — 227% of the City's Municipal Housing Target — while flagging a "mismatch between the types of housing that people are seeking" and what is likely to be built, which the report ties to the Housing Action Plan's push for "a broader mix of rental, affordable, and ground-oriented homes." Toronto's employment base was projected to grow from 1,607,800 (2016) to 1,938,800-2,101,200 jobs by 2051. The report's own decision history traces a chain of preceding research bulletins — Housing Occupancy Trends 1996-2016 (adopted July 2019), Right-Sizing Housing and Generational Turnover (June 2021), Neighbourhood Change and Intensification (November 2021, which "informed the Expanding Housing Options in Neighbourhoods initiative"), and the 2021 Toronto Employment Survey (May 2022) — establishing that EHON's policy groundwork predates its 2024 final report by several years. An "Equity Impact Statement" appears as a standard report section, framing housing as "a fundamental human right" (792bead8a78e).

2024, Downsview Secondary Plan / By-law 708-2024 (oth-2024, 8f831b3b375d): Council adopted Amendment 716 to the Official Plan (By-law 708-2024, enacted June 27, 2024; in force August 9, 2024, except lands south of Wilson Avenue subject to Ontario Land Tribunal appeal OLT-24-000829) at its May 22-24, 2024 meeting. The plan converts former Downsview airport/aerospace-employment lands — described as having "significant physical links" to that heritage and to Indigenous land use "since time immemorial" — into a mixed-use, parks, and institutional-area district, with a new GO Rail station and Sheppard Avenue transit-corridor extension. Structurally new is the "Community Development Plan" (CDP), introduced as a companion instrument to the Secondary Plan and Urban Design Guidelines, explicitly described as embedding equity "with a focus on Indigenous and Black communities" into the area's development (8f831b3b375d).

2024, Expanding Housing Options in Neighbourhoods (EHON): Major Streets Study — Final Report (oth-2024-2, b15d9b34cce4): Traces back to a July 16, 2019 Council motion (MM9.36, "Tackling the Missing Middle and the Yellowbelt") directing City Planning to study increasing housing permissions in Neighbourhoods-designated (stable low-rise) areas. An Interim Report (June 2022) and Proposals Report (September 2023) preceded the May 9, 2024 Final Report, adopted by Council May 22, 2024 (item PH12.3), with the implementing Official Plan Amendment and Zoning By-law 569-2013 Amendment adopted June 26, 2024 (originally By-laws 608/609-2024, later renumbered 1061/1062-2025). The amendment permits small-scale apartment buildings and townhouses along "major streets" within Neighbourhoods designations — explicit "gentle intensification" of areas the Official Plan had previously protected as stable. The by-laws were appealed to the Ontario Land Tribunal; the OLT issued a decision resolving the appeal and bringing both into force on September 11, 2025 — the most recent dated event anywhere in this archived series (b15d9b34cce4).

Priorities added, dropped, renamed

  • Added — public-engagement innovation as a named priority track: "Growing Conversations" (2014, e31e6b6703f5) expands into the civic-lottery Toronto Planning Review Panel and a dedicated Youth Engagement Strategy (2015, 04db5cfb84b1). No comparable standing engagement-innovation program is named in the 2013 Strategic Plan itself, which frames communication/engagement mainly as an internal capacity gap to fix (3f26ee763b23).
  • Added — TOcore / downtown-specific growth management: first named in 2014 (e31e6b6703f5) as a new city-wide downtown study, still described as underway in 2015 (04db5cfb84b1); absent from the 2013 Strategic Plan's own priority list.
  • Added — Municipal Comprehensive Review / Growth Plan conformity as a mandate driver: the provincially legislated MCR and Land Needs Assessment cycle is central by 2023 (792bead8a78e) but is not mentioned in any 2013-2015 document read for this series; ⚠️ still being checked exactly when this became an operative Division priority, since no archived document bridges 2015-2022.
  • Added — Expanding Housing Options in Neighbourhoods (EHON) / "Missing Middle": originates from a 2019 Council motion, developed 2022-2024, and by 2024 produces the first Official Plan/zoning changes in this series that explicitly intensify "Neighbourhoods"-designated land (b15d9b34cce4) — a reversal in framing from the 2013-2015 materials, where such areas are discussed only as sites to protect from incompatible development, not as intensification targets.
  • Added — explicit equity instruments: the Community Development Plan tool with a named Indigenous/Black-community equity focus (Downsview, 2024, 8f831b3b375d) and the standardized "Equity Impact Statement" report section (2023, 792bead8a78e) have no equivalent in the 2013-2015 materials, which discuss "diverse audiences" and broadened participation in general terms but do not use a formal equity-statement or equity-focused planning instrument.
  • Added — Employment Area conversion as active policy: the 2022 Employment Areas study recommends converting specific hectare totals of designated employment land to residential/mixed-use/park/institutional uses (92e243740bd1), and the 2024 Downsview Secondary Plan executes exactly this kind of conversion at scale on a former-employment site (8f831b3b375d). The 2013-2015 materials read do not discuss employment-land conversion as a policy lever.
  • Renamed (provincial-level, not Division-initiated) — OMB to OLT: the 2013 Strategic Plan builds a dedicated support function around the Ontario Municipal Board (OMB) (3f26ee763b23); the 2024 documents refer exclusively to the Ontario Land Tribunal (OLT) for appeals (b15d9b34cce4; 8f831b3b375d). This reflects Ontario's provincial tribunal renaming/restructuring rather than a Division decision; the mechanics and exact transition date are not established anywhere in this archived series. ⚠️ still being checked.
  • Uncertain continuity / possibly dropped — the 2013-2018 Strategic Plan's five-direction framework: last confirmed as an active progress-tracking structure in the 2015 annual report (04db5cfb84b1: "two thirds of the Plan's 44 actions are now underway"). The only other document naming the plan explicitly, the thin 2018 stub, references it solely as the source of the "annual report to Council" requirement, not the five-direction framework itself (92380f4f88db). No archived document from 2022-2024 refers to the 2013-2018 Strategic Plan, its successor (if any), or its five directions by name. ⚠️ still being checked whether a successor strategic plan exists and was simply not archived, or whether the Division shifted to a different planning-priority mechanism (e.g., the MCR/Our Plan Toronto process itself functioning as the organizing frame by 2022-2024).

Budget & mandate inflection points

  • 2013 — First Strategic Plan adopted, establishing the five-direction framework and naming development-review resource pressure (est. 80% of staff time vs. 50-60% best practice) as the central internal problem to solve (3f26ee763b23).
  • 2014-2015 — Staff complement grew from 350 (2014) to 373 (2015) (e31e6b6703f5; 04db5cfb84b1). Section 37 community-benefit contributions secured through development review rose from $38 million (2014) to $57 million (2015) — these are developer-secured public-benefit contributions tied to specific approvals, not the Division's own operating budget, but they mark a growing scale of development-review activity (e31e6b6703f5; 04db5cfb84b1).
  • July 16, 2019 — Council motion MM9.36 directs City Planning to study "Missing Middle" housing options in Neighbourhoods areas — the origin point of what becomes the 2024 EHON Major Streets Official Plan/zoning amendments (b15d9b34cce4, retrospective account).
  • August 4, 2020 — Council commences the current Municipal Comprehensive Review of the Official Plan for Growth Plan conformity (792bead8a78e, retrospective account).
  • 2020 — Provincial Growth Plan ("A Place to Grow," 2020) and Provincial Policy Statement (2020) take effect, setting the legislated framework (population/employment forecasts to 2051, Employment Area protection and conversion rules, minimum intensification targets) that the 2023 Land Needs Assessment operationalizes (792bead8a78e).
  • Extended to July 2023 — the City's legislated MCR conformity deadline, extended from the original schedule by ministerial approval following a February 2022 Council request (792bead8a78e).
  • May 22-24, 2024 — a single Council meeting adopts both the EHON Major Streets Official Plan/zoning amendments (item PH12.3) and the Downsview Secondary Plan (item PH12.1/By-law 708-2024) — the two largest substantive land-use policy actions in this archived series, adopted essentially concurrently (b15d9b34cce4; 8f831b3b375d).
  • August 9, 2024 — Downsview Secondary Plan comes into force (except lands under active OLT appeal) (8f831b3b375d).
  • September 11, 2025 — Ontario Land Tribunal resolves the EHON Major Streets appeal, bringing the Official Plan Amendment and Zoning By-law Amendment into force — the most recent dated event in the archived series (b15d9b34cce4).

Ontario/Toronto relevance

City Planning is a division of the City of Toronto itself, so every document in this series is directly about Toronto's own land-use governance; there is no indirect-relevance question to resolve, unlike an arm's-length agency. The Division's mandate and tools operate inside a provincial legal framework documented across the series: the Planning Act (referenced as statutory authority for Official Plan amendments and by-laws, 8f831b3b375d), the Provincial Policy Statement and "A Place to Grow" Growth Plan for the Greater Golden Horseshoe (setting Toronto's growth forecasts, Employment Area protection rules, and intensification targets to 2051, 792bead8a78e), the Places to Grow Act (legislating the Municipal Comprehensive Review requirement, 792bead8a78e), and the provincial appeals tribunal — named as the Ontario Municipal Board in the 2013 materials and as the Ontario Land Tribunal in the 2024 materials (3f26ee763b23; b15d9b34cce4; 8f831b3b375d). The series documents the Division's core Toronto-specific outputs directly: the Official Plan and its amendments, Secondary Plans (Downsview), area studies (South Niagara, Six Points, Port Lands, Midtown in Focus, TOcore), and city-wide policy studies (Land Needs Assessment, Employment Areas study, EHON). No further "relevance" grounding is needed beyond what is cited elsewhere in this brief, since the entire corpus is first-party City of Toronto material.
