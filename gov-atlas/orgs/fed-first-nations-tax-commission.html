First Nations Tax Commission - backgrounder

Backgrounder / GOV-Q10-WAVE1-d1 / 2026-08-04 / registry row: fed-first-nations-tax-commission (this library's government-document registry) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

The First Nations Tax Commission (FNTC) is established under the First Nations Fiscal Management Act, S.C. 2005, c. 9 (https://lois-laws.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/F-11.67/FullText.html, confirmed via search of the Justice Laws site this review). FNTC's stated mission "is to assist First Nations governments in the exercise of their jurisdiction over local revenue systems, and to promote and support positive relationships between First Nations and their taxpayers" (FNTC FAQs page, https://fntc.ca/first-nations-tax-commission-faqs/, cited via search this review — direct fetch of fntc.ca returned HTTP 403 to this tool). FNTC "approves laws made under the First Nations Fiscal Management Act," assists First Nations in developing laws and by-laws, provides training and dispute resolution services, protects the integrity of the First Nation property tax system, and promotes First Nation property taxation and economic development (same source).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

FNTC is one of the shared-governance institutions created under the First Nations Fiscal Management Act (alongside the First Nations Financial Management Board and First Nations Finance Authority) and functions as the regulator/approval body for local revenue laws that First Nations governments make under the Act — chiefly property tax and related by-laws (https://fntc.ca/first-nations-tax-commission-faqs/, cited via search this review). Its registered document shelf consists of annual reports running from 2007/08 through 2024/25, indicating an unbroken run of yearly public reporting on this function (registry: fed-first-nations-tax-commission-ar-2008 through -ar-2025, this library's government-document registry).

Governance & reporting line

FNTC "is a shared governance First Nation institution" composed of 10 commissioners, including a Chief Commissioner and Deputy Chief Commissioner; nine of the ten Commissioners are appointed by the Governor in Council on the recommendation of the responsible Minister (cited via search this review). FNTC sits within the Crown-Indigenous Relations portfolio, with the Minister of Crown-Indigenous Relations as the responsible Minister (federal-organizations.canada.ca organization profile, OrgID=FNN, fetched this review — enabling legislation there is confirmed as "First Nations Fiscal Management Act (S.C. 2005, c. 9)"; the first Chief Commissioner and other Commissioners were appointed 2006-11-22 and 2007-04-30 respectively per that profile, consistent with the Commission becoming operational shortly after the Act's 2005 assent).

FNTC is Government of Canada-funded through the Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada / Finance Canada appropriations that support First Nations Fiscal Management Act institutions generally (search-confirmed context this review), but no dollar figure specific to FNTC is confirmed here.

Institutional history

FNTC's direct statutory predecessor was the Indian Taxation Advisory Board (ITAB), created in 1989 by the Minister of Indian Affairs to oversee the new First Nations property-taxation power added to the Indian Act by the 1988 "Kamloops Amendment" (Bill C-115), which for the first time enabled First Nations to levy property taxes on non-First Nations interests on reserve lands (cited via search this review, referencing FNTC's own "30th Anniversary of Bill C-115" retrospective at https://fntc.ca/30th-anniversary-of-bill-c-115/ — page itself returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch this review, so this history is cited from search-engine summary of that page rather than a direct quote; ⚠️ still being checked against a direct fetch next pass). FNTC itself was established in 2005 when the First Nations Fiscal Management Act received Royal Assent, formally superseding ITAB's advisory role with a statutory commission; FNTC is headquartered in Kamloops, British Columbia (cited via search this review).
