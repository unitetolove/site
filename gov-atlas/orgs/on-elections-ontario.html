Elections Ontario - backgrounder

Backgrounder / 2026-07-30 / registry row: on-elections-ontario (this library's government-document registry) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

Elections Ontario is the operating name of the Office of the Chief Electoral Officer, governed by the Election Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. E.6, consolidated at ontario.ca/laws (https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90e06, fetched and verified directly via r.jina.ai render). Section 3.1 provides "there shall be a Chief Electoral Officer who is an officer of the Assembly," appointed by order of the Legislature following selection by a multi-party panel chaired by the Speaker; the Act makes the CEO "responsible for the administration of this Act" (same source). Elections Ontario is also governed by the Election Finances Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. E.7 ⚠️ still being checked (this second statute cited on the org's own about page but not independently re-fetched and confirmed this review).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

Elections Ontario administers provincial general elections, by-elections and referendums; maintains the Permanent Register of Electors; and, as of January 1, 2024, provides preliminary elector lists from that register to municipal clerks for municipal, DSSAB and some school-board elections (which the office does not itself administer) (https://www.elections.on.ca/en/about-us.html, fetched directly). It owns and operates elections.on.ca and RegisterToVoteON.ca (same source). Registry notes describe it as the "Office of the Chief Electoral Officer, Officer of the Legislature" (registry row registry id: on-elections-ontario).

Governance & reporting line

Elections Ontario is described on its own site as "a non-partisan office of the Legislative Assembly of Ontario," led by the Chief Electoral Officer, who is appointed with unanimous consent of the Legislature (https://www.elections.on.ca/en/about-us.html). The current CEO, Greg Essensa, was appointed in June 2008 and is the seventh person to hold the position (same source).

Budget scale

Budget scale: ⚠️ not yet confirmed against an original source. No standalone operating-budget or Estimates figure for the office was located in this review; provincial Officers of the Legislature are typically funded through the Legislative Assembly's own Estimates rather than a ministry vote.

Institutional history

The position of Chief Election Officer was first held by Allan Dymond, appointed in 1919 to administer that year's general election and referendum campaigns; the title changed to "Chief Electoral Officer" in 2007 "to be consistent with other Canadian jurisdictions" (https://www.elections.on.ca/en/about-us.html). Subsequent office-holders are listed on the same page (1934, 1939, 1955, 1982, 2001, 2008).

Elections Ontario - strategy evolution

2026-08-02 / registry: on-elections-ontario / grounded in archived copies (cited document id + sha256) / read through our research file for that body

TL;DR: The clearest added priority across the two readable documents is formal risk-management/preparedness planning, elevated to a named strategic pillar ("Processes") in the 2024-2030 plan after emerging piecemeal as pandemic-era crisis response in the 2022 post-event report. The most notable quietly-changed element is the framing of technology itself: a hard-won "modernization" achievement story in 2022 becomes a more cautious, risk-balanced "measured and principled innovation" posture by 2024, with AI named for the first time as both an opportunity and a threat. The single clearest load-bearing number is the 2022 general election's actual delivery cost of $145,332,012 against a projected budget of $158,706,922 (a ~8.4% underrun). The open question this two-document record cannot resolve is what happened in the 2019-23 strategic-plan cycle's own stated priorities versus outcomes, or how the "five key strategic priorities" named for 2019-23 evolved into the four-pillar (People/Processes/Technology/Innovation) structure of 2024-2030 — no intermediate document bridges them.

It administers provincial general elections, by-elections and referendums; maintains the Permanent Register of Electors; and, since January 1, 2024, supplies preliminary elector lists to municipal clerks for municipal, DSSAB and some school-board elections it does not itself administer. The current Chief Electoral Officer, Greg Essensa, was appointed in June 2008 and is the seventh person to hold the position; the office traces to 1919. Priority evolution

Two usable documents 22 years apart cannot support a trend line. What they establish: in 2022, Elections Ontario frames its work around a named "2019-23 strategic plan" built on five priorities (integrity/public trust, team collaboration, election readiness, "measured and principled" modernization, and elector engagement/communication), developed in the wake of the 2018 election's first-time deployment of vote tabulators and ePoll books, and executed under sustained COVID-19 disruption. By 2024, the successor plan restructures around four differently-named pillars (People, Processes, Technology, Innovation) with a formal risk-management framework as a named strategy for the first time and a substantially widened list of named external pressure factors (extreme weather, AI/misinformation, declining institutional trust, demographic shift, changing nature of work). No archived document from the intervening years (2019-23 annual reports, a stated-but-not-archived "2019-2023 Strategic Plan," or the "2018 General Election Post-Event Report" cited within the 2022 report) is in this set, so the mechanism of that restructuring cannot be traced.

Priorities added, dropped, renamed

A 24-year gap (2000 to 2022) and then a 2-year gap (2022 to 2024) separate the three data points available; a genuine added/dropped/renamed table would imply a continuity this record does not have. Two additions are directly documented between the 2022 and 2024 texts: risk management becomes an explicit named strategy ("Establish a risk management framework that monitors risks, identifies responsive strategies, and informs a preparedness plan," 2024-2030 plan) where the 2022 report treats risk response as pandemic-specific ad hoc measures; and AI is named for the first time as an explicit priority-shaping factor ("A changing elections context" section, 2024-2030 plan), entirely absent from the 2022 report's own forward-looking "Beyond 2022" section, which instead centers declining voter turnout and engagement.

Budget & mandate inflection points

  • 2018 general election — first-time deployment of vote tabulators and electronic poll books ("ePoll books"), described as "the most significant change to Ontario's electoral process in over a century," using 22,000 ePoll books and 6,000 vote tabulators serving over 90% of electors (4df7311cda6c).
  • 2019 — 2019-23 strategic plan developed, the first Elections Ontario strategic plan to include key performance indicators (4df7311cda6c).
  • February 27, 2020 — Ottawa—Vanier and Orléans by-elections delivered as a live test of post-2018 process changes, immediately preceding the pandemic (4df7311cda6c).
  • November 30, 2020 — Chief Electoral Officer submits the "Special Report on Election Administration and the COVID-19 Pandemic" to the Legislative Assembly, recommending legislative change (4df7311cda6c).
  • June 2, 2022 — 43rd Ontario general election delivered; actual cost $145,332,012 against a projected budget of $158,706,922, with a standing KPI to stay within 5% of the initial budget projection (4df7311cda6c). Recorded voter turnout of 44% is described in the report as the lowest in the province's history (4df7311cda6c). Since 2018, Elections Ontario has leased voting equipment to nine provinces/territories and 54 Ontario/BC/Manitoba/Yukon municipalities under its Voting Technology Sharing Program, generating $2,993,640 returned to the Consolidated Revenue Fund (4df7311cda6c).
  • January 1, 2024 — per the backgrounder, Elections Ontario begins providing preliminary elector lists from the Permanent Register to municipal clerks for municipal, DSSAB and some school-board elections — not itself documented in either archived text read this review, carried from the backgrounder only.
  • 2024 — 2024-2030 Strategic Plan ("Accelerating Excellence") adopted, explicitly named as the office's longest-duration plan to date (5d41f3731f37).

Ontario/Toronto relevance

Elections Ontario is a province-wide, Toronto-headquartered body throughout both readable documents: its 2024-2030 plan lists a Toronto head-office address (26 Prince Andrew Place, Toronto, M3C 2H4) (5d41f3731f37), and the 2022 report's transmittal letter is addressed to the Speaker at Queen's Park, Toronto (4df7311cda6c). Both documents describe operations across all 124 Ontario provincial electoral districts and note the province's size as an operational challenge ("a province which rivals entire countries in both size and population," covering 1.076 million square kilometres) (4df7311cda6c). The 2022 election served 10.7 million electors through more than 7,000 voting locations and over 50,000 poll officials (4df7311cda6c; figures repeated as "11 million electors" and "over 7,000 locations" in the 2024 plan, 5d41f3731f37 — the two documents give slightly different elector-count figures, ⚠️ still being checked which is current). Relevance to this registry is direct and institutional (province-wide election administration headquartered in Toronto), not merely adjacent.
