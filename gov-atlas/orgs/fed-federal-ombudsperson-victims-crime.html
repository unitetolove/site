Federal Ombudsperson for Victims Of Crime (Office of the) - backgrounder

Backgrounder / 2026-07-30 / registry row: fed-federal-ombudsperson-victims-crime (this library's government-document registry) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

The Office was created in 2007 by Order in Council as an independent resource to help ensure the federal government meets its responsibilities to victims of crime (canada.ca/en/office-federal-ombudsperson-victims-crime.html, fetched via https://www.canada.ca/en/office-federal-ombudsperson-victims-crime.html; corroborated by Wikipedia). It is non-statutory in origin - no dedicated founding act was located - but its current functions are anchored in two statutes: the Canadian Victims Bill of Rights, S.C. 2015, c. 13, s. 2 (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-23.7/index.html - link verified, title/citation confirmed) and the complaint-handling provisions of the Corrections and Conditional Release Act (2024-25 Annual Report, https://www.canada.ca/en/office-federal-ombudsperson-victims-crime/publications/annualreports-rapportsannuels/ar2425.html, fetched via https://r.jina.ai/[same URL]). ⚠️ still being checked - the specific Order in Council number/date establishing the Office was not located at a fetchable primary source this review.

Roles, responsibilities & scope

The Office promotes victims' access to existing federal programs and services, addresses complaints from victims under the Corrections and Conditional Release Act, promotes awareness of victims' needs and concerns, identifies and reviews emerging and systemic issues affecting victims, and facilitates access to federal programs through information and referrals (2024-25 Annual Report, as above). In FY2024-25 it opened 1,741 cases and handled over 8,100 communications, a 68% workload increase over the prior year (same source).

Governance & reporting line

Led by the Federal Ombudsperson (Benjamin Roebuck as of the source consulted), described by the Office's own site as operating "independently and at arm's length from the Government of Canada" (canada.ca/en/office-federal-ombudsperson-victims-crime.html, as above). Registry row (registry id fed-federal-ombudsperson-victims-crime) classifies org_type as "office" with no listed parent, and its notes state the Office "is not a separate appropriated entity" and publishes no departmental plan, using an annual report instead. ⚠️ still being checked - a specific ministerial reporting line was not confirmed at a fetchable primary source this review, though the Office's historical placement is administratively within the justice/public-safety portfolio.

Office of the Federal Ombudsperson for Victims of Crime - strategy evolution

2026-07-31 (rolling continuation) / registry: fed-federal-ombudsperson-victims-crime / grounded in archived copies (cited document id + sha256) / read through our research file for that body

TL;DR: The biggest priority addition is the Office's own governance/independence reform, escalating from a flagged concern in FY2022-23 to a formal, Venice-Principles-invoking "RECOMMENDATION TO GOVERNMENT" in FY2023-24 — stating the Office is the only federal ombudsperson in Canada with a three-year term and that its Justice-Canada-dependent funding undermines its independence. The biggest quietly dropped element is the "arm's-length" self-description, used consistently through FY2020-21, then absent from every report FY2022-23 onward, replaced by pointed language about lacking an enabling statute. The most load-bearing number is that the Office's budget has remained near its original 2007 envelope of $1.5 million despite more than 40% inflation, even as total expenditures reached $2,085,913 and files opened nearly tripled to 1,741 by FY2024-25. One open question: whether the "Ombudsperson" to "Ombud" title shortening recommended in the FY2023-24 report was ever formally enacted — the most recent report still uses "Ombudsperson" throughout with no indication the change occurred.

Backgrounder summary

The Office was created in March 2007 by Order in Council as an arm's-length federal resource for victims of crime, with no dedicated founding statute; its current functions are anchored in the Canadian Victims Bill of Rights (CVBR, S.C. 2015, c. 13, s. 2) and the complaint-handling provisions of the Corrections and Conditional Release Act (backgrounder). Its mandate is to promote victims' access to federal programs, review complaints about CCRA compliance, raise awareness of victims' needs, and identify systemic issues affecting victims of crime (backgrounder; ar-2008, 89f1b9480dbc). It reports to the Minister of Justice, describes itself across most of this series as operating "at arm's length" from government (ar-2021, 88070714160f), though that framing drops out of the reports from FY2022-23 onward as the Office began publicly flagging its lack of an enabling statute and dependence on Department of Justice funding as a threat to its independence (ar-2023, 881dc278882a; ar-2024, b6896f7e63ad). The backgrounder flagged budget scale as ⚠️ still being checked; this series largely resolves that gap, with a near-complete run of total-expenditure figures from $558,910 in FY2007-08 (ar-2008, 89f1b9480dbc) to $2,085,913 in FY2024-25 (ar-2025, ccfb1c3557cd).

Priority evolution

FY2007-08 (ar-2008, 89f1b9480dbc): Inaugural report. The Office was created March 2007 as an arm's-length organization within the Department of Justice, exclusively federal jurisdiction, with four mandate pillars: promoting victim access to federal programs/referrals; addressing CCRA compliance complaints; raising criminal-justice-system awareness of victims' needs; identifying emerging/systemic issues. First-year recommendations: incorporated into the Correctional Service of Canada's independent review panel (adopted — the panel's October 2007 report "incorporated all of the Ombudsman's recommendations"); legislation requiring Internet Service Providers to give police customer name/address information in child-exploitation investigations; a review of restitution options for victims; and a review of the National Sex Offender Registry's access rules. The Office logged 555 contacts in its first year and reported expenditures of $558,910.

FY2008-09 (ar-2009, 866f271f42ce): New recommendations: a CCRA Schedule 1 amendment recognizing child pornography and luring as violent offences for accelerated-parole purposes (partially adopted by June 2009); $5 million in federal funding for Child Advocacy Centres (not adopted this year); deportation-status notification for victims under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (stalled — CBSA and Citizenship and Immigration Canada each disclaimed jurisdiction); and automatic imposition of the Victim Fine Surcharge under Criminal Code s.737(5)/(6) (government pushback on removing judicial discretion). The Office shifted its casework metric from raw "contacts" to "files opened" this year (419 files), added a Senior Legal Counsel position, and expenditures nearly doubled to $1,059,056.

FY2009-10 (ar-2010, 419838ccf3f0): A leadership transition spans this report: the first Ombudsman's term ended during the year (roughly a four-month vacancy followed, based on the subsequent report's confirmation that a successor was appointed in August 2010). The Office released its first two special reports — Every Image, Every Child (nine recommendations on Internet-facilitated child sexual abuse) and Towards a Greater Respect for Victims in the Corrections and Conditional Release Act (13 recommendations, including entrenching the Canadian Statement of Basic Principles of Justice for Victims of Crime in the CCRA). New asks included an RCMP Act referral-policy amendment, action on sexual violence in the Canadian Forces, and a federal Missing Persons Index. Files opened rose 13% to 474; the Child Advocacy Centre funding ask was renewed for a third year, still unfunded.

FY2010-11 (ar-2011, 424696c3a5ca): A new Ombudsman took office in August 2010, roughly four months into the fiscal year. This was the series' strongest year for adopted recommendations: Bill S-6 ("Serious Time for Serious Crime," faint-hope clause reform), Bill C-464 (bail decisions/accused's children), Bill C-48 (ending sentence discounts for multiple murders), Bill C-22 (mandatory ISP reporting of child sexual abuse images), and Bill C-21 (white-collar crime penalties) all received Royal Assent in March 2011 incorporating recommendations the Office had made before parliamentary committees. The Child Advocacy Centre funding ask was finally adopted: in October 2010 the federal government committed more than $5 million. However, Bill C-39 (further CCRA victim-rights amendments) died on the order paper when the 2011 election was called. Files opened jumped 61% to 766, with Ontario the largest single source of contacts. Funding was described as derived from the Department of Justice's Federal Victims Strategy in five-year increments; a renewal request was filed in March 2011.

FY2011-12 (ar-2012, 4e2d6f8e475f): The Office released its major special report Shifting the Conversation (February 2012), organizing recommendations around three areas — information for victims, meaningful participation in the justice system, and tangible supports (Victim Fine Surcharge enforcement, restitution). Before the Safe Streets and Communities Act (Bill C-10) passed, the Office pressed unsuccessfully for several additional victim-information provisions. In a rare instance of outright rejection, the Office recommended against Bill C-19 ending the long-gun registry, citing its "demonstrated effectiveness in reducing long-gun related homicides"; the bill passed regardless in April 2012. Budget 2011 confirmed a renewed five-year funding envelope described as providing "approximately $1.5 million in actual spending annually." Files opened: 546.

FY2012-13 (ar-2013, 438c0cf1c852): Marking the Office's sixth anniversary, this report notes the government's February 2013 announcement of plans to develop a Victims Bill of Rights — the first appearance in the series of what becomes the CVBR. Bill C-36 (Protecting Canada's Seniors Act, elder-abuse sentencing factors) was adopted as introduced; Bill C-44 (Helping Families in Need Act) passed in December 2012 without incorporating the Office's recommended amendments. The multi-year push for a federal Missing Persons Index was reported explicitly stalled, with the Minister of Public Safety stating current legislation did not permit the requested DNA-index structure. The Office launched its first social-media channels (YouTube, Flickr) this year. Files opened: 603 (a 10% increase), 203 of them from Ontario.

[Gap: FY2013-14 not archived — see Residuals & gaps.]

FY2014-15 (ar-2015, 22a35d209258): Dominated by Bill C-32, the Canadian Victims Bill of Rights, tabled April 3, 2014. The Office published two position documents (Cornerstone for Change; Strengthening the Victims Bill of Rights), submitted 29 recommendations, and testified before both House and Senate committees; as of the report's close the CVBR had not yet passed. Other bill-specific submissions covered Bills C-13, C-26, C-36, C-43, C-591, C-14, C-479, C-483, and C-489, with mixed outcomes (Bill C-591 amended in line with the Office's recommendation; Bill C-479's recommendations explicitly "not adopted by the Committee"; Bill C-489's central ask rejected). Files opened: 546, 42% from Ontario.

FY2015-16 (ar-2016, 525b39f251b8): Major statutory inflection point: the Canadian Victims Bill of Rights came into force July 23, 2015 (with related CCRA amendments following June 1, 2016). This immediately created a new systemic problem the Office began tracking: fragmentation of victim complaint mechanisms across federal departments, since the CVBR required each department to stand up its own process. The Office launched a Data Mapping Study with Statistics Canada's Canadian Centre for Justice Statistics and completed its first-ever formal systemic review (financial assistance for parole-hearing attendance), which the Department of Justice partially accepted but explicitly rejected the recommendation for supervisory oversight of every application as "burdensome to staff." Files opened fell to 453; this is also the year "OFOVC role and mandate" was dropped as a tracked complaint-topic category.

FY2016-17 (ar-2017, e1a827ef30e7): Marking the Office's tenth anniversary. A second systemic review covered the Federal Income Support for Parents of Murdered or Missing Children grant, finding it "considerably underused" — only $223,300 paid out against up to $10 million available annually, with administrative costs "about 14 times higher" than payouts to families. The Office broadened its consultative footprint substantially: submissions on CBSA inadmissibility regulations, federal prison-farm re-employment of offenders, cannabis legalization, a federal gender-based-violence strategy, cyber-victimization, military justice, national security, and record suspensions. Files opened continued declining, to 399.

FY2017-18 (ar-2018, d322410c23e8): A major leadership disruption: the Ombudsman's seven-year term ended November 15, 2017, and no successor is named in this report — a vacancy begins. This is the first report whose canada.ca URL uses "ombudsperson," although the report's own prose still says "Ombudsman" throughout. The Office finalized its PMMC systemic review (15 recommendations, delivered June 2017; the Minister's response did not address them point by point) and ran cross-country focus groups feeding into the federal Criminal Justice System Review. The Office explicitly attributes a drop in files opened, to 377, to "reduced public presence" during the vacancy.

FY2018-19 (ar-2019, ed77cb5e56d0): A new Ombudsman was appointed in October 2018, ending a vacancy the report's own footnote dates November 15, 2017 to September 30, 2018 (roughly 10.5 months). Given the mid-year appointment, this report covers a partial working year: five formal submissions plus one letter, including support for Bill C-77 (extending CVBR-equivalent rights into the military justice system, an ask the Office had pressed since 2009) and Bill C-83 (parole-hearing audio-recording access). This report is materially thinner than adjacent years — its casework charts are unpopulated placeholders (see Residuals & gaps) — but it contains the series' first forward-looking reference to the Independent Civilian Review into Missing Person Investigations conducted by the Toronto Police Service.

FY2019-20 (ar-2020, d3db8bf0f563): The Office established an Indigenous Advisory Circle and an Academic Advisory Circle, and began work on a five-year CVBR progress report. Thirty-five recommendations were made to government this year. Bill C-77 received Royal Assent in June 2019, finally closing the near-decade-old statutory gap on military-justice victims' rights. When COVID-19 arrived in March 2020, the Parole Board of Canada barred all hearing observers, including registered victims; the Office intervened, and by April 22, 2020 the Minister of Public Safety directed the Board to implement teleconferencing nationally — a fast, directly attributed policy win. The Office also delivered a formal submission to the Toronto Police Service's Independent Civilian Review into Missing Person Investigations, recommending online case profiles, a multidisciplinary missing-person team, and cultural-competence training. Staff complement grew from nine to twelve.

FY2020-21 (ar-2021, 88070714160f): Marking the CVBR's fifth anniversary, the Office released a Progress Report on the Canadian Victims Bill of Rights (November 2020) with 15 recommendations to strengthen the Act, including naming the Office itself as "the single authority" for reviewing victim complaints against federal bodies, and deleting CVBR sections 27-29 (which deny victims a court-appeal remedy for rights violations). The Office cites its governing authority as Orders in Council P.C. 2014-0615 (May 27, 2014) and P.C. 2016-0750 (August 23, 2016). Files opened reached a then-record 644, with 80 recommendations made across 35 submissions and letters. Total expenditures: $1,453,848.

FY2021-22 (ar-2022, b259d27493c8): A second major leadership transition: the Ombudsman departed the role (effective September 30, 2021, per the following year's report; formally noted in this report as "October 2021"), and no successor is named — a vacancy begins that runs into the following fiscal year. Notably, the Office's own institutional name shifts from "Ombudsman" to "Ombudsperson" in this report's body text, before any new appointee had been named. New work included a Frontline Service Provider Advisory Circle, the Office's first client satisfaction survey, and publication-ban reform recommendations. Files opened rose 18.8% to 765, but the number of recommendations made fell to 40 (from 80), which the Office links to the vacancy.

FY2022-23 (ar-2023, 881dc278882a): A new Ombudsperson was appointed October 24, 2022, ending a vacancy the report's footnote precisely dates September 30, 2021 to October 24, 2022 — about 13 months, the longest gap in the series. This report is the first to name the full predecessor lineage. The "arm's-length" self-description, present in every prior report reviewed, does not appear in this report — instead the Office describes itself as a "special advisor" to the Ministers of Justice and Public Safety. The Office explicitly states its budget has not increased since its 2007 creation ($1.5 million approved then) "despite more than 40% inflation and increased workloads," and flags for the first time that its three-year appointment term and Justice-Canada-dependent funding may compromise its independence. Files opened dipped to 648, which the following year's report attributes to the vacancy.

FY2023-24 (ar-2024, b6896f7e63ad): The Ombudsperson's first full fiscal year. Bill S-12 received Royal Assent October 26, 2023, delivering the incoming Ombudsperson's stated top priority — automatic, rather than on-request, victim notification for federally sentenced offenders. The Office pushed CCRA reform on prison-transfer notification after a high-profile security-reclassification transfer drew public attention (a footnote citation to CBC coverage references the Bernardo case without naming it in the report's own body text). Most significantly, this report contains a formal, boxed "RECOMMENDATION TO GOVERNMENT" invoking the Venice Principles on ombudsman independence, explicitly stating the Office is "the only federal ombudsperson in Canada with a three-year term," that its funding "dependency... can undermine the perceived and actual independence of the Office," and that peer federal ombuds offices operate on budgets of $4.1-7.9 million against the Office's much smaller envelope. It also launched its first systemic investigation since 2017, on sexual violence in the criminal justice system (opened March 11, 2024). Files opened surged to 1,035, a 60% increase the report explicitly ties to recovery from "the year-long absence of an Ombud." Total expenditures: $1,757,537.

FY2024-25 (ar-2025, ccfb1c3557cd): The sexual-violence systemic investigation continued under an Expert Advisory Circle chaired by a named survivor-advocate co-chair, drawing on 107 survivor interviews and over 600 stakeholder interviews, with a final report due Fall 2025 (outside this report's window). The Office issued an open letter marking the CVBR's tenth anniversary (July 2024, 12 recommendations) and released a dedicated report, Strengthening Access to Justice for Victims of Hate Crime in Canada (December 10, 2024, 13 recommendations, based on 187 police and victim-service-organization survey responses). This is the first report in the series to cite the CVBR's full statutory reference, "Canadian Victims Bill of Rights, S.C. 2015, c.13, s.2." The Office also began advocating for a federal Gender-Based Violence Commissioner and for a defined Criminal Code term for "femicide." Casework volume nearly tripled from two years earlier: 1,741 files opened, a 68% year-over-year increase, plus over 8,100 associated emails and calls. Total expenditures: $2,085,913.

  • October 26, 2023 — Bill S-12 receives Royal Assent, delivering automatic victim-notification rights — the incoming Ombudsperson's stated top priority (ar-2024, b6896f7e63ad).
  • FY2023-24 report — First formal, numbered recommendation that government review and reform the Office's own Order-in-Council mandate (extend the three-year term, rename "Ombudsperson" to "Ombud," legislate authority within the CVBR, align budget with peer federal ombuds offices reported at $4.1-7.9 million) (ar-2024, b6896f7e63ad).
  • March 11, 2024 — First systemic investigation since 2017 launched, on sexual violence in the criminal justice system; final report due Fall 2025, outside this archived series (ar-2024, b6896f7e63ad; ar-2025, ccfb1c3557cd).
  • FY2024-25 — Total expenditures reach $2,085,913, against a starting 2007 approved budget the Office itself describes as $1.5 million and "minimally changed" since (ar-2023, 881dc278882a; ar-2025, ccfb1c3557cd).

Ontario/Toronto relevance

Ontario is the single largest source of the Office's casework in every year this series reports a location breakdown — for example 42% of contacts in FY2014-15 (ar-2015, 22a35d209258), 203 of 603 files in FY2012-13 (ar-2013, 438c0cf1c852), and 229 of 765 files in FY2021-22 (ar-2022, b259d27493c8) — though this reflects Ontario's population share within a nationally scoped mandate rather than a Toronto-specific program. Direct Toronto engagement is concentrated in two windows. In the Office's earliest years it met with the Toronto Police Service's Chief of Police, referenced the Toronto-based Clarke Institute of Psychiatry and The Gatehouse (a Toronto child-abuse-survivor support organization) as expert/service contacts, and attended several Toronto-hosted conferences (ar-2009, 866f271f42ce; ar-2010, 419838ccf3f0). That direct engagement largely disappears from the reports covering FY2010-11 through FY2017-18, which mention Ontario only at the provincial level. It resurfaces substantially from FY2018-19: the Office made a formal, detailed submission to the Independent Civilian Review into Missing Person Investigations conducted by the Toronto Police Service, examining that service's handling of missing-person cases with particular attention to 2SLGBTQQIA and other marginalized communities, recommending online case profiling, a dedicated multidisciplinary missing-person team, and cultural-competence training for investigators (ar-2019, ed77cb5e56d0; ar-2020, d3db8bf0f563). Later reports add scattered Toronto touchpoints — a Toronto Metropolitan University panelist at an Office-linked webinar (ar-2022, b259d27493c8) and the Ombudsperson speaking at a Toronto academic conference on men's experiences of family violence, alongside a footnoted, unnamed reference to Ontario-based offences via news coverage (ar-2024, b6896f7e63ad). No Ontario or Toronto reference was found in the most recent report (ar-2025, ccfb1c3557cd). On balance, Toronto/GTA relevance in this series is mostly indirect — proportional to Ontario's population share of a national caseload — with the Toronto Police Service missing-persons review standing out as the one instance of genuine, specific engagement with a City of Toronto institution and systemic issue.
