Durham Regional Police Service - backgrounder

Mandate & statutory basis

Durham Regional Police Service (DRPS) is a municipal police service governed by Ontario's Community Safety and Policing Act, 2019 (S.O. 2019, c. 1, Sched. 1), consolidated and current to 2025-06-05 at e-Laws (https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/19c01, fetched and verified directly). The Act requires that "every municipal board shall maintain a police service" (s. 22) and defines "adequate and effective policing" to include crime prevention, law enforcement, public-peace maintenance, emergency response, and victim assistance (s. 11) (same source). DRPS is the police service maintained for the Regional Municipality of Durham (registry row: durh-durham-regional-police-service, parent board durh-durham-regional-police-services-board).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

DRPS delivers front-line and specialized policing across Durham Region, organized into five operational divisions (West, East, North, Central West, Central East) plus specialty units including 9-1-1 Communications, Air Support, and Serious and Organized Crime (https://www.drps.ca/about-us/, fetched directly). Its statutory service obligations (crime prevention, law enforcement, emergency response, victim assistance, public-order maintenance) are set by CSPA 2019 s. 11, which applies to every Ontario police service (https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/19c01). ⚠️ VERIFY - an explicit DRPS jurisdiction list (the eight Durham-area municipalities) was not confirmed against an official DRPS or Durham Region page this session.

Governance & reporting line

DRPS operates under a Chief of Police who is recruited, appointed, and annually performance-reviewed by the civilian Durham Regional Police Services Board under CSPA 2019 s. 37 (https://durhampoliceboard.ca/about-us/, fetched directly; statutory duty confirmed at https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/19c01). The Board is DRPS's registry-listed parent org (durh-durham-regional-police-services-board).

Backgrounder summary

DRPS is a municipal police service maintained under Ontario's Community Safety and Policing Act, 2019 (CSPA), governed by the civilian Durham Regional Police Services Board, which appoints and performance-reviews the Chief of Police (backgrounder, citing https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/19c01 and https://durhampoliceboard.ca/about-us/). The backgrounder flags two unresolved items — an explicit list of DRPS's eight-municipality jurisdiction and a specific budget total — that this brief's own reads partially resolve: bp-2019 confirms Durham Region's constituent municipalities by name (Ajax, Whitby, Oshawa, Clarington, Brock, Scugog, Uxbridge, and implicitly Pickering via acknowledgements) (6f7e4a14ccb9), and ar-2023 supplies a specific 2023 budget total ($266.4 million gross expenditure) (3c11848513ce) — see Residuals & gaps for what remains unconfirmed (founding date/instrument).

Priority evolution

2017-2019 (bp-2019, 6f7e4a14ccb9): Under Chief Paul Martin and Board Chair Roger Anderson, the Business Plan names three strategic goals — Deliver Community Safety through Collaboration, Demonstrate Excellence in Core Service Delivery, Build Strength in Our Membership — built from an environmental scan and consultation (500+ public conversations, 3,300+ survey responses, 80+ internal staff consulted). Sub-objectives foreground community partnerships (the Durham Connect multi-agency risk-intervention table), road safety, bias-free policing training, and workforce diversity recruitment, with the Board's own review of the prior 2014-2016 plan reporting a 20% youth-crime-rate decrease (2013-2015) and a 5% overall crime-rate decrease (2013-2015) as its inherited baseline.

2023 (ar-2023, 3c11848513ce): New Chief Peter Moreira (sworn in March 24, 2023, the ninth Chief, succeeding Chief Rollauer) names three personal mandate priorities — Building Trust, Enhancing Strategic Partnerships, Advancing the Modernization of the Service — and launches "Forward. Together.," an internal organizational-review project (initiated June 2023) explicitly structured around those same three themes. In parallel, the report frames itself as reporting against the "previous Plan's (2020-2022)" goals, restated with the same three titles as the 2017-2019 plan but re-worded sub-objectives (e.g., "Attract a skilled workforce that reflects the diversity of the community" tracked via a 55%-racialized-applicant figure; "Prevent re-offending" tracked via an 18% violent-repeat-offender share, described as trending down from 23% in 2018). A new Strategic Plan (covering 2024 onward) is reported as having been developed during 2023 via a fresh consultation round (thousands of residents, ~4,000-response external survey, 50+ service-provider consultations, a Diversity Advisory Committee Town Hall).

2024 (sp-2024, 3680588f128b; ar-2024, cd8cec8946dd): The new Strategic Plan reframes the same three-goal architecture again, this time as Be a leader in innovative and modernized policing / Have strong strategic partnerships that enhance community safety / Earn the trust and confidence of members and local communities — explicit continuity of theme (innovation/modernization, partnerships, trust) with bp-2019's goals, but new titles and, for the first time in this series, an explicit tie to Durham Region's own Community Safety and Wellbeing (CSWB) Plan risk-factor categories (criminal involvement, racism, homelessness, mental health, substance use, victimization) as an organizing input. The ar-2024 landing page confirms DRPS's 50th anniversary year, an expanded Command team (two new Deputy Chiefs plus General Counsel), and 76 new positions added in the 2024 budget, but does not itself carry a comparable detailed statistical section to ar-2023's.

Across the full window, the record supports one clear claim: DRPS's strategic framing shifted from a 2017-2019 plan built bottom-up from environmental-scan/consultation themes (collaboration, service-delivery excellence, membership strength) toward a 2023-2024 framing built substantially around a new Chief's personally-stated mandate priorities (trust, partnerships, modernization), with the underlying three-goal structure and several sub-themes (workforce diversity, re-offending, road safety, partnership with community/social-service agencies) persisting in substance even as titles changed each cycle.

Priorities added, dropped, renamed

  • Added — explicit "modernization" as a named strategic pillar, tied to legislative change. Absent by name from bp-2019's three goals (which speak of "innovation" only as a cross-cutting theme, not a goal title). First appears as one of Chief Moreira's three named 2023 mandate priorities (ar-2023, 3c11848513ce: "Advancing the Modernization of the Service") and becomes goal-title language in sp-2024 ("Be a leader in innovative and modernized policing," 3680588f128b). ar-2024 explicitly ties modernization to the CSPA 2019 as "not only a strategic priority — it is a legislative necessity" (cd8cec8946dd).
  • Added — a formal internal organizational-review project ("Forward. Together."). Not present in bp-2019. First named in ar-2023 as launched June 2023 under A/Director Holly Britton and Superintendent Paul Hallett, framed around building trust, forging partnerships, and modernization (3c11848513ce).
  • Renamed — the three-goal titles themselves, twice. bp-2019's "Deliver Community Safety through Collaboration / Demonstrate Excellence in Core Service Delivery / Build Strength in Our Membership" (6f7e4a14ccb9) is restated with the same titles but reworded sub-objectives as the "2020-2022" plan reviewed in ar-2023 (3c11848513ce), then replaced with new titles entirely in sp-2024 and echoed in ar-2024's "Strategic Plan Review" section (3680588f128b; cd8cec8946dd). ⚠️ VERIFY: no document in this set explicitly states why the goal titles changed between the 2020-2022 plan and the 2024 plan, nor whether a 2023-only interim plan existed.

Budget & mandate inflection points

  • 2023 total gross expenditure: $266,416,000 against $242,441,000 net program costs ($322 per capita, $922 per household) (3c11848513ce) — the only year in this series with a full financial-results table read. Patrol Operations ($88.5M budget) and Serious & Organized Crime ($44.6M) are the two largest program lines; Capital shows the largest percentage variance ($6.6M budget vs. $13.7M actual, a 2.7%-of-total overrun) (3c11848513ce). No comparable financial table is present in bp-2019, sp-2024, or ar-2024's captured content, so no multi-year budget trend is established by this archive alone.
  • 2023 budget added 41 new positions; 2024 budget added 76 new positions — ar-2023 states 41 additional positions were included in the 2023 budget (3c11848513ce); ar-2024's Chair's message states the 2024 budget added 76 new positions (cd8cec8946dd). Read together these describe an accelerating staffing-investment trend across the two years, though the two figures come from different documents' framing (one retrospective on 2023, one on the 2024 budget) rather than a single consistent series.
  • CSPA 2019 as a mandate-adjacent legislative inflection. ar-2024 frames the Community Safety and Policing Act, 2019 explicitly as redefining expectations ("accountability, responsiveness, and community engagement") and driving the Service's modernization agenda as "a legislative necessity," not merely a strategic choice (cd8cec8946dd) — this is the only document in the set that frames legislative change as a direct driver of strategic priorities.
  • Chief-of-Police transition, March 2023. Chief Rollauer's departure (retired early 2023, after a 3-year term) and Chief Peter Moreira's appointment (a former Toronto Police Service Staff Superintendent, DRPS's ninth Chief) is the single clearest leadership inflection point in the readable series, accompanied by two new Deputy Chiefs (Chris Kirkpatrick, Kim Yeandle) also recruited from the Toronto Police Service in Fall 2023 (3c11848513ce).

Ontario/Toronto relevance

Direct, as a GTA-adjacent upper-tier-region police service (Durham Region sits in the eastern GTA). Two specific Toronto linkages are established in the readable text: (1) the incoming Chief and both new Deputy Chiefs in 2023 were recruited directly from the Toronto Police Service (3c11848513ce); (2) DRPS jointly developed and launched a "Firearm Bail Dashboard" with the Toronto Police Service starting in 2022, later joined by the Ontario Provincial Police and Ottawa Police, tracking bail-compliance subjects (203 subjects by November 2022, growing to 555 by March 2024) (3c11848513ce). Beyond these two ties, the series describes DRPS as serving Durham Region specifically (population 753,500 in 2023, the "10th largest municipal police service in Canada") rather than any Toronto-specific mandate (3c11848513ce).
