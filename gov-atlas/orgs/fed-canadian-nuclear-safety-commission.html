Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission - backgrounder

Backgrounder / 2026-07-30 / registry row: fed-canadian-nuclear-safety-commission (this library's government-document registry) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

The Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission (CNSC) is established as "a body corporate" under s.8 of the Nuclear Safety and Control Act, S.C. 1997, c. 9 (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/n-28.3/page-1.html, fetched and verified; assented March 20, 1997; registry row fed-canadian-nuclear-safety-commission). Section 9 sets its objects: regulate the development, production and use of nuclear energy and nuclear substances, equipment and information to prevent unreasonable risk to health, safety, security and the environment, and to disseminate objective scientific, technical and regulatory information to the public (same source).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

Under s.9, CNSC licenses and inspects all nuclear facilities and activities in Canada — power plants, uranium mines and mills, research reactors, and medical/industrial/academic use of radioactive material — and regulates to meet Canada's international nuclear non-proliferation obligations (same source).

Governance & reporting line

An independent, quasi-judicial administrative tribunal and court of record; up to seven permanent members are appointed by the Governor in Council, and one is designated President (https://www.cnsc-ccsn.gc.ca/eng/the-commission/, fetched, and WebSearch of CNSC "Commission members" pages). CNSC states it "is independent of, but not isolated from, government" and reports to Parliament through the Minister of Natural Resources (now Energy and Natural Resources) (same sources).

Budget scale

Planned spending rising from $199.8M (2025-26) to $207.3M (2026-27); financed through voted parliamentary appropriations plus a statutory revenue-spending authority letting CNSC spend most licence-fee revenue (cost-recovery model for regulated activities, with voted funding covering fee-exempt activities like hospitals/universities and public/international obligations), per The Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission's 2026–27 Departmental Plan (registry strategy_current_url: https://www.cnsc-ccsn.gc.ca/eng/corporate/plans-results/rpp/dp-2026-2027/).

Institutional history

Established March 20, 1997 by the Nuclear Safety and Control Act (long title: "An Act to establish the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission"), replacing the Atomic Energy Control Board that had regulated nuclear activities since 1946 (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/n-28.3/page-1.html, fetched).

Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission - strategy evolution

2026-08-02 / registry: fed-canadian-nuclear-safety-commission / grounded in archived copies (cited document id + sha256) / read through our research file for that body

TL;DR: The biggest priority addition across the readable record is small modular reactors (SMRs): absent as a named priority through 2018, headline by 2019-20, and by 2024-25 backed by a $50M (2022-2027) federal readiness investment and Canada's first reactor construction licence in over 50 years (OPG's Darlington New Nuclear Project, BWRX-300, ar-2025 c9b76519c1cd). The biggest quietly dropped element is the CNSC's own "4 strategic priorities" framing (modern/trusted/global/agile), used continuously from at least 2018-19 through 2023-24, which contracted to "2 overarching themes" (trusted, modern) in the 2024-25 Departmental Plan before the 4-pillar framing was restored in the 2025-26 Departmental Plan — an unexplained one-year wobble in how the organization presents its own strategy. The most load-bearing number is total actual spending of $202.4 million in 2024-25 (up from $143.5 million in 2021-22), driven substantially by SMR-readiness hiring (694 nuclear-regulation FTEs in 2024-25, planned to reach 762 by 2027-28). One open question: the outcome of the Federal Court's February 2025 direction that the CNSC and Canadian Nuclear Laboratories resume UNDRIP-consistent consultation with Kebaowek First Nation on the Chalk River Near Surface Disposal Facility, due back to the Court by September 30, 2026, unresolved within the archived series.

Backgrounder summary

The CNSC is an independent, quasi-judicial administrative tribunal and court of record established March 20, 1997 by the Nuclear Safety and Control Act (S.C. 1997, c. 9), replacing the Atomic Energy Control Board (regulating since 1946). Under s.9 it licenses and inspects all nuclear facilities and activities in Canada — power plants, uranium mines and mills, research reactors, and medical/industrial/academic radioactive-material use — to prevent unreasonable risk to health, safety, security and the environment, and to disseminate objective scientific/technical/regulatory information to the public. Up to seven Governor-in-Council-appointed permanent members sit on the Commission; it reports to Parliament through the Minister of Natural Resources (renamed Energy and Natural Resources during the archived period). Planned spending for 2025-26 was $199.8 million rising to $207.3 million by 2026-27, financed mostly through licence-fee cost recovery plus voted appropriations for fee-exempt activities (backgrounder, our research file for that body).

Priority evolution

2017-18 (ar-2018, 0142e37e7841), Michael Binder's final report as President after 10+ years: No explicit numbered "strategic priorities" list; the report frames the CNSC's decade under Binder around the post-Fukushima (2011) safety-culture review and an "Integrated Regulatory Review Service" international assessment. Licence renewals are underway for Point Lepreau, Chalk River, and McClean Lake; the Pickering licence-renewal hearing (Part 1) is held in April 2018. SMRs appear only as a "pre-licensing vendor design review" list of eight vendor concepts (Terrestrial Energy, NuScale, Ultra Safe/Global First Power, Westinghouse, LeadCold, Advanced Reactor Concepts, URENCO, Moltex, SMR LLC/Holtec, StarCore) — a technical-review-pipeline item, not a headline strategic priority.

2018-19 (ar-2019, a2b57f61591f), Rumina Velshi's first report as President (appointed Aug 2018): First explicit statement of CNSC's "four key priorities": (1) a modern approach to nuclear regulation, (2) being a trusted regulator, (3) maintaining global nuclear influence, (4) improving management effectiveness. Global First Power's SMR site-preparation licence application at Chalk River is submitted (March 2019) and is the first live SMR licensing file named in the series.

2019-20 (ar-2020, 246f98d085f2): The fourth priority is renamed from "management effectiveness" to "to be an agile organization" — the CNSC's own report frames the four priorities as "modern / trusted / global / agile," the framing that recurs through 2023-24. A "regulatory readiness strategy" for advanced reactors is developed; COVID-19 hits at fiscal year-end (business continuity plan activated March 15, 2020).

2020-21 (ar-2021, d573b47cf76e): Same four-priority framing retained under pandemic conditions; Canada's SMR Action Plan is released by the Minister of Natural Resources; "Project Athena," a comprehensive strategic/organizational review, begins (initiated 2019, expected completion 2021-22). CNSC becomes first federal regulator in Canada to require pre-placement/random drug-and-alcohol testing for safety-critical positions.

2021-22 (ar-2022, 05409ec69c74), CNSC's 75th-anniversary report: Same four priorities retained. First construction-adjacent SMR milestone: OPG selects BWRX-300 technology for the Darlington site (Dec 2021), described as the first grid-scale SMR project in Canada and in a G7 nation. Bruce Power discloses elevated hydrogen-isotope uptake in pressure tubes (July 2021), prompting special public Commission hearings and an External Advisory Committee on Pressure Tubes. Project Athena concludes with an Executive Team workshop and review of 600+ staff suggestions.

2022-23 (ar-2023/drr-2023, ba196fd4a6e9/beb98879f779), Velshi's final report as President (5-year term ends 2023): Same four priorities retained. OPG's Darlington New Nuclear Project application — the CNSC's first licence-to-construct application in 30 years — is received. The Transformation Management Office (TMO) is created to drive the post-Project-Athena transformation agenda. The Office of the Auditor General's 2022-23 audit of low- and intermediate-level radioactive waste management returns positive findings.

2023-24 (ar-2024/drr-2024/dp-2024, 2f31a11ba6bc/cfe11d9a79a5/bfcb40971fc3): dp-2024 (planning document, published under Velshi) retains and explicitly names "the CNSC's 4 strategic priorities: modern, trusted, global, agile." The 2023-24 annual report, however — published under Ramzi Jammal as Acting CEO after Velshi's term ended — drops the four-priority narrative framing entirely and reports strictly against the Treasury-Board-standard Departmental Results Framework (Result 1-4 indicator tables), the first year this format supersedes the priority narrative in the annual report itself. Substantively: the Commission authorizes construction of the Near Surface Disposal Facility (NSDF) at Chalk River (Jan 2024); Darlington Unit 3's final refurbishment hold point is lifted (July 2023); Bruce Unit 6's fourth hold point is lifted (Sept 2023).

2024-25 (ar-2025/drr-2025, c9b76519c1cd/0cf59ec24b0f), Pierre Tremblay's first report as President and CEO: The four-priority framing (modern/trusted/global/agile) returns to the annual report's "Key priorities" section, but the CNSC's own 2024-25 Departmental Plan (dp-2025, 9ec1baeeef1d) had organized the same year's plans under only "2 overarching themes: to be trusted and to be modern" — a contraction not explained in either document. Substantively, this is the highest-density year in the readable record: the Commission issues Canada's first nuclear power reactor construction licence in more than 50 years (OPG's Darlington New Nuclear Project, GE Hitachi BWRX-300); Darlington Unit 1's final refurbishment milestone is met (Nov 2024); the Federal Court rules (Feb 2025) that the Commission erred in not directly considering UNDRIP's free-prior-informed-consent principle in its Kebaowek First Nation consultation on the Chalk River NSDF, and orders resumed consultation by September 30, 2026; a CNSC position paper distinguishes fusion-energy regulation from fission-energy regulation as a discrete future workstream.

2025-26 (dp-2026, d6a6657ff52e): The four-priority framing (now called "4 pillars: modern, trusted, global, agile") is restored in full, alongside a UNDRIP Act Action Plan commitment and continued SMR/fusion regulatory-readiness language.

Priorities added, dropped, renamed

  • Added — small modular reactors (SMRs) as a headline priority: present only as a technical vendor-review pipeline in ar-2018 (0142e37e7841); becomes an explicit strategic-priority element from ar-2020 (246f98d085f2) onward, reaching an operational milestone with Canada's first reactor construction licence in 50+ years in ar-2025 (c9b76519c1cd).
  • Added — fusion energy as a distinct regulatory workstream: first named via a 2022 white paper and a "Fusion Coordination Team" (dp-2024, bfcb40971fc3; ar-2024, 2f31a11ba6bc); by ar-2025 (c9b76519c1cd) the CNSC has published a position paper stating fusion cannot be regulated the same way as fission given differing risk profiles.
  • Added — artificial intelligence readiness: first appears as a contracted research paper in dp-2024/ar-2024 (bfcb40971fc3/2f31a11ba6bc); by ar-2025 (c9b76519c1cd) the CNSC has co-published a trilateral AI white paper with the US NRC and UK ONR and leads an industry AI-deployment roadmap.
  • Added — Indigenous reconciliation strategy with named strategic pillars: ar-2021 (d573b47cf76e) is the first report to name a 5-pillar Indigenous Reconciliation Strategy (modernizing consultation, strengthening governance, cultural competency, integrating Indigenous knowledge, reducing participation barriers); the Indigenous and Stakeholder Capacity Fund is created in ar-2023/2022-23 (ba196fd4a6e9) and by ar-2025 (c9b76519c1cd) is operationally tied to a Federal Court order (Kebaowek First Nation/UNDRIP).
  • Renamed — the fourth of the "four strategic priorities": "improve management effectiveness" (ar-2019, a2b57f61591f) is renamed "to be an agile organization" starting ar-2020 (246f98d085f2); the "agile" label is retained through ar-2025/dp-2026.
  • Quietly dropped, then restored — the "four strategic priorities" framing itself: continuously present (as "modern/trusted/global/agile") from ar-2019 (a2b57f61591f) through dp-2024 (bfcb40971fc3); dp-2025 (9ec1baeeef1d) compresses this to "2 overarching themes: to be trusted and to be modern," with "global" and "agile" absent as named top-level themes for that one planning document; the 2023-24 annual report (2f31a11ba6bc), published the same season under Acting CEO Jammal, also drops the priority-narrative framing in favour of the bare Departmental Results Framework. Neither document explains the change. dp-2026 (d6a6657ff52e) restores the full four-pillar framing. No CNSC document in this series names a reason for either the contraction or the restoration.
  • Quietly dropped — the "improving management effectiveness" personal-goal language (Binder-era STEM promotion commitment, ar-2019 a2b57f61591f) gives way to a more institutionalized "Women in STEM (WISTEM)" initiative from ar-2020 (246f98d085f2) onward, eventually formalized as a recurring "STEM Workshop for Indigenous Girls" (first held per ar-2024, 2f31a11ba6bc; second annual iteration per ar-2025, c9b76519c1cd).
  • Leadership discontinuity, not a stated priority change: Michael Binder (President through Aug 2018) → Rumina Velshi (Aug 2018-2023) → Ramzi Jammal (Acting CEO, reporting drr-2024/ar-2024, 2f31a11ba6bc) → Pierre Tremblay (President and CEO from ar-2025, c9b76519c1cd). The reports do not explain the Acting-CEO interval's duration.

Budget & mandate inflection points

  • Feb 2015 (retrospective, ar-2018 0142e37e7841): NRU reactor shutdown announced (executed March 2018).
  • 2022 federal budget: $50.7M (later reported as $50M/2022-2027 in ar-2024/ar-2025) allocated to strengthen CNSC's capacity to regulate SMRs and advanced reactors (dp-2024, bfcb40971fc3; ar-2024, 2f31a11ba6bc; ar-2025, c9b76519c1cd).
  • July 2023 / Sept 2023: Final refurbishment hold points lifted for Darlington Unit 3 and Bruce Unit 6 respectively (ar-2024, 2f31a11ba6bc).
  • January 2024: Commission authorizes construction of the Near Surface Disposal Facility at Chalk River (ar-2024, 2f31a11ba6bc).
  • 2023-24 fiscal year: Total actual spending $170.0 million, up from $153.0 million in 2022-23 (ar-2024, 2f31a11ba6bc), driven by SMR-readiness FTE growth and the new Indigenous and Stakeholder Capacity Fund.
  • November 2024: Darlington Unit 1 refurbishment's final regulatory milestone met (ar-2025, c9b76519c1cd).
  • 2024-25 fiscal year: Commission issues the Darlington New Nuclear Project (BWRX-300) construction licence — "the first licence to construct a reactor in Canada in more than 50 years" (ar-2025, c9b76519c1cd) — coinciding with total actual spending rising to $202.4 million (nuclear-regulation FTEs: 694, up from 644 in 2023-24) (ar-2025, c9b76519c1cd).
  • February 2025: Federal Court ruling on Kebaowek First Nation's judicial review of the Chalk River NSDF licence, finding the Commission erred in not directly considering UNDRIP's free-prior-informed-consent principle; consultation ordered resumed, due back to the Court by September 30, 2026 (ar-2025, c9b76519c1cd) — the single clearest unresolved thread in the archived series.
  • 2024-25 to 2025-26: "4 strategic priorities" framing contracts to "2 overarching themes" in dp-2025 (9ec1baeeef1d), then is restored as "4 pillars" in dp-2026 (d6a6657ff52e); no CNSC document explains either move.
  • 2025-26 to 2027-28 (planned, dp-2026/ar-2025): Planned spending continues rising ($199.7M → $202.7M → $206.0M) and nuclear-regulation FTEs continue rising (729 → 760 → 762), attributed to new industry (SMR) projects, partially offset by the government-wide "Refocusing Government Spending" reduction target ($1.354M in 2024-25 rising to $2.54M/year by 2026-27) (ar-2025, c9b76519c1cd).

Ontario/Toronto relevance

Ontario/Toronto-region relevance is genuinely direct and load-bearing across this entire series, not a boilerplate mention. The CNSC maintains permanent site offices at the Pickering, Darlington, Bruce, and Chalk River sites, plus a Mississauga regional office (ar-2019, a2b57f61591f). Substantively: - Pickering Nuclear Generating Station (Ontario Power Generation, Pickering): licence renewed for 10 years in Dec 2018 (ar-2019, a2b57f61591f); OPG has repeatedly sought and received Commission authorization to extend commercial operation past originally planned shutdown dates — to Sept 30, 2026 as of ar-2023 (ba196fd4a6e9), pending further OPG applications through ar-2025. This is an active, recurring regulatory file across nearly every year of the series, not a passing mention. - Darlington Nuclear Generating Station (OPG, Clarington/Bowmanville, GTA): subject of a multi-year refurbishment programme tracked hold-point-by-hold-point across ar-2019 through ar-2025 (a2b57f61591f through c9b76519c1cd); Unit 2 resumed commercial operation in 2020, Unit 3's final hold point lifted July 2023, Unit 1's final milestone met Nov 2024 (ar-2024/ar-2025). Darlington is also the site of the Darlington New Nuclear Project — Canada's first SMR construction licence (BWRX-300), issued in the 2024-25 reporting year (ar-2025, c9b76519c1cd) — and of new medical-isotope (molybdenum-99, cobalt-60) production licensing (ar-2023/ar-2024/ar-2025). - Bruce Nuclear Generating Station (Bruce Power, Kincardine, Ontario — outside the GTA but within the province): major-component-replacement programme tracked across the same years; the 2021 pressure-tube hydrogen-uptake discovery triggered special public hearings and sector-wide CNSC notices (ar-2022, 05409ec69c74). - Chalk River Laboratories (Canadian Nuclear Laboratories, Deep River, Ontario): site of the Near Surface Disposal Facility (construction licence authorized Jan 2024, ar-2024), the Global First Power SMR site-preparation application, and — as of ar-2025 (c9b76519c1cd) — the subject of the Federal Court's Kebaowek First Nation/UNDRIP ruling. - Other Ontario sites named across the series: Port Hope and Port Granby (historic low-level waste, Municipality of Clarington and Town of Port Hope), the Nuclear Power Demonstration reactor (Rolphton), and BWXT Nuclear Energy Canada's Toronto and Peterborough fuel-fabrication facilities (ar-2018/ar-2019, 0142e37e7841/a2b57f61591f). The record does not establish the CNSC operating a facility inside the City of Toronto itself (headquarters is Ottawa; the Toronto-adjacent presence is BWXT's licensed facility and the Mississauga regional office) — Toronto/GTA relevance runs through Pickering, Darlington, and the licensees operating in the wider GTA/Ontario power-generation footprint, which the record treats as central, recurring regulatory business rather than incidental geography.
