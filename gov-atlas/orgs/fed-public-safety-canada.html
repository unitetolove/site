Public Safety Canada - backgrounder

Backgrounder / 2026-07-30 / registry row: fed-public-safety-canada (this library's government-document registry) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

Public Safety Canada is governed by the Department of Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness Act (S.C. 2005, c. 10), consolidated and current to 2026-06-14 at laws-lois.justice.gc.ca (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-31.55/, fetched and verified directly). Section 2 establishes the Department; section 4 sets out the Minister's powers, duties and functions (same source). The Department's own Departmental Plan also names the Emergency Management Act (2007) as enabling legislation (https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/dprtmntl-pln-2026-27/index-en.aspx, fetched directly).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

Public Safety Canada develops policy, legislation, and programs to help Canada respond to national security threats, support community crime response, build resilience, and strengthen emergency management (https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/dprtmntl-pln-2026-27/index-en.aspx). Its 2026-27 Departmental Plan lists three core responsibilities: National Security ($44.2M), Community Safety ($938.8M), and Emergency Management ($1.18B) (same source). The Department coordinates the Public Safety Portfolio's partner agencies (CBSA, CSIS, Correctional Service of Canada, Parole Board of Canada, RCMP) and three independent review bodies (Civilian Review and Complaints Commission for the RCMP, Office of the Correctional Investigator, RCMP External Review Committee) (same source).

Governance & reporting line

The Department reports to the Minister of Public Safety (Gary Anandasangaree) and the Minister of Emergency Management (Eleanor Olszewski); its institutional head is Tricia Geddes (https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/dprtmntl-pln-2026-27/index-en.aspx). Portfolio partner agencies including CBSA and RCMP report through this same Public Safety Portfolio structure (same source).

Budget scale

~$2.25 billion total planned spending for 2026-27, with 1,600 planned full-time equivalents; the wider Public Safety Portfolio (Department plus five partner agencies and three review bodies) commands over $9 billion annually and more than 66,000 employees nationwide (Public Safety Canada 2026-27 Departmental Plan, https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/dprtmntl-pln-2026-27/index-en.aspx, doc-shelf n_docs: 2, doc_years 2023-2024).

Institutional history

Public Safety Canada was established in 2003 to coordinate federal departments and agencies responsible for national security and public safety, and now operates under the Department of Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness Act (2005) and the Emergency Management Act (2007) (https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/dprtmntl-pln-2026-27/index-en.aspx; statute confirmed at https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-31.55/).

Budget & mandate inflection points

  • Emergencies Act invoked February 14-23, 2022 — the central subject of the 2023 Commission report; Public Safety Canada's Deputy Minister (Rob Stewart) reported to the Cabinet Committee on Safety, Security, and Emergencies on February 3, 2022 that the Ottawa Police Service "would not be able to bring the protests to a conclusion without the assistance of the federal government," a meeting cited as part of the lead-up to invocation seventeen days before this report's letter of transmittal was written by the Public Order Emergency Commission's own Commissioner (fae142655230). Federal Public Safety Minister Marco Mendicino is separately named as a recipient of the Ottawa Police Services Board's public request for 1,800 additional officers (February 7, 2022) (fae142655230).
  • Commission Recommendation 29 — "The federal government should initiate a review to ensure that the federal government agencies with a responsibility for the collection or analysis of security intelligence are fully coordinated among themselves," aimed at minimizing duplication and improving information-sharing at the federal level (fae142655230) — a direct, if generic (not department-specific), recommendation bearing on Public Safety Canada's coordinating mandate.
  • First Nations and Inuit Policing Program (FNIPP) funding history: created 1991, updated 1996; new federal funding of $44.8M (2018, 4 years) plus $144.4M (2018, 5 years), then $540.3M plus $126.8M ongoing (2021, "to stabilize existing police services and to expand the program"), plus $43.7M over 5 years (2021) to co-develop legislation recognizing First Nations policing as an essential service (05cb2fb8b7be).
  • FNIPP expenditures rose from $155M (2018-19) to $242M (2022-23) combined RCMP + Public Safety Canada spending, but $13 million of 2022-23 program funds went unspent, and Public Safety Canada was "at risk of not disbursing over $45 million" of 2023-24 funds as of October 2023 (05cb2fb8b7be) — the OAG frames this as a critical management shortcoming despite the large funding increases, and notes this is a repeat finding from the OAG's prior 2014 audit of the same program.
  • RCMP staffing shortfall under FNIPP: in 2022-23, 61 of 467 funded community-tripartite-agreement officer positions remained vacant; only 38% of a 26-community sample had RCMP detachments reporting officers could spend 100% of their time on the dedicated community role (05cb2fb8b7be).
  • OAG recommendation: given "long-standing issues" and the federal government's reconciliation commitments, Public Safety Canada should work with First Nations, Inuit communities, provinces/territories, and the RCMP "to develop and implement a renewed approach to the program" (05cb2fb8b7be).

Ontario/Toronto relevance

Indirect for both documents, on different bases. The 2023 Commission report discusses Ontario extensively but centred on Ottawa (not Toronto) — the Ambassador Bridge blockade (Windsor) and other Southern Ontario border-crossing protests are covered in dedicated sections, and "Toronto" appears only once, listed among "Protests in other locations" as a brief regional mention, not as a site of sustained narrative focus (fae142655230). The 2024 OAG report on FNIPP states self-administered police service agreements (one of the program's two agreement types) "are mostly found in Ontario and Quebec," and separately notes Ontario has First Nations/Inuit communities in higher population bands (8,000+ people) than most other provinces (05cb2fb8b7be) — this establishes the program operates in Ontario First Nations communities generally, but neither document identifies any Toronto-specific presence, program, or facility; Public Safety Canada's Ontario/Toronto relevance in this two-document window is provincial/program-level at most, not city-specific.
