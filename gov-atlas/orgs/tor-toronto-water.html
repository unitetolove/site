Toronto Water - backgrounder

Backgrounder / 2026-07-30 / registry row: tor-toronto-water (this library's government-document registry) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

Toronto Water is a non-statutory administrative division of the City of Toronto — it is not a distinct corporation or local board but a City service division created and organized under the City's general municipal powers in the City of Toronto Act, 2006, S.O. 2006, c. 11, Sched. A (https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/06c11 — fetched and verified, title confirmed). Its day-to-day regulatory authority is set out in Toronto Municipal Code Chapter 851, Water Supply, and Chapter 681, Sewers (https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/municode/1184_851.pdf and .../1184_681.pdf — both fetched and verified as live, current chapter text). ⚠️ still being checked whether a specific council-approved terms-of-reference or divisional by-law (beyond the Municipal Code chapters) further formalizes the division.

Roles, responsibilities & scope

Toronto Water is responsible for drinking-water treatment and supply, wastewater collection and treatment, and stormwater management across the city, operating roughly $90 billion in infrastructure assets (eight treatment plants, pumping stations, water/sewer mains, laboratories and yards) with about 1,700 staff, serving over 3.6 million residents and businesses in Toronto and parts of York and Peel regions (toronto.ca/services-payments/water-environment/toronto-water/, fetched). Seven business sections cover water-quality testing/regulatory compliance, wastewater collection and treatment, stormwater management/emergency response, capital planning and asset management, customer/meter service, and bylaw enforcement (same source, fetched).

Governance & reporting line

Toronto Water sits within the City's Infrastructure Services cluster, under the Deputy City Manager, Infrastructure Services (registry row: tor-toronto-water, parent_org field), and is led by a General Manager (Lou Di Gironimo, per toronto.ca/services-payments/water-environment/toronto-water/, fetched). As a division rather than a board, it has no independent governing body of its own and answers through the City's administrative hierarchy to City Council.

Budget scale

2026 approved operating budget: $542.4 million gross expenditures, funded by $1,654.1 million in water/wastewater rate revenue, with the $1,111.7 million net surplus applied to capital financing ("2026 Operating Budget for Toronto Water of $542.361 million gross, $1,654.083 million revenue and $1,111.722 million net") (2026 Operating Budget and 2026-2035 Capital Budget and Plan: Toronto Water, registry row: strategy_current_url, fetched).

Institutional history

Toronto Water was constituted as a City division in the post-2000 municipal-amalgamation reorganization of Toronto's public works functions rather than by a distinct founding statute; ⚠️ still being checked exact year/administrative instrument of the division's creation and any prior name (e.g., "Water and Wastewater Services") — not grounded this review beyond the current Municipal Code and division-page citations above.

Toronto Water - strategy evolution

2026-08-02 / registry: tor-toronto-water / grounded in archived copies (cited document id + sha256) / read through our research file for that body

TL;DR: The record is dominated by two long-running capital megaprojects — the Don River and Central Waterfront Project (DR&CW, cost rising from "over $3.7 billion" in 2023 to an implied larger figure as the Basement Flooding Protection Program's identified local-sewer backlog reached $18.6 billion by end-2024 — of which $12 billion sits above the City's own $68,000-per-property funding threshold — and the biggest quietly-dropped priority is the stormwater charge, actively consulted on from 2020 through spring 2024 before being paused and then recommended for indefinite suspension in the January 2025 report, replaced by a smaller, incentive-based green infrastructure subsidy concept. The clearest load-bearing number is the count of remaining City-owned lead water services, which fell steadily across every year read — 20,700 (end 2022) → 19,400 (2023) → 15,835 (2024) → 13,664 (2025) — alongside the first-ever appearances of tap-water lead exceedances at the tighter 5 ppb Health Canada guideline (2024: two residential samples of ~1.7%; 2025: one sample, 2.1%), a threshold the division has never regulatorily exceeded but is edging toward as Ontario considers adopting it. An October 2025 Auditor General report separately found that 94% of a sampled set of Toronto Water sewer/wastewater state-of-good-repair contracts missed their planned completion dates and that required change-order and progress-payment documentation was missing in the majority of sampled cases. Open question: whether the Q3 2025 committee report-backs on green infrastructure incentives and expanded Basement Flooding Protection Subsidy Program (BFPSP) options — both promised in the January 2025 report — were adopted; nothing in this archived set post-dates that report on the incentive-design question.

Backgrounder summary

Toronto Water is a non-statutory City of Toronto administrative division (not a distinct corporation or board), operating under the City of Toronto Act, 2006 and Toronto Municipal Code Chapters 851 (Water Supply) and 681 (Sewers). It runs roughly $90 billion in infrastructure — eight treatment plants, pumping stations, watermains and sewers — with about 1,700 staff serving over 3.6 million people in Toronto and parts of York and Peel. It reports through the Deputy City Manager, Infrastructure Services to City Council and is led by General Manager Lou Di Gironimo, named consistently as report signatory across every document in this series. The backgrounder cites a 2026 approved operating budget of $542.4 million gross / $1,654.1 million revenue / $1,111.7 million net surplus applied to capital; none of the 18 archived documents in this review independently confirm those exact figures (⚠️ still being checked — not cross-checked here, as none of the 18 docs is a budget document).

Priority evolution

2022 (ar-2022 ff0f758ef4bd; sr-2022 12ac7145740e; sr-2022-2 6e67835d65d2; sr-2022-3 f41d8a49b8ae): Baseline year. The four regulatory Annual Reports show routine per-plant compliance (four water treatment plants — R.C. Harris, R.L. Clark, F.J. Horgan, Island — plus the distribution system), zero non-compliance incidents, and capital line items totalling roughly $226M across watermain replacement ($96.1M), watermain rehabilitation ($65.8M), water service replacement ($37.6M) and facility rehab/standby power ($22.6M) (ar-2022, ff0f758ef4bd). The Lead in Drinking Water Mitigation Strategy Annual Report shows 20,700 remaining City-owned lead water services (down from ~38,000 estimated in 2014 and ~65,000 in 2007), and zero regulated tap-water samples exceeding either the 10 ppb Ontario limit or the tighter 5 ppb Health Canada guideline then under MECP consideration (sr-2022-3, f41d8a49b8ae).

2023 (ar-2023 4cfcd1de08da; oth-2023 4d4668101dc1; sr-2023-3 096dc1c72027): The Wet Weather Flow Master Plan (WWFMP) Implementation Update — the only document in this series presenting a multi-decade strategic plan directly — reports the 2023-2032 Capital Budget and Plan allocating $4.3 billion to WWFMP implementation (on top of $1.88 billion already spent 2017-2022), names the Don River and Central Waterfront Project (DR&CW) as "the City's most significant water quality improvement initiative and one of the largest infrastructure projects of its kind in Canada" at an estimated cost "over $3.7 billion" over a 25-year build-out, and flags a new financial pressure: Ontario's Bill 23 (More Homes Built Faster Act, 2022) removed the City's ability to collect development charges for sewer infrastructure, an approximate $2.3 billion revenue loss over 10 years (oth-2023, 4d4668101dc1). Distribution-system capital spend for the year totalled roughly $209M (ar-2023, 4cfcd1de08da). Remaining lead services fall to 19,400 (sr-2023-3, 096dc1c72027).

2024 (ar-2024 bbab0168e08f; sr-2024-3 feb1bf4a132e; sr-2024-2 26cc2b914c7d): First appearance in this series of a tap-water lead sample exceeding the tighter 5 ppb Health Canada guideline: two residential samples (1.7% of samples collected) exceeded 5 ppb, though the regulated 10 ppb Ontario compliance standard remained fully met (sr-2024-3, feb1bf4a132e). Remaining lead services fall to 15,835 — the largest single-year drop in the series (sr-2024-3, feb1bf4a132e). The System Summary Report records the only non-N/A regulatory non-compliance entry found anywhere in this 18-document set: a 58-minute coagulation-module flow-meter outage on 08/08/2024, inspected and calibrated same-day (sr-2024-2, 26cc2b914c7d).

January 2025 (oth-2025 81f8c96d9371): The "Reducing Stormwater Runoff and Mitigating Basement Flooding" report is the clearest single inflection-point document in the series. It closes out a multi-year stormwater-charge and water-service-charge consultation process (first round late 2022, public round paused April 2024) by recommending City Council "indefinitely suspend" further consideration of both charges, redirecting policy energy instead toward a new, smaller-scale green infrastructure incentive subsidy (up to $5,000/property) and expanded Basement Flooding Protection Subsidy Program options, with committee report-backs promised "no later than Q3 2025." It also discloses that all 67 Basement Flooding Protection Program (BFPP) environmental-assessment study areas were completed as of December 2024, and that the total identified BFPP local-sewer project backlog stands at $18.6 billion, of which only $5.5 billion clears the City's own $68,000-cost-per-benefitting-property funding threshold — at the 2024-2033 capital plan's ~$113M/year average local-project spend, staff estimate roughly 50 years to construct the projects that already qualify (oth-2025, 81f8c96d9371).

2025 (ar-2025 a294dd66d22d; sr-2025-2 15cb6d188bf9; oth-2025-2 10af13b54958): Remaining lead services fall to 13,664; one residential sample (2.1%) now exceeds the 5 ppb guideline (sr-2025-2, 15cb6d188bf9). An October 2025 Auditor General audit of Toronto Water's stormwater and wastewater state-of-good-repair contract management — the most critical document in the series — finds 94% (17 of 18) of sampled sewer/forcemain/pump-station SOGR projects completed January 2020-March 2025 missed their originally planned completion date; $3.11 million was paid in additional consultant fees/escalation costs due to delays with a further $5 million in dispute; $122,000 was found in consultant-invoice mark-up overpayments; 57% of sampled change orders and 91% of sampled progress payments lacked required supporting documentation. The audit issued 12 recommendations (oth-2025-2, 10af13b54958).

Priorities added, dropped, renamed

  • Added (disclosed, not newly created) — green infrastructure incentive program for private property: absent as a named program from the 2022-2023 record; proposed as a new $5,000-per-property subsidy concept in the January 2025 report, explicitly positioned as the policy substitute for the shelved stormwater charge (oth-2025, 81f8c96d9371).
  • Dropped/suspended — stormwater charge and water service charge: actively consulted on 2020-2021 (industrial/commercial-only version) and 2022 through spring 2024 (all-property version, paused April 2024); the January 2025 report recommends indefinite suspension of both, citing public-consultation cost concerns (79% of surveyed homeowners worried about utility-bill impact) and overlap with the new green infrastructure incentive concept (oth-2025, 81f8c96d9371).
  • Escalating — lead water service replacement: not a new priority, but its scale and framing sharpen across the series as the remaining-services count nearly halves (20,700 → 13,664, 2022-2025) while regulated exceedances remain at zero but non-regulated 5 ppb exceedances appear for the first time in 2024-2025 — the reports do not yet reframe this as a compliance risk, but the trend is directionally toward one if Ontario adopts the 5 ppb guideline (sr-2022-3 through sr-2025-2).
  • Renamed — Lead Service Replacement program table structure: the "Planned (Capital Project) Water Service Replacement" figures are revised retroactively in each subsequent year's report (e.g., 2022's planned/capital watermain-linked replacements are reported as 230 in the 2023 report but 1,115-1,168 in the 2024 and 2025 reports), attributed in-report to "service cards received in batches" and incorporated only "to date" as of each report's cutoff — see Residuals & gaps.
  • No evidence of a dropped priority for the DR&CW project or BFPP — both remain continuously described as top capital priorities across every year read, with cost figures escalating rather than being cut ($4.3B WWFMP 2023-2032 plan in oth-2023 vs. $4.5B 2024-2033 forecast referenced in oth-2025).

Budget & mandate inflection points

  • 2023-2032 Capital Budget and Plan — $4.3 billion allocated to WWFMP implementation, on top of $1.88 billion already spent 2017-2022 (oth-2023, 4d4668101dc1).
  • April 2024 — second-round public consultation on the stormwater charge and water service charge paused, pending alignment with the City's broader climate resilience strategy, a proposed commercial parking levy, and the long-term financial plan (oth-2025, 81f8c96d9371).
  • July 2024 — City Council direction (2024.MM20.24) following July-August 2024 flooding events, requesting the review that produced the January 2025 report (oth-2025, 81f8c96d9371).
  • December 2024 — all 67 Basement Flooding Protection Program environmental-assessment study areas reported complete (oth-2025, 81f8c96d9371).
  • January 2025 — staff recommend indefinite suspension of both the stormwater charge and the water service charge; BFPP local-sewer backlog disclosed at $18.6 billion ($12B+ above the City's own funding threshold) (oth-2025, 81f8c96d9371).
  • October 2025 — Auditor General audit finds systemic SOGR contract-management and documentation failures across Toronto Water's stormwater/wastewater capital delivery (oth-2025-2, 10af13b54958).

Ontario/Toronto relevance

Direct. Toronto Water is a City of Toronto division; every document in this series is Toronto-specific by construction — treatment plants, distribution mains, and capital programs are all located within (or, for the R.C. Harris/R.L. Clark/F.J. Horgan/Island plants and the DR&CW project's Ashbridges Bay component specifically, physically sited inside) the City of Toronto, with limited water supply extending into southern York Region and (per the backgrounder) parts of Peel Region. The Wet Weather Flow Master Plan and Basement Flooding Protection Program operate under the Toronto and Region Remedial Action Plan / Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement framework, coordinating with the Toronto and Region Conservation Authority (TRCA) on Toronto-specific watercourses (Don River, Humber, Mimico Creek, Black Creek, etc.) and City-owned assets. No provincial or federal body appears as more than a funding/regulatory counterparty (MECP as regulator; federal Disaster Mitigation and Adaptation Fund as a co-funder of BFPP projects) — Toronto Water itself is the direct municipal service delivery body throughout.
