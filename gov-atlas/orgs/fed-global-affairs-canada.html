Global Affairs Canada - backgrounder

Backgrounder / 2026-07-30 / registry row: fed-global-affairs-canada (this library's government-document registry) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

The Department is continued by section 2 ("Continuation of the Department") of the Department of Foreign Affairs, Trade and Development Act, S.C. 2013, c. 33, s. 174, confirmed current to 2026-06-14 and last amended 2025-06-26 on Justice Laws (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/F-27.5/). Section 10 addresses the "Powers, Duties and Functions of the Minister" governing the department's conduct of foreign affairs, trade and international-development responsibilities (DFATD Act, s. 10, Justice Laws).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

Global Affairs Canada leads Canada's diplomatic relations, international trade, and international development policy and delivery, per its own 2026-27 Departmental Plan (international.canada.ca/en/global-affairs/corporate/reports/departmental-plan/global-affairs-2026-2027-departmental-plan); the registry notes its rss_url is a feed directory page rather than a raw feed link (registry row: fed-global-affairs-canada, notes field).

Governance & reporting line

The Department reports to four ministers: Anita Anand, Minister of Foreign Affairs; Maninder Sidhu, Minister of International Trade; Dominic LeBlanc, President of the King's Privy Council for Canada (Canada-U.S. trade responsibilities); and Randeep Sarai, Secretary of State (International Development) (2026-27 Departmental Plan, international.canada.ca/en/global-affairs/corporate/reports/departmental-plan/global-affairs-2026-2027-departmental-plan).

Budget scale

Total planned spending for 2026-27, including internal services, is $7,224,120,822 (2026-27 Departmental Plan, international.canada.ca/en/global-affairs/corporate/reports/departmental-plan/global-affairs-2026-2027-departmental-plan).

Institutional history

Global Affairs Canada's institutional lineage dates to 1909 and represents a 2013 merger of the Department of Foreign Affairs and International Trade (DFAIT) with the Canadian International Development Agency (CIDA), formalized under the Department of Foreign Affairs, Trade and Development Act, S.C. 2013, c. 33, s. 174 (2026-27 Departmental Plan, international.canada.ca; Justice Laws, https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/F-27.5/).

Backgrounder summary

Global Affairs Canada is continued by section 2 of the Department of Foreign Affairs, Trade and Development Act, S.C. 2013, c. 33, s. 174, with s. 10 setting out the Minister's powers, duties and functions over foreign affairs, trade and international-development responsibilities; the department's institutional lineage dates to 1909 and reflects a 2013 merger of the Department of Foreign Affairs and International Trade (DFAIT) with the Canadian International Development Agency (CIDA). It leads Canada's diplomatic relations, international trade and international-development policy and delivery, and as of the 2026-27 Departmental Plan reports to four ministers — Anita Anand (Foreign Affairs), Maninder Sidhu (International Trade), Dominic LeBlanc (President of the King's Privy Council, Canada-U.S. trade), and Randeep Sarai (Secretary of State, International Development) — with total planned 2026-27 spending of $7,224,120,822.

Priority evolution

2018-19 (dp-2019, 5c3dd932eb5f; drr-2019, 51f36615053f): The department's four stated priorities were: strengthening the rules-based international order (Canada's 2018 G7 presidency, campaign for a 2021-22 UN Security Council seat); advancing Canada's feminist foreign policy, implemented through the Feminist International Assistance Policy (FIAP); pursuing a "progressive trade agenda"; and maintaining constructive relations with the United States, dominated by NAFTA renegotiation, named as "one of the top priorities" in the Minister's mandate letter (5c3dd932eb5f). Ministers were Chrystia Freeland (Foreign Affairs), Marie-Claude Bibeau (International Development and La Francophonie) and François-Philippe Champagne (International Trade). Total actual spending for the year was $7,056,875,302 (51f36615053f).

2019-20 (dp-2020, bb962cc983fc; drr-2020, 35ecd7237f96): The four priorities were renamed and broadened: revitalizing the rules-based international order; eradicating poverty via FIAP, "released in June 2017"; pursuing "diversified, modern and inclusive trade"; and strengthening Canada's place in North America (broadened from "U.S. relations" to include Mexico, reflecting the new Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement, CUSMA). The portfolio also split: a fourth minister, Mélanie Joly (Tourism, Official Languages and La Francophonie), was added, and international development was paired with "Women and Gender Equality" under Maryam Monsef (bb962cc983fc). The department hosted the Women Deliver global conference in Vancouver (June 2019, 8,000 delegates) and launched the Equality Fund, alongside a $1.4-billion annual commitment to women's, children's and adolescent health (35ecd7237f96). At the outset of the COVID-19 pandemic the department created the COVID-19 Emergency Loan Program for Canadians Abroad (35ecd7237f96). Total actual spending was $7,176,903,198 (35ecd7237f96).

2020-21 (dp-2021, 0e043ce4f058; drr-2021, 49fa8b130d45): COVID-19 response dominated the year. Canada committed $940 million to the WHO-led Access to COVID-19 Tools (ACT) Accelerator and $220 million to the COVAX vaccine facility, and conducted what the department describes as "the largest repatriation effort in Canadian history," returning tens of thousands of Canadians from over 100 countries (49fa8b130d45). On February 15, 2021, Canada launched the Declaration Against Arbitrary Detention in State-to-State Relations, which had 60 country endorsements plus the EU by year-end (49fa8b130d45). The four stated priorities (rules-based international system, diversified trade, bilateral relations, eradicating poverty) continued largely unchanged in framing (0e043ce4f058). Total actual spending spiked to $9,341,706,896, the highest figure in the archived series (49fa8b130d45).

2021-22 (dp-2022, 4e6e50d99e1b; drr-2022, 18164a577de8): The department organized its COVID-19 response around three explicit pillars — fighting the pandemic, managing financial stress/stabilizing economies, and supporting the vulnerable/green recovery (4e6e50d99e1b). Planning language for a "new integrated approach to the Indo-Pacific region" first appears here, still at the development stage (4e6e50d99e1b). Following Russia's invasion of Ukraine (February 2022, within this fiscal year), Canada imposed sanctions on 645 individuals and 80 entities as of March 31, 2022 (18164a577de8). Total actual spending fell back to $8,074,210,761 (18164a577de8).

2022-23 (dp-2023, 22d0928cc4b2; drr-2023, 6e1a3ebadde7; oth-2023, ee8bb11fa77e): Canada launched its whole-of-government Indo-Pacific Strategy this year, backed by $1 million per year for Canadian business-chamber support and an additional frigate deployed to the region (6e1a3ebadde7). New internal initiatives appear: the Anti-Racism Strategy (2021-2026), the Action Plan on Reconciliation with Indigenous Peoples (2021-2025), and a new Cultural Diplomacy Strategy; a third National Action Plan on Women, Peace and Security was in development (22d0928cc4b2). Separately, the Auditor General tabled Report 4 (March 27, 2023) finding that GAC "was unable to show how the approximately $3.5 billion in bilateral development assistance it provides each year" improved outcomes for women and girls, citing incomplete project files and finding that 24 of 26 outcome indicators did not actually measure outcomes; the audit further found GAC missed two of FIAP's three spending commitments — the 50%-to-sub-Saharan-Africa target (actual: 45-49% across 2017-18 to 2021-22) and the 15%-gender-targeted-projects target (actual: 3-14%) — while meeting the 80%-gender-integrated-projects target (actual: 81-90%) (ee8bb11fa77e). Total actual spending was $9,254,203,627, later described by the department as including $732 million in COVID-19-related transfer payments (6e1a3ebadde7; e1709398f343).

2023-24 (dp-2024, ece78fe212f5; drr-2024, e1709398f343): The department began rolling out the "Future of Diplomacy: Transforming Global Affairs Canada" initiative, launched in May 2022, aimed at "modernizing operations and strengthening Canada's diplomatic capacity" (ece78fe212f5). Canada signed a long-term security cooperation agreement with Ukraine and co-chaired the International Coalition for the Return of Ukrainian Children; it also opened the NATO Climate Change and Security Centre of Excellence in Montréal (October 2023) and, in September 2024, signed a modernized Canada-Ukraine Free Trade Agreement (e1709398f343). GAC appointed its first special envoy for the Indo-Pacific and first Indo-Pacific trade representative, and chaired the CPTPP in 2024 (e1709398f343). The department "played its part in the Refocusing Government Spending initiative announced in Budget 2023" and reported achieving its expected reduction (e1709398f343). Total expenses decreased by $848 million (10%) versus 2022-23, explained by the absence of the prior year's $732 million in COVID-19 transfer payments and $440 million in sunsetting program payments, partly offset by $210 million in higher salary costs from renewed collective agreements (e1709398f343).

2024-25 (dp-2025, ae209b98724d — abbreviated "at a glance" plan only; drr-2025, 2fb17a97d8c1): GAC "launched Canada's 2025 G7 presidency" with over 300 domestic engagements to shape its priorities (2fb17a97d8c1). It responded to Russia's continued war on Ukraine by contributing to the Special Tribunal for the Crime of Aggression against Ukraine and to peace-summit diplomacy, and responded to the Israel-Hamas conflict with ceasefire calls, sanctions under the Special Economic Measures Act, and mine-action support in Gaza (2fb17a97d8c1). For the first time in the series, the record shows explicit Canada-U.S. trade friction: GAC "defended market access for key sectors such as steel, aluminum, automotive and softwood lumber, including by advancing legal challenges under CUSMA, before the [WTO] and in U.S. courts" (2fb17a97d8c1). The Refocusing Government Spending reductions continued and grew: $118,718,936 in 2024-25, rising to a planned $179,214,936 in 2025-26 and $243,374,436 annually from 2026-27, including sunsetting Middle East Strategy funding as of April 2025 (ae209b98724d). Total actual spending was $9,044,090,743, and Canada attracted $85.5 billion in foreign direct investment, described as the second-highest level in history (2fb17a97d8c1). By this report's tabling, the International Development portfolio is held by Randeep Sarai as Secretary of State (International Development) rather than as a full Minister — a change from the prior year's Departmental Results Report, which listed Ahmed Hussen as Minister of International Development (e1709398f343; 2fb17a97d8c1).

2025-26 (dp-2026, 5a0db2a2813d — abbreviated "at a glance" plan only): The four-priority structure collapses to three broader statements: promoting Canada's economy (market access, fair competition, supply-chain resilience); protecting security and prosperity from international security threats "in close cooperation with allies"; and contributing to international efforts on poverty, conflict and climate while promoting democracy and human rights (5a0db2a2813d). Canada's G7 presidency and the relationship with the United States are named as the year's "key focus areas." Notably, no "feminist" framing of any kind appears in this document — the first departmental plan in the archived series without it. Total planned spending (including internal services) was $8,437,090,075, with 13,235 planned FTEs (5a0db2a2813d).

2026-27 (dp-2027, 9af5cbccfd22): The department's Departmental Results Framework is explicitly and formally restructured, "streamlined from five core responsibilities, 16 departmental results, and 46 indicators to two core responsibilities, five departmental results, and 28 indicators" (9af5cbccfd22). The five long-standing core responsibilities collapse into "Advancing Canada's interests and addressing global challenges" (merging diplomacy, trade and development) and "Assisting Canadians abroad and supporting Canada's overseas presence" (merging consular services and mission-network support). New priority language centers on establishing "a new economic and security relationship with the United States," a Trade Diversification Strategy intended to "double Canada's non-U.S. exports within the next decade," and recalibrating international assistance "to advance shared economic priorities" while maintaining commitments to poverty reduction and gender equality (9af5cbccfd22). A Comprehensive Expenditure Review imposes deep, escalating cuts: $560,825,000 in 2026-27, rising to $747,033,000 in 2027-28 and $1,120,350,000 in 2028-29, with an anticipated reduction of roughly 1,240 FTEs by 2028-29 (9af5cbccfd22). Total planned spending for 2026-27 is $7,224,120,822 — matching the backgrounder figure and the lowest planned-spending total in the archived series — with 12,647 planned FTEs (9af5cbccfd22).

Priorities added, dropped, renamed

  • Added — Indo-Pacific Strategy: first appears as a planning intention in dp-2022 (4e6e50d99e1b, FY2021-22); launched as a whole-of-government strategy in FY2022-23 (dp-2023/drr-2023, 22d0928cc4b2/6e1a3ebadde7); deepens through a special envoy and trade representative in FY2023-24 (drr-2024, e1709398f343) and remains a named priority through dp-2027 (9af5cbccfd22).
  • Added — Ukraine as a sustained standing priority: begins as a sanctions response following the February 2022 invasion (drr-2022, 18164a577de8); escalates to a bilateral security cooperation agreement and a modernized free trade agreement (drr-2024, e1709398f343); reaches co-founding a Special Tribunal for the Crime of Aggression against Ukraine (drr-2025, 2fb17a97d8c1).
  • Added — "Future of Diplomacy" / "Transforming GAC" modernization initiative: launched May 2022 under the longer name "Future of Diplomacy: Transforming Global Affairs Canada" (dp-2024, ece78fe212f5); shortened to "Transforming GAC initiative" by dp-2025/drr-2025 (ae209b98724d; 2fb17a97d8c1).
  • Added, then dropped — Anti-Racism Strategy (2021-2026) and Action Plan on Reconciliation with Indigenous Peoples (2021-2025): appear as named internal priorities from dp-2022 (4e6e50d99e1b) through drr-2025 (2fb17a97d8c1); absent from both dp-2026 (5a0db2a2813d) and dp-2027 (9af5cbccfd22).
  • Dropped — "feminist foreign policy" / Feminist International Assistance Policy as organizing frame: present as the department's central rhetorical frame from dp-2019 (5c3dd932eb5f, FIAP "released in June 2017") continuously through drr-2025 (2fb17a97d8c1); the word "feminist" does not appear at all in dp-2026 (5a0db2a2813d) or dp-2027 (9af5cbccfd22), even though narrower "gender equality" commitments survive as a specific programming line in dp-2027 (9af5cbccfd22, "maintaining Canada's commitments to poverty reduction, gender equality").
  • Renamed/restructured — five Core Responsibilities collapse to two: "International Advocacy and Diplomacy," "Trade and Investment," "Development, Peace and Security Programming," "Help for Canadians Abroad" and "Support for Canada's Presence Abroad" persist unchanged from dp-2019 (5c3dd932eb5f) through dp-2026 (5a0db2a2813d); dp-2027 (9af5cbccfd22) merges them into "Advancing Canada's interests and addressing global challenges" and "Assisting Canadians abroad and supporting Canada's overseas presence."
  • Renamed/downgraded — International Development portfolio rank: held by a full Cabinet Minister of International Development continuously from dp-2019 (5c3dd932eb5f, Bibeau) through drr-2024 (e1709398f343, Hussen); appears as "Secretary of State (International Development)" (Sarai) by drr-2025 (2fb17a97d8c1) and dp-2027 (9af5cbccfd22) — a change in rank within the same functional portfolio, with no document in the archived series stating the reason or exact date.
  • Added — explicit "Canada-U.S. Trade" ministerial responsibility: first appears in dp-2027 (9af5cbccfd22), where Dominic LeBlanc's title expands to "President of the King's Privy Council for Canada and Minister responsible for Canada-U.S. Trade, Intergovernmental Affairs, Internal Trade and One Canadian Economy" — paired with drr-2025's (2fb17a97d8c1) first explicit mentions of CUSMA legal disputes over steel, aluminum, automotive and softwood lumber.
  • Quietly dropped — UN Security Council 2021-22 seat campaign: named as an active priority in dp-2019, dp-2020 and dp-2021 (5c3dd932eb5f; bb962cc983fc; 0e043ce4f058); does not appear again in dp-2022 (4e6e50d99e1b) or any later document in the series, with no stated outcome.

Ontario/Toronto relevance

The archived series does not establish any Toronto-specific footprint for Global Affairs Canada, and the word "Toronto" does not appear in any of the 17 documents read. The department's headquarters is in Ottawa (125 Sussex Drive, per dp-2023, 22d0928cc4b2), and its Ontario references in this series are incidental to national programming rather than evidence of a Toronto or GTA office or initiative: the Trade Commissioner Service is credited with facilitating Volkswagen's $7-billion EV battery plant investment in St. Thomas, Ontario (drr-2023, 6e1a3ebadde7), the University of Western Ontario appears as a partner on a Rwanda health-programming project (drr-2020, 35ecd7237f96), and Ottawa hosted the Caribbean Development Bank's 54th Annual Meeting (drr-2025, 2fb17a97d8c1). Given that Global Affairs Canada's core work is a globally distributed mission network and Ottawa-based policy function rather than a regionally delivered domestic program, the absence of a Toronto-specific footprint in this record is unsurprising and should be reported honestly rather than stretched into local relevance.
