Integrity Commissioner - backgrounder

Backgrounder / 2026-07-30 / registry row: tor-integrity-commissioner (this library's government-document registry) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

The Office of the Integrity Commissioner (OIC) is a statutory accountability office. The City of Toronto Act, 2006, Part V ("Accountability and Transparency"), ss. 158-159, requires City Council to appoint an Integrity Commissioner (statute link verified by direct fetch, table of contents confirmed: https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/06c11). The Toronto Municipal Code, Chapter 3 ("Accountability Officers"), Article 4 (§§3-4.1 to 3-4.5), sets the Commissioner's term, powers/duties/limitations under Part V, responsibilities, and complaint procedures (verified by direct fetch: https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/municode/1184_003.pdf).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

The OIC provides "advice, education, policy recommendations, and complaint resolution to City Council, local boards, their members, and the public" on the City's Codes of Conduct, the Municipal Conflict of Interest Act, and related bylaws/policies/legislation governing ethical behaviour (2026 Budget Summary, https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/95ee-2026-Public-Book-OIC-V1.pdf). It is one of the City's four Accountability Officers, alongside the Auditor General, Lobbyist Registrar and Ombudsman (same source; Municipal Code Ch. 3 structure, above).

Governance & reporting line

The Integrity Commissioner is an independent officer appointed by, and reporting directly to, City Council rather than sitting within the City Administration management hierarchy (COTA 2006 s.158; Municipal Code Ch.3 Art.1, "Independence and accountability," §3-1.2, verified by direct fetch). The current Commissioner is Paul Muldoon; the Office is located at 375 University Ave., Suite 202 (org page: https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/accountability-operations-customer-service/accountability-officers/integrity-commissioner/).

Budget scale

~$0.873M gross 2026 operating budget; $0.773M net; no capital budget/plan (2026 Budget Summary: Office of the Integrity Commissioner, https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/95ee-2026-Public-Book-OIC-V1.pdf).

Institutional history

The office's statutory basis is COTA 2006 (in force since Jan. 1, 2007), ss.158-159 (verified above). ⚠️ still being checked - the registry's doc-shelf lists annual-report documents spanning 2005-2024 (20 docs, tor-integrity-commissioner), which predates COTA 2006's coming into force and suggests Toronto's Integrity Commissioner function may have existed earlier under prior municipal authority; the exact original appointment date/instrument was not grounded this review.

Office of the Integrity Commissioner (Toronto) - strategy evolution

2026-08-01 / registry: tor-integrity-commissioner / grounded in archived copies (cited document id + sha256) / read through our research file for that body

TL;DR: The biggest priority addition is MCIA advice-and-complaint jurisdiction under Bill 68 (effective March 1, 2019), the largest single expansion in the office's history, extending it from Code-of-Conduct-only oversight to statutory conflict-of-interest enforcement across every Ontario municipality. The most notable dropped/unimplemented item is the 2015 Sossin external review's recommendation to cross-appoint the Integrity Commissioner and Lobbyist Registrar, referred for further study and never adopted. The load-bearing number is the office's budget growth from a $90,000 part-time founding allocation (2004) to $730,730 by 2024, with a full-time Commissioner only established in September 2014. The open question the brief flags most directly: the year-over-year budget drop from $762,406.78 (2020) to $639,670.85 (2021) is stated as fact in both source documents but never explained.

Backgrounder summary

The Office of the Integrity Commissioner (OIC) is a City of Toronto statutory accountability office. City Council appointed the position by resolution in March 2004, and the first Commissioner assumed duties September 1, 2004 — this predates the City of Toronto Act, 2006 (COTA), Part V, ss.158-159, which put the office on a statutory footing effective January 1, 2007. The Municipal Code Chapter 3 ("Accountability Officers") sets the Commissioner's term, powers and complaint procedures. The OIC provides advice, education, policy recommendations and complaint resolution on the City's Codes of Conduct, the Municipal Conflict of Interest Act (MCIA) and related bylaws, for members of Council and — since 2006-2007 — members of local and adjudicative boards. It is one of the City's four Accountability Officers alongside the Auditor General, Lobbyist Registrar and Ombudsman, reporting directly to Council rather than through the City Manager. The current (2026) gross operating budget is ~$0.873M; this brief traces the budget from its $90,000 part-time-role origin.

Priority evolution

2004-2005, first annual report (bf353dac207c): City Council authorized recruitment of a City Integrity Commissioner by resolution in March 2004; David Mullan was appointed as the City's first Integrity Commissioner and assumed duties September 1, 2004, on an initially part-time basis, with an annualized budget of $200,000 (four-month 2004 budget: $90,000). The founding mandate, set by Council motion (no statute yet existed), was narrow: "to provide independent and consistent complaint prevention and resolution, advice, opinion and education respecting the application of the Code of Conduct for members of Council" and general interpretation of the MCIA. The Commissioner had no jurisdiction over City staff conduct, no jurisdiction over civilian members of agencies/boards/commissions, no jurisdiction over Council/committee floor behaviour (left to the Speaker), no financial-disclosure function, and no sanctioning power (Council alone could impose consequences on a finding). Two 2005 events reshaped the trajectory: the September 2005 release of the Bellamy Commission's Report on the Toronto Computer Leasing Inquiry, which endorsed and called for enhancement of the office, and the December 15, 2005 introduction of Bill 53 ("Stronger City of Toronto for a Stronger Ontario Act"), which would give the office a statutory basis (bf353dac207c). First-period caseload: 21 formal complaints received over 16 months, 1 Code violation found (bf353dac207c).

2006 (3270c1e158be): COTA received royal assent and came into force January 1, 2007, putting the office "on a statutory footing" with "permanent status" for the first time. In response to COTA and the Bellamy recommendations, Council adopted significant Code of Conduct revisions (September 2006): the gift/benefit rule was tightened (no more "custom" exception, no gifts from lobbyists, $500 cap), improper use of influence became a standalone offence, obstructing the Integrity Commissioner and reprisals against complainants became violations, and — to meet COTA s.157(2) — the Code was extended to cover members of local boards (restricted definition) for the first time. COTA-derived statutory sanctions (reprimand, up to 90 days' loss of pay) were written into the Code (3270c1e158be).

2007, End of Term Report (80a15951d26a): Mullan's final report (his four-year term ended August 31, 2008) records the 2007 extension of jurisdiction to cover local board members in practice, and sets out a list of unresolved mandate questions that recur across the whole series: the narrow, pecuniary-only scope of the MCIA; whether the office should have jurisdiction over harassment/human-rights complaints, Members' staff, and behaviour at Council/committee meetings; whether the Commissioner should hold sanctioning power (Mullan recommended against — sanctioning should stay with Council); and the need for a fixed term, protection from at-will dismissal, and a distinct budget-setting mechanism to entrench independence (80a15951d26a).

2008, Interim Commissioner (85c892e6c035): Lorne Sossin served as Interim Integrity Commissioner (October 2008-September 2009) following Mullan's departure. His term produced the "Policy Framework for Toronto's Accountability Officers," adopted by Council April 29-30, 2009 — the first governance bylaw entrenching independence features (administrative autonomy, distinct budget process) for all four Accountability Officers collectively (85c892e6c035).

2009-2010 (b987000dca7e): Janet Leiper began as Integrity Commissioner September 8, 2009. On October 9, 2009, Council enacted Toronto Municipal Code Chapter 3 ("Accountability Officers"), formally requiring annual reporting (b987000dca7e).

2010-2011 (a2be658bbe42): Office relocated to 375 University Avenue, Suite 202 (co-located with the Lobbyist Registrar and Ombudsman). First full election-cycle experience of the complaint moratorium mechanism, adjusted for a shifted municipal election date (a2be658bbe42).

2011-2012 (e13133869087): The Public Inquiries Act, 2009 amendments (in force June 1, 2011) required Complaint Protocol updates. The "Councillor Expense Policy" was renamed the "Constituency Services and Office Budget Policy." Formal complaints rose 122% and informal complaints rose ~188% year over year — the first of several sharp caseload step-changes in the series (e13133869087).

2012-2013 (51407920bf46): A judicial review proceeding under the MCIA — the first court case to test the relationship between Code of Conduct findings and the MCIA — was heard and appealed during this period, prompting a joint Integrity Commissioner/City Solicitor report to Council. The report recommends moving to a full-time Integrity Commissioner starting with the next appointment (September 2014), citing sustained high caseload (51407920bf46).

2013-2014, Leiper's final report (43b19507fd73): Confirms the transition to a full-time Commissioner (Valerie Jepson, starting September 6, 2014). A Code of Conduct/Complaint Protocol modernization package ("Integrity by Design") was presented to Executive Committee in May 2014 but deferred indefinitely — the Code would not be comprehensively reviewed again for another eight years (43b19507fd73).

2014-2015, Jepson's first report (1811d02bc662): Marks the office's 10th anniversary. Reports a growing complaint backlog inherited from the caseload growth of prior years; Council approved an additional Investigator/Analyst position (filled in 2015) alongside the full-time-Commissioner transition, bringing total budget from $299.1K (2014) to $427.6K (2015) (1811d02bc662).

Jul-Dec 2015 transition report (bece760b7eae): Marks the shift from July-June to calendar-year reporting. City Council commissioned an external review of all four Accountability Officers, led by Lorne Sossin (by then Dean of Osgoode Hall Law School); its most significant recommendation — cross-appointing one person as both Integrity Commissioner and Lobbyist Registrar — was referred for further study and not implemented. The office launched a consolidated gift-disclosure registry and filed comprehensive submissions to the Province's Municipal Legislation Review, recommending that COTA and the MCIA be merged into a single framework, independence be further entrenched, and mandatory financial disclosure be introduced for elected officials — submissions that anticipated the province's Bill 68 reforms two years later (bece760b7eae).

2016 (79878f44c3f3): Council created three new adjudicative tribunals (Toronto Local Appeal Body, Rooming House Licensing Commissioner, Administrative Penalty Tribunal), each bound by the Code of Conduct for Adjudicative Boards and thus expanding the office's local-board caseload. Separately, the office assumed a new statutory duty to receive and manage harassment complaints regarding the City Clerk, City Solicitor and City Manager, under the province's Sexual Violence and Harassment Action Plan Act, 2016. On November 16, 2016, the Province introduced Bill 68, "Modernizing Ontario's Municipal Legislation Act, 2016" (79878f44c3f3).

2017 (5bda7ad3fbb2): Bill 68 passed into law in 2017; its provisions extending integrity-commissioner jurisdiction to MCIA advice and complaints across every Ontario municipality were confirmed to come into force March 1, 2019 — the office began an extended preparation period (5bda7ad3fbb2).

2018, Jepson's final report (90d3518b8780): The Investigator/Analyst position was converted into a Legal Counsel role ahead of the MCIA jurisdiction taking effect. The Commissioner publicly flagged two structural concerns recurring since Mullan's term: repeated Council refusal of contingency-funding requests for external legal/investigative capacity (framed as a risk to independence), and a pattern of Council members questioning investigation findings/lines of inquiry when receiving reports, which the Commissioner argued should be limited strictly to the penalty question (90d3518b8780).

2019, Jonathan Batty's first report (034826ad729c): On March 1, 2019, the office's authority was enhanced by Bill 68: the Commissioner gained jurisdiction to give advice on and receive/investigate applications under MCIA ss.5, 5.1 and 5.2 — the single largest jurisdictional expansion in the office's history, extending its reach from Code-of-Conduct-only to statutory conflict-of-interest enforcement. The same year, Toronto's Council was reduced from 45 to 26 members following the 2018 municipal election, while the office's local-board caseload (~1,100 appointees) was unaffected. Batty, who began November 30, 2019, adopted a five-year Strategic Plan (2019-2024) (034826ad729c).

2020 (ddbc3bae434d): COVID-19 shifted office operations to fully remote; investigations were briefly paused, then resumed via video interview. The office's first dedicated Legal Counsel (Kate Zavitz) started February 2020. On December 16, 2020, Council approved a standing reserve fund for external legal/investigative contingencies — addressing the funding-flexibility concern Jepson had raised in 2018 (ddbc3bae434d).

2021 (85b336ae82ac): On April 7, 2021, Council directed the Commissioner to conduct the first comprehensive review of the Codes of Conduct since their original 2008 adoption — covering conflict-of-interest provisions, inclusive/plain language, and structure (85b336ae82ac).

2022 (ad9d46297ef4): The Code of Conduct review concluded: in June 2022 Council adopted updated Codes of Conduct for Members of Council, Local Boards and Adjudicative Boards, broadening the conflict-of-interest rule to cover apparent conflicts (not just pecuniary ones), adding a prohibition on derogatory conduct, and adding a new rule governing Members' charitable-fundraising activity, informed by what the Commissioner describes as the first public survey ever conducted by an integrity commissioner. The office also completed its first-ever inquiry under the MCIA. The 2022 regular municipal election triggered the statutory pre-election blackout on new complaints/applications (August 19-October 24, 2022) (ad9d46297ef4).

2023, Batty's final report (4b3c10a1a291): The City's Mayor at the time announced a resignation in early 2023 and requested the Commissioner investigate conduct dating back to 2020; the resulting inquiry was a major draw on the office's resources, with findings adopted by Council in October 2023 (individual not named per this brief's scope). The resignation triggered an unscheduled mayoral by-election, requiring the office to re-issue election-period guidance outside the normal four-year cycle and — because no statutory blackout applies to by-elections — to continue receiving and investigating complaints through the by-election period. A companion policy report on harassment and discrimination involving elected officials was also delivered. The report situates the office's 20-year history: created in the wake of a procurement scandal following the 1998 amalgamation of Metropolitan Toronto's six constituent cities, investigated by the Bellamy Commission (appointed 2002, reported September 2005), whose recommendations (consistency, expertise, profile, "rules must have teeth") are described as still framing the office's rationale two decades on (4b3c10a1a291).

2024, transition to Paul Muldoon (bf69a443f7c6): The office received a record complaint volume, 60% above 2023, driven partly by mass/duplicate campaign-style submissions on single issues. A new distinct duty was added: the Integrity Commissioner became the designated "Ethics Executive" for Members of Council's staff (a role separate from the Code-of-Conduct/MCIA jurisdiction over Members themselves). In December 2024 the Province introduced Bill 241, which would have imposed a single standardized Code of Conduct on all 444 Ontario municipalities; it died on the order paper with the calling of a provincial election, but the Commissioner flagged it as a mandate risk to monitor. Jonathan Batty's five-year term ended; Paul Muldoon began as Commissioner December 1, 2024, and the office marked its 20th anniversary with a joint event ("IntegriTea") bringing together all five Commissioners to date (bf69a443f7c6).

Priorities added, dropped, renamed

  • Added — statutory basis and permanent status: absent 2004-2006 (office existed only by Council resolution); COTA in force January 1, 2007 (3270c1e158be).
  • Added — jurisdiction over local boards (restricted definition): absent from the founding 2004 mandate (bf353dac207c); added via the September 2006 Code of Conduct revision to meet COTA s.157(2), operative from 2007 (3270c1e158be; 80a15951d26a).
  • Added — full-time Commissioner: the role was part-time 2004-2014; recommended by Leiper in 2012-2013 (51407920bf46) and implemented starting with Jepson's September 2014 appointment (43b19507fd73; 1811d02bc662).
  • Added — harassment-complaint intake for senior officials: new 2016 duty regarding the City Clerk, City Solicitor and City Manager under provincial legislation (79878f44c3f3).
  • Added — MCIA advice-and-complaint jurisdiction: the office's mandate through 2018 was Code-of-Conduct-only (with MCIA limited to informal interpretation, never binding advice or complaint intake); Bill 68 gave the Commissioner full MCIA advice/application jurisdiction effective March 1, 2019 — the largest single jurisdictional expansion in the series (5bda7ad3fbb2; 034826ad729c).
  • Added — reserve fund for external legal/investigative resources: absent through 2019 (a recurring unmet request, e.g. 90d3518b8780); approved by Council December 16, 2020 (ddbc3bae434d).
  • Added — "Ethics Executive" role for Members' Council staff: new and distinct from the Members-of-Council mandate; first reported in 2024 (bf69a443f7c6).
  • Renamed — "Councillor Expense Policy" → "Constituency Services and Office Budget Policy": renamed by Council July 2012, with consequential Complaint Protocol amendments (e13133869087).
  • Renamed/restructured — Codes of Conduct: essentially unrevised in substance from 2008 until the comprehensive June 2022 rewrite (broadened conflict-of-interest rule, new derogatory-conduct and fundraising provisions, plain/inclusive language); an earlier 2014 attempt at modernization ("Integrity by Design") was deferred indefinitely by Executive Committee and never revived (43b19507fd73; 85b336ae82ac; ad9d46297ef4).
  • Dropped/never implemented — cross-appointment of Integrity Commissioner and Lobbyist Registrar: recommended by the 2015 Sossin external review of the Accountability Officers; referred for further study and not adopted in this series (bece760b7eae).
  • Threatened, not enacted — province-wide standardized Code of Conduct (Bill 241): introduced December 2024, died on the order paper with the provincial election call; flagged by the incoming Commissioner as a mandate risk to watch, not a completed change (bf69a443f7c6).

Ontario/Toronto relevance

This is a City of Toronto office in its entirety, so relevance is direct rather than inferential — every claim above is about Toronto's own municipal government. Two provincial linkages recur throughout the series and are worth naming explicitly: first, the office's statutory basis and every major jurisdictional change trace to Ontario legislation — COTA (2006, in force 2007), the Public Inquiries Act, 2009 amendments (2011, e13133869087), and above all Bill 68, "Modernizing Ontario's Municipal Legislation Act" (2016-2019), which gave Toronto's Commissioner MCIA jurisdiction and simultaneously made an integrity commissioner mandatory for all 444 Ontario municipalities, a change Toronto's Commissioner explicitly framed as extending "the model that Toronto City Council pioneered on its own initiative in 2004" province-wide (79878f44c3f3). Second, the office sits inside a peer network of Ontario municipal integrity commissioners: the founding Commissioner consulted the then-provincial Integrity Commissioner Coulter Osborne in 2004 (bf353dac207c); by 2011 there were 17 other Ontario municipalities with integrity commissioners, and Toronto helped found and repeatedly hosted the Municipal Integrity Commissioners of Ontario (MICO) network (a2be658bbe42; multiple later reports). The provincial 2024 Bill 241 proposal — a single standardized Code of Conduct for all 444 municipalities — would, if revived, directly constrain Toronto's own Code of Conduct authority (bf69a443f7c6).
