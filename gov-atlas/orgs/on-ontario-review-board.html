Ontario Review Board - backgrounder

Backgrounder / GOV-Q10-WAVE1-d1 / 2026-08-04 / registry row: on-ontario-review-board (research/atlas/government_orgs.csv) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

"The Ontario Review Board (ORB or Board) is a unique tribunal that forms a critical component of the Canadian justice system. Although the ORB has jurisdiction only within the province of Ontario, it was created pursuant to and is governed by the Criminal Code of Canada" (ORB Annual Report 2024-25, "Overview of the Ontario Review Board," p.5, https://www.orb.on.ca/scripts/en/resources/ORB-Annual-Report-2024-25-English%20FINAL-s.pdf, fetched this pass). Part XX.1 of the Criminal Code provides: "A Review Board shall be established or designated for each province to make or review dispositions concerning any accused in respect of whom a verdict of not criminally responsible by reason of mental disorder or unfit to stand trial is rendered, and shall consist of not fewer than five members appointed by the Lieutenant Governor in Council of the province..." (same source, quoting Part XX.1). The ORB's "procedures are established solely by the Criminal Code and the Public Inquiries Act. Unlike adjudicative agencies that are created by provincial statute, the ORB is not subject to the provisions of the Statutory Powers Procedure Act. Appeals from decisions of the ORB are made to the Court of Appeal for Ontario" (same source).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

The ORB "has jurisdiction over individuals, referred to in the Criminal Code as 'accused,' who Ontario courts have found to be either unfit to stand trial (unfit), or not criminally responsible on account of mental disorder for the commission of a criminal offence (NCR)" (ORB Annual Report 2024-25, p.5). Following a hearing the Board renders one of three dispositions: an absolute discharge, a discharge subject to conditions, or detention in hospital subject to conditions, weighing "the need to protect the public from dangerous persons, the mental condition of the accused, the reintegration of the accused into society and the other needs of the accused" (same source, p.5-6). In fiscal 2024-25 the Board had 1,590 accused persons under its jurisdiction, received 224 new accused (57 unfit, 167 NCR), conducted 1,913 hearings, and granted 100 absolute discharges (same source, "Caseload Statistics," p.9-10).

Governance & reporting line

Board members are appointed by Order-in-Council; the Chairperson must be a judge (current or retired, or entitled to be appointed) of the Federal Court or a provincial superior, district or county court, and the Chairperson may designate other qualified members as Alternate Chairpersons (ORB Annual Report 2024-25, "Board Composition," p.6). Panels typically sit five members: a Chairperson or Alternate Chairperson, two psychiatrists (or one psychiatrist and one psychologist), a legal member, and a public member; quorum is three (same source). As of March 31, 2025 the Board had 132 members - a full-time Chairperson plus 26 Alternate Chairpersons, 23 legal members, 44 psychiatrists, 21 psychologists and 18 public members, all Ontario residents appointed by Order-in-Council (same source, "Appointees," p.11). "The ORB is supported by 17 staff within the Ministry of Health (MOH)," led by a Senior Manager and Registrar (same source, "Staff," p.11).

Budget scale

For fiscal year 2024-25 the ORB reported total expenditures of $7,057,851, up from $5,964,258 in 2023-24 - salaries & wages $1,374,582, benefits $236,615, transportation and communications $524,627, services $4,916,853 (largely member/panel remuneration and hearing-related costs), and supplies and equipment $5,174. By function, annual hearings accounted for $3,926,025 and initial hearings for $1,179,694 of the total (ORB Annual Report 2024-25, "Financial Summary," p.16, fetched this pass).

Institutional history

The ORB's structure and powers derive from Part XX.1 of the Criminal Code, which under earlier federal amendment "evolved from an advisory body (pre-1992) to the final decision-maker in these cases, replacing the former Lieutenant Governor warrant system" (search-derived summary of Part XX.1 history, cross-referenced against ORB's own "Overview" page confirming the Board's current statutory basis, orb.on.ca link above). ⚠️ VERIFY - the exact year Ontario's own Review Board was first established/designated under the province's Lieutenant Governor warrant predecessor system, and the precise date its current Criminal Code Part XX.1 form took effect in Ontario specifically, were not independently confirmed against a primary source this pass.

TL;DR: The newly-read 15-document Business Plan series (bp-2015 through bp-2029, covering plans from 2012-2015 through 2026-2029, never cited in the v1.0 brief) surfaces a funding-mechanism inflection the AR series never mentions: the ORB's budget moved off a statutory "Vote and Item" allocation frozen at $7,375,400 for roughly eight straight fiscal years (FY2012-13 through FY2019-20) to Ministry-of-Health-allocated budgeting, effective (per the Board's own two most recent plans, which disagree by exactly one year — see Residuals) either April 1, 2023 or April 1, 2024; the Board's newest projections (bp-2029) show total expenditures climbing to $8,250,478 by FY2028-29, the highest figure anywhere in the 40-document record. The BP series also supplies the first primary-sourced account of the Board's pre-1992 origins — a dedicated "History" section (present bp-2015 through bp-2026, then dropped) tracing the 1892 Criminal Code, the pre-1992 "Lieutenant Governor's Board of Review," the 1991 Supreme Court ruling, and Bill C-30's February 4, 1992 creation of Review Boards — which narrows, without fully closing, the backgrounder's open question about Ontario's pre-1992 predecessor system. The biggest priority quietly dropped mirrors the AR series' own 2024-25 restructuring: the BP series' "History," "Relationship with the Ministry of Health," "Environmental Scan," "Changes to the Criminal Code," and "Communications Plan" sections disappear in two stages (bp-2028 drops most of them; bp-2029 completes a full prose-based reorganization built around the Agencies & Appointments Directive). One load-bearing number, newly legible only by reading the BP series alongside the AR series: Board membership — reported by ARs on a March 31 fiscal-year-end snapshot and by BPs on a December 31 calendar-year snapshot — bottomed at 132 (ar-2025, March 31, 2025) and then grew to 147 within the same calendar year (bp-2029, December 31, 2025), a +15-member, nine-month partial recovery the AR-only v1.0 brief could not see. One open question, now better specified rather than resolved: whether the federal Criminal Code amendment enabling non-consensual virtual hearings — requested since 2021 and still being "spearheaded" by the Chair "on behalf of Review Boards Canada" per the 2024-2027 plan (bp-2027) — has been enacted; the Board's newest plan (bp-2029, 2026-2029) still describes the fix as unresolved, relying on an ad hoc, consent-based workaround.

Backgrounder summary

The ORB is "a unique tribunal that forms a critical component of the Canadian justice system," created under Part XX.1 of the Criminal Code with jurisdiction over accused persons found not criminally responsible (NCR) or unfit to stand trial, but administratively "not subject to the provisions of the Statutory Powers Procedure Act" the way provincial tribunals are (ORB Annual Report 2024-25, p.5, c5ce9dc702c0). The Board is chaired by a sitting or retired superior-court judge, sits panels of five (Chair/Alternate Chair, two psychiatrists or a psychiatrist-and-psychologist, a legal member, a public member), and had 132 members and 17 Ministry-of-Health staff as of March 31, 2025 (same source, p.6, 11). Backgrounder-cited FY2024-25 figures — 1,590 accused under jurisdiction, 224 new accused (57 unfit, 167 NCR), 1,913 hearings, 100 absolute discharges, $7,057,851 total expenditures (up from $5,964,258 in FY2023-24) — are all independently re-confirmed against the primary text below (D-042: this brief relays, and separately re-verifies, rather than asserting independent discovery); the FY2023-24 figure is also independently corroborated in the newly-read bp-2028 (d85420e59f26), which cites the identical $5,964,258 as an "actual" expenditure line. The backgrounder's one open item — the exact year Ontario's Review Board was first established under the pre-1992 Lieutenant-Governor-warrant predecessor system — is narrowed but not resolved by the newly-read Business Plan series' own "History" section; see Priority evolution and Residuals & gaps.

Priority evolution

2012-2015 (bp-2015, 45c224a8d1b7) — the earliest strategic document in either series: this plan predates every other document read for this brief and captures the Board mid-transition to a new Chair ("The ORB is currently working with a new Chair and is prepared for a transitional year going forward"), anticipating what the AR series (ar-2012, 5c603d53859f) confirms as Richard D. Schneider's June 27, 2012 appointment. Membership stood at 164 (46 Chair/Alternates, 11 Lawyers, 81 Psychiatrists/Psychologists, 26 Public Members) — the highest single figure anywhere in either series. This is also the only vantage in the full 40-document record from which the Board narrates its own pre-1992 institutional history: a dedicated "History" section states that in 1892, in adopting Britain's draft Criminal Code, Canada gave Lieutenant Governors custody of "mentally disordered accused," with an advisory body in Ontario called "the Lieutenant Governor's Board of Review"; that the Supreme Court struck this system down in 1991 as Charter-violating; and that "on February 4, 1992, Bill C-30 was enacted creating Review Boards in every province and territory," eliminating the Lieutenant Governor's Warrant system and introducing the term "not criminally responsible." This directly, if not completely, narrows the backgrounder's ⚠️ VERIFY on the pre-1992 predecessor: it is now grounded in the Board's own primary planning document (not merely a "search-derived summary," as the backgrounder characterized it) rather than resolved to an exact founding year for the Ontario body specifically (see Residuals). bp-2015 also documents a chronic structural budget deficit: the Board's Vote-and-Item allocation was frozen at $3,975,400 from FY2008-09 through FY2011-12 while actual expenditure ran $6.8-7.3M every year — a roughly $3M/year gap the Board says was closed only by a FY2012-13 "right-sizing" (see Budget & mandate inflection points).

2013-2019 (bp-2016 through bp-2022, 8f4c3e20a223 through a4ae1c184f87) — the Schneider-era BPs, run in parallel with the AR series' own account: the BP series corroborates, and adds granularity to, the AR-based narrative already established for this period (Schneider's "three/four key target objectives" formula, the Conway Charter-jurisdiction confirmation, Bill C-14 victim-notification duties). The BP series' own legislative tracking is more precise than the ARs': bp-2016 (8f4c3e20a223) flags the reform under its original bill number, "Bill C-54," warning "the Board's obligations in this regard will add considerably to the time required to manage our case load" if proclaimed "as presently drafted"; bp-2017 (86e778b687dc) reports it renumbered "Bill C-14," "expected...to come into force on July 10, 2014"; bp-2018's new History-section addendum (d2ca02fe806b) confirms it "came into force" on that date — a date that differs by one day from the AR series' own account (ar-2015, a3fe53eac5e8, cites July 11, 2014; ⚠️ VERIFY, see Residuals). The BP series also tracks an administrative-funding thread invisible in the ARs: bp-2018 (d2ca02fe806b) records the Board "striving to reach an arrangement whereby ORB ordered assessments...will be funded by the Health Boards Secretariat"; bp-2019 (83507e45496f) confirms the arrangement was "reached"; bp-2023 (bdb6d762803a) reports the Secretariat "presently negotiating with the Forensic Facilities Group terms that would clearly make this arrangement applicable to all Board-ordered Assessments" — a multi-year, incrementally-won funding concession the AR series never mentions. Board membership, tracked by the BPs on a December 31 snapshot (distinct from the AR series' March 31 fiscal-year-end convention — see Residuals), rose from 158 (June 1, 2013, bp-2016) to a series peak of 170 (December 31, 2017 and again December 31, 2018, bp-2021/bp-2022, 5ba619958ec7/a4ae1c184f87) before falling sharply to 149 (December 31, 2019, bp-2023, bdb6d762803a) — the same recruitment-strain era the AR series dates to ar-2019's (33bbdef2179b) first "Recruitment Activities and Membership" section.

2019-2023 (bp-2023 through bp-2026, bdb6d762803a through eb44bdbb1f7c) — pandemic, virtual-hearing litigation, and a fuller "Legal Challenges" narrative than the ARs give: the BP series' account of the ORB's virtual-hearings legal saga is materially more detailed than the AR series' own "Legal Challenges" section. bp-2024 (c831e5cc71f6, the 2021-2024 plan) is the first to name it directly: "One such challenge resulted in a ruling that the Board was not able to proceed electronically without the accused's consent...The Board has retained counsel to argue this adverse ruling at the Court of Appeal." bp-2025 (744266765cf0) reports the Court of Appeal ruling against the Board and a stopgap: "the Court's offer of courtroom space...Two such hearings have been held in courtrooms. Unfortunately, the Court's support has been withdrawn." bp-2026 (eb44bdbb1f7c) reports the Board pivoting again: "the Board has been able to find solutions in ad hoc arrangements with several Forensic Facilities." Membership continued to slide through this window: 153 (December 31, 2020, bp-2024) and 153 again (December 31, 2021, bp-2025, though with a different Alternate-Chair/legal/public-member mix — a real change, not stale carry-forward) before dropping to 148 (December 31, 2022, bp-2026). This period also introduces a new named accountability instrument alongside ATAGAA: bp-2029 later identifies "the Agencies & Appointments Directive (AAD)" as governing supplementary risk reporting — its first appearance anywhere in the 40-document record (see Priorities added, dropped, renamed).

2023-2027 (bp-2027, 65e1cf2b4ad1, the 2024-2027 plan) — the modernization slate, first announced in real time, months before the AR series records it: where the AR series' account of the Dambrot-era modernization push relies on ar-2024 and ar-2025 (published after the fact), bp-2027 documents the same initiatives as they were launched, with more precise dates. It reports the Users Consultation Forum was "formally announced" in November 2023, with its "first meeting...held in January 2024" — dates the AR series never states. It reports a Restriction-of-Liberty ("ROL") virtual-hearing pilot beginning implementation in "March 2024," involving "regularly scheduled panels...positioned to hear ROL's virtually from any hospital" — again, more granular than the AR series' account. It reports a hospital-report-timeliness collaboration "completed" in "May 2023," producing "a report of its work on this initiative and a hospital report template." And, in a passage the AR series does not carry, it reports the Chair had "spearheaded" a renewed federal advocacy effort "on behalf of Review Boards Canada to ask the federal government to enact amendments to the Criminal Code of Canada to afford Review Boards greater flexibility in responding to urgent circumstances such as a pandemic" — the clearest evidence yet that the 2021 request (ar-2021, a34f8bd24c13; ar-2022, de45bc27435b) was still being actively pursued as of this plan, not merely sent once and left unanswered. Membership fell to a series low of 136 (December 31, 2023, bp-2027) — below even the pandemic-era trough — a decline the plan attributes partly to attrition among "retired justices and respected senior lawyers who serve as Alternate Chairpersons," whose "numbers dwindle as a result of illness and retirement."

2025-2029 (bp-2028, d85420e59f26; bp-2029, a283444bcc8b) — recovery, a full report restructure, and new disclosures absent from every earlier document: bp-2028 (2025-2028 plan) reports membership recovering to 141 (December 31, 2024) and confirms the hospital-report-timeliness project achieved only "mixed success," with the Board planning to "measure the outcome of the initiative." It also contains the record's first explicit AI-governance disclosure: "The Board is committed to continuing to modernize its operations, including embracing the use of technology. The Board does not, however, use artificial intelligence in any fashion including in its operations or adjudicative decision making." bp-2029 (2026-2029 plan) elevates this into its own named section, "Inventory of the ORB's Artificial Intelligence Use Cases," repeating the same no-AI-use statement verbatim — evidently in response to a province-wide AI-disclosure expectation, not a Board-specific initiative. bp-2029 also discloses, for the first time anywhere in the record, that "the Board's office space is shared with other adjudicative tribunals within the MOH, including the Consent and Capacity Board (CCB), Health Professions Appeal and Review Board (HPARB) and Health Services Appeal and Review Board (HSARB)" — a plausible, though undated, explanation for the 10th-to-9th-floor office move the AR series shows but does not explain (see Residuals). It reports a "new Rules of Procedure" project underway — "the first significant update to these guidelines since the early 2000s," anticipated "completed in 2026-27" — and a new "READI" (Race, Equity, Antiracism, Diversity, and Inclusion) training initiative, neither mentioned in any earlier document in either series. Membership recovered further to 147 (December 31, 2025) — 15 more than the AR series' 132 (March 31, 2025) reported just nine months earlier, meaning the "quietly shrinking" membership trend the v1.0 brief flagged had already reversed by the time bp-2029 was written (see TL;DR). Structurally, bp-2029 completes a wholesale reorganization begun in bp-2028: the old tabular "Risk Assessment and Management" format becomes prose-based "Risk Identification, Assessment and Mitigation Strategies," organized under five named subheads (Adjudicative Integrity and Public Safety; Membership Stability and Statutory Quorum; Statutory Timelines; Operational Continuity and Digital Proceedings; Management of Case Complexity and Appellate Obligations) and explicitly tied to "regular risk reporting under the Agencies & Appointments Directive (AAD)" — a structural echo of the AR series' own ar-2025 restructuring that the v1.0 brief flagged as possibly "a one-off transition-year artifact." Read together, the two series' parallel restructurings in the same 2024-2026 window make it more likely this is a durable convention than a one-off (still ⚠️ VERIFY; see Residuals).

  • FY2012-13 "right-sizing," with an unresolved figure discrepancy — bp-2015 (45c224a8d1b7) states the "right-sized" FY2012-13 allocation as $7,035,900; bp-2016 (8f4c3e20a223), written one year later, restates the same historical FY2012-13 allocation as $7,375,400 — a $339,500 upward revision for an already-closed fiscal year that no document explains (⚠️ VERIFY, see Residuals).
  • FY2012-13 through FY2019-20 (roughly eight fiscal years) — the statutory Vote-and-Item allocation then held flat at exactly $7,375,400 in every BP's Printed Estimates table from bp-2016 (8f4c3e20a223) through bp-2024 (c831e5cc71f6), even as actual expenditure fluctuated year to year — a multi-year nominal freeze the AR series does not surface because ARs report expenditure, not the underlying Vote allocation.
  • FY2020-21 — FY2021-22, allocation drift begins — the frozen $7,375,400 allocation drops to $7,137,000 (FY2020-21, bp-2025, 744266765cf0) and $7,102,100 (FY2021-22, bp-2026, eb44bdbb1f7c), modest reductions bracketing the pandemic years, shortly before the allocation mechanism itself is retired.
  • June 27, 2012 — Chair transition from The Honourable Douglas H. Carruthers, Q.C. to The Honourable Mr. Justice Richard D. Schneider (ar-2012, 5c603d53859f), anticipated in real time by bp-2015's (45c224a8d1b7) note that the Board was "currently working with a new Chair."
  • July 10/11, 2014 — Bill C-14 (Not Criminally Responsible Reform Act) comes into force, adding victim-notification and High-Risk-Accused administrative obligations; BP series consistently dates this to July 10 (bp-2017, 86e778b687dc; bp-2018, d2ca02fe806b), one day earlier than the AR series' July 11 (ar-2015, a3fe53eac5e8) — ⚠️ VERIFY discrepancy.
  • FY2019-20 — COVID-19 remote operations stood up by fiscal year-end (ar-2020, b2f66a481168); Transportation & Communications spending collapses across both series' figures, reaching a low of $37,263 in bp-2025's (744266765cf0) FY2021-22 projection, consistent with the AR series' own pandemic-era Transportation & Communications figures.
  • June 13, 2023 — Chair transition from Schneider to The Honourable Michael Dambrot, K.C. (ar-2024, 888a940c3df8).
  • April 1, 2023 or April 1, 2024 — the Board's funding mechanism changes, with the Board's own two most recent plans disagreeing on the effective date: bp-2027 (65e1cf2b4ad1, the 2024-2027 plan) states "The Board no longer receives its annual funding allocation through a separate Vote and Item. As of April 1, 2023 and going forward, the Board's funding will be allocated through the Ministry." bp-2028 (d85420e59f26, the 2025-2028 plan, written one year later) states the identical change took effect "As of April 1, 2024," with its Printed Estimates table marking FY2023-24 "N/A*" and footnoting "Since April 1, 2024, the Board's funding is allocated through the Ministry budget." This is a genuine one-year discrepancy in the Board's own account of its most significant funding-governance change in the readable record (⚠️ VERIFY, see Residuals) — but the change itself, ending the roughly decade-long frozen Vote-and-Item allocation, is corroborated by both documents and is the strongest new budget/mandate finding this pass unlocks.
  • FY2023-24 total expenditure: $5,964,258 — reported identically in bp-2028 (d85420e59f26) and re-confirmed independently in the backgrounder/ar-2024 chain (888a940c3df8) already cited in the v1.0 brief; the first cross-series (AR-vs-BP) exact-figure match this pass identifies.

Ontario/Toronto relevance

The ORB is headquartered at 151 Bloor Street West in downtown Toronto throughout the entire 2001-2025 record — 10th Floor, M5S 2T5 from ar-2001 (6edd33017526) through at least ar-2023 (1b4d6589c97a) — moving to 9th Floor, M5S 1S4 by ar-2024 (888a940c3df8), coinciding with the Dambrot-era Chair transition; the newly-read bp-2029 (a283444bcc8b) supplies a plausible, though still undated, explanation the AR series never gives — that "the Board's office space is shared with other adjudicative tribunals within the MOH, including the Consent and Capacity Board (CCB), Health Professions Appeal and Review Board (HPARB) and Health Services Appeal and Review Board (HSARB)" — consistent with a shared-tribunal consolidation, but the exact date of the floor move remains ⚠️ VERIFY (see Residuals). Every one of the 15 newly-read Business Plans repeats a direct, dateable Toronto institutional connection the AR-based v1.0 brief did not cite: the Board states it "was instrumental in the establishment of the Mental Disorder Court ('102 Court') in the City of Toronto," created "to identify and expedite the assessment of mentally disordered persons who have come into conflict with the law" and later serving as a model for "seven mental health courts in the province" (identical or near-identical language, bp-2015 through bp-2027, e.g. 45c224a8d1b7; 65e1cf2b4ad1). As a Part XX.1 Criminal Code tribunal, the ORB's caseload jurisdiction remains province-wide; for nearly the entire series no report — AR or BP — breaks out hearing locations, hospital sites, or caseload by geography. The single exception remains ar-2025 (c5ce9dc702c0), which names all 11 Ontario forensic hospital sites in a Hospital-Report-timeliness table, including CAMH (Toronto), Ontario Shores (Whitby), and Waypoint (Penetanguishene). Toronto/GTA relevance is therefore best described as direct-but-partial: confirmed institutional presence (head office; CAMH named as a hearing/detention site; the Board's own account of founding the 102 Court) plus indirect province-wide caseload relevance, rather than a documented Toronto-specific caseload share.
