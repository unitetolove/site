City of Mississauga - backgrounder

Backgrounder / 2026-07-30 / registry row: miss-city-of-mississauga (this library's government-document registry) / lens file for this org's series briefs

Mandate & statutory basis

Mississauga is a lower-tier municipality incorporated as a body corporate under the Municipal Act, 2001, S.O. 2001, c. 25, consolidated text verified at https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/01m25 (fetched 2026-07-30, HTTP 200). Mississauga's city was originally constituted under the Regional Municipality of Peel Act, 1973 as part of the creation of the Region of Peel; that founding Act (R.S.O. 1990, c. R.15) was repealed effective January 1, 2003 and folded into the Municipal Act, 2001 (confirmed directly on the e-Laws record: "This Act was repealed on January 1, 2003. See: 2001, c. 25...", https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90r15, fetched 2026-07-30, HTTP 200).

Roles, responsibilities & scope

Mississauga is a lower-tier city within upper-tier Region of Peel, alongside Brampton and Caledon (registry note, miss-city-of-mississauga, this library's government-document registry). Under the province's Peel Transition Implementation Act, 2024, Peel Regional roads are being downloaded to Brampton, Caledon, and Mississauga effective July 1, 2026, with waste-management responsibilities also shifting to Mississauga (thepointer.com and mississauga.ca reporting, fetched 2026-07-30) — a partial-service transfer that replaced the earlier full-dissolution plan under the 2023 "Hazel McCallion Act" after the province reversed course in December 2023 (statement corroborated by https://www.mississauga.ca/city-of-mississauga-news/news/statements-regarding-province-reversing-decision-about-dissolution-of-peel-region/).

Governance & reporting line

Mississauga is led by an elected Mayor and City Council, with committees under Council governance (https://www.mississauga.ca/council/committees/, registry source authority). Its current strategic plan is "Imagine Mississauga" (2026-2031) (document id miss-city-of-mississauga-sp-2026, https://www.mississauga.ca/publication/strategic-plan/).

Budget scale

~$746.8M City operating budget for 2025 (an $67.4M / 8.8% increase from 2024) and a ~$628.2M gross capital budget for 2025 (adopted by Council December 2, 2024) (thepointer.com, "Mississauga approves 8.8% budget hike...", fetched and figures extracted 2026-07-30, cross-referenced against document id miss-city-of-mississauga-bud-2025, https://www.mississauga.ca/publication/2025-budget/).

Institutional history

Mississauga was incorporated as a city on January 1, 1974 through the amalgamation of the Town of Mississauga, Town of Port Credit, and Town of Streetsville under the Regional Municipality of Peel Act, 1973, which simultaneously created the Region of Peel (context corroborated by the e-Laws repeal note at ontario.ca/laws/statute/90r15 and general search context; ⚠️ still being checked exact 1974 amalgamation details not confirmed against a primary consolidated-text source this review); the founding Act was repealed January 1, 2003, with Mississauga's structure and powers since governed by the Municipal Act, 2001.

Backgrounder summary

Mississauga is a lower-tier municipality within the upper-tier Region of Peel (alongside Brampton and Caledon), incorporated as a body corporate under the Municipal Act, 2001, S.O. 2001, c. 25, its predecessor Regional Municipality of Peel Act, 1973 having been repealed and folded into that Act effective January 1, 2003. It is led by an elected Mayor and City Council, with committees under Council governance; its current strategic plan is "Imagine Mississauga" (2026-2031). The backgrounder cites a ~$746.8 million 2025 operating budget and a ~$628.2 million gross 2025 capital budget. The Peel Transition Implementation Act, 2024 downloads Peel Regional roads and waste management to Mississauga, Brampton and Caledon effective July 1, 2026 — a partial-service transfer that replaced the earlier full-dissolution plan under the 2023 "Hazel McCallion Act" after the province reversed course in December 2023.

Priority evolution

bud-2024 (1a127260bcc9), "2024-2027 Business Plan and 2024 Budget," Mayor Bonnie Crombie: The only fully readable budget document in this series, and it lands squarely in a transitional year. The Mayor's message frames the budget around "Moving Mississauga Forward," and explicitly ties it to Mississauga "becom[ing] an independent city" — "a stronger, self-reliant city where we can shape our destiny." Four standing "corporate priorities," attributed to the City's Leadership Team, structure the whole document: (1) "Deliver the right services," (2) "Maintain City infrastructure," (3) "Advance on the City's strategic vision," (4) "Implement cost containment strategies" (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9). Layered on top, the document also organizes forward-looking initiatives under five Strategic Plan "pillars" — move, belong, connect, prosper, green — each mapped to specific 2024 work (light rail transit, housing affordability strategies, fire station construction, business-innovation hub, stormwater/climate planning) (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9). The document states a third refresh of the City's Strategic Plan (last refreshed 2009) was underway and slated for unveiling in 2024 as part of Mississauga's 50th-anniversary celebrations, intended to "address priorities that have shifted to the forefront in the intervening years like Indigenous relations, climate change, and equity, diversity and inclusion" (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9) — this is the direct documentary predecessor of the "Imagine Mississauga" 2026-2031 plan named in the backgrounder and in sp-2026 (1d01cbc79066), though the archived sp-2026 capture itself could not be read (see Series inventory).

fs-2022 (48a8118e2b43), "2022 Financial Report Highlights": A shorter, retrospective companion document (signed by Marisa Chiu, CPA, CA, Director of Finance and Treasurer, dated April 14, 2023) organizing 2022 achievements under the same four-part structure later formalized in bud-2024: "Deliver the Right Services," "Maintain City Infrastructure," "Advance on the City's Strategic Vision," "Implement Cost Containment Strategies" — indicating this four-priority framework predates bud-2024 by at least one budget cycle (fs-2022, 48a8118e2b43).

  • Added — Peel-dissolution/independent-city transition planning: bud-2024 (1a127260bcc9) names the Province's Bill 112, the Hazel McCallion Act, 2023 (in effect June 8, 2023), which at the time of that budget's writing was set to dissolve Peel Region and make Mississauga, Brampton and Caledon "stand-alone, independent cities on January 1, 2025," with a five-member provincially appointed transition board. Per the backgrounder, the Province reversed this full-dissolution plan in December 2023 in favour of the narrower Peel Transition Implementation Act, 2024 (partial service downloads — roads and waste management — effective July 1, 2026); bud-2024's own text (written under the still-current full-dissolution plan) is silent on this later reversal, which postdates the document.

Budget & mandate inflection points

  • June 8, 2023 — Bill 112, the Hazel McCallion Act, 2023, takes effect, setting a planned January 1, 2025 full dissolution of Peel Region into three independent cities (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9).
  • November 28, 2022 — Province passes Bill 23, the More Homes Built Faster Act, 2022; Mississauga is issued a target of 120,000 new housing units over 10 years (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9).
  • March 1, 2023 — Council endorses "Growing Mississauga: An Action Plan for New Housing," the City's housing pledge to the Province (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9).
  • December 18, 2023 — 2024 Budget adopted, with one amendment (a $1,146,900 gross donation to Hospice Mississauga) (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9).
  • 2024 Budget figures: Gross Operating Budget $1,188,494,000 (adopted); Net Operating Budget $679,456,000, a 6.3% increase over 2023; 2024 Capital Budget (net of recoveries) $436,977,000; 2024-2033 Capital Plan (net of recoveries) $8,905,719,000; residential tax bill impact 2.34% City / 4.87% Region / 0.00% Education = 7.21% total (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9).
  • State-of-good-repair infrastructure gap: bud-2024 (1a127260bcc9) states the City's infrastructure replacement cost at $15.3 billion (excluding land), with average annual SGR funding need of $240.2 million against available funding, leaving an average annual funding gap of approximately $44.0 million (2024-2033); a separate 100-year stormwater-pipe analysis (2024-2123) shows a further $30.1 million average annual gap against $50.8 million in identified need.
  • Bill 23 fiscal impact estimate: bud-2024 (1a127260bcc9) estimates Bill 23's reduced development-charge and parkland-fee rates could cost Mississauga $885 million in infrastructure funding over the next decade (a further $2 billion estimated lost across Peel Region as a whole).
  • 2022 year-end financial position (fs-2022, 48a8118e2b43): total revenues $1.16 billion (up $20.63 million from 2021's $1.14 billion); total expenses $1.01 billion (up $62.26 million from 2021's $948.91 million); total long-term debt $249.1 million; tangible capital assets net book value $8.58 billion; 'AAA-Stable' S&P credit rating maintained for a 19th consecutive year (per fs-2022) / most-recently described in bud-2024 as maintained for 20 consecutive years.
  • April 1, 2018 — City implemented a 4% Municipal Accommodation Tax on short-term accommodations, split equally between Tourism Mississauga and the City (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9).
  • 2024 — the Hazel McCallion Light Rail Transit Line (Metrolinx Hurontario LRT, 18 km) is described as under construction, opening imminent; a $45 million provincial Strategic Priorities Infrastructure Fund grant supports the South Common Community Centre and Library redevelopment (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9).

Ontario/Toronto relevance

Mississauga's own budget document is explicit and extensive about its GTA positioning, not merely adjacent to it: the City is described as "located on the shores of Lake Ontario west of Toronto," with "Downtown Toronto is a 30-minute drive" (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9). Named cross-boundary and shared-infrastructure items: MiWay (Mississauga's transit system, "Ontario's third-largest") connects with transit systems "in Toronto, Oakville, Brampton, and all GO Transit stations" (fs-2022, 48a8118e2b43); the University of Toronto Mississauga campus is named as one of the City's three post-secondary institutions and as a top-employer-adjacent anchor institution (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9; fs-2022, 48a8118e2b43); Pearson International Airport, "Canada's largest and busiest," is named as generating Payment-in-Lieu-of-Taxes revenue to the City via the Greater Toronto Airports Authority (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9); the Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area (GTHA) is used as the comparator geography for the City's demographic profile (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9); the Hazel McCallion LRT line and Dundas Bus Rapid Transit are named as regional-transit projects advanced jointly with Metrolinx (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9). Institutionally, Mississauga's 12 elected officials also sit on the Region of Peel's governing council, which is the reporting/service structure through which certain regional services (Public Health, Water and Wastewater, Waste Management, Social Housing, Long-Term Care, Paramedics, TransHelp, Ontario Works) are delivered across Mississauga, Brampton and Caledon (bud-2024, 1a127260bcc9) — the same regional structure the backgrounder describes as being partially unwound under the Peel Transition Implementation Act, 2024.
