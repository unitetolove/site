Indigenous context: Parks Recreation Access

No Indigenous person or organization has reviewed, endorsed, or participated in this work, and this page says so.

What the general finding misses

A word-boundary scan of the FLAGSHIP for Indigenous / First Nations / Métis / Inuit returns zero occurrences — confirmed this review. The FLAGSHIP names, in its own waterfront section, Biidaasige Park and Ookwemin Minising (the new Port Lands island) as the "generational public-space opportunity" of the Port Lands transformation, but never states that both names are Anishinaabemowin words gifted through a City-council-authorized, Indigenous-led naming process completed in November 2024 — nor names a single Indigenous organization, Elder, or nation involved in that naming, in the design of the park itself, or in any of the other Indigenous-led placemaking already active in Toronto's park system (High Park, the Toronto Islands, Exhibition Place). This overlay supplies that missing content.

Naming as placemaking: Ookwemin Minising and Biidaasige Park, in the process's own record

The City of Toronto's own November 1, 2024 news release — cited here as non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) for its own institutional facts, with the one directly quoted Elder's words travelling as Indigenous-authored within it per this library's Indigenous-sources framework's grey-area rule (a quoted individual's own words carry their own provenance even inside a document authored by someone else) — states the naming process:

"The announcement comes after a year-long engagement and consultation process where the City of Toronto turned over naming power to the Indigenous community as part of its commitment to reconciliation... This process included an Indigenous Advisory Circle comprised of Elders, Knowledge Keepers, language speakers, youth and community members, as well as broader engagement with First Nations, Inuit and Métis communities. The Circle met seven times this year, walking the land to discuss the area's history, cultural values and stories to determine the names." — City of Toronto, New island in the Port Lands named Ookwemin Minising, place of black cherry trees, November 1, 2024, https://www.toronto.ca/news/new-island-in-the-port-lands-named-ookwemin-minising-place-of-black-cherry-trees/ (⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) — a City-authored account of its own process, not an Indigenous position)

The Circle's chosen names, in the City's own reporting of them: Ookwemin Minising ("place of the black cherry trees") and Biidaasige Park ("sunlight shining towards us"), both Anishinaabemowin/Ojibwemowin. The engagement was led by MinoKamik Collective (already catalogued in this page’s nature-biodiversity-ravines companion overlay for its Port Lands ecological-restoration work — not re-derived here) under Elder Shelley Charles, who is directly quoted in her own words in the same release:

"I couldn't understand when [my father] said these are third and fourth generation trees as all the old ones were cut down. I cried as I couldn't understand how people could cut down these tree beings... Our footprints of our ancestors are here. When we think of this tree and how everyone wants to restore Indigenous plants, I can just imagine the conversation from other-than-human beings from land, water, air, insects, animals: it will be like seeing a long-lost friend!" — Elder Shelley Charles (Chippewas of Georgina Island First Nation, MinoKamik Collective), quoted in the same City of Toronto release (Indigenous-authored — her own words, individually attributed, within a non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people)-classed document)

The City's own park page on Indigenous placekeeping (re-fetched this review) states, as a non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) fact, which named nations it identifies as historically present on the site and what design elements the park now carries — cited here at the general level the City itself publishes, per S4:

"Indigenous communities – including the Petun, the Wendat, the Haudenosaunee and the Anishnabeg – had been fishing, hunting, growing food, gathering medicines, celebrating, healing and coming together in the area now known as Biidaasige Park, the Don River and Lake Ontario, long before European nations arrived." — City of Toronto, Indigenous Placekeeping at Biidaasige Park, https://www.toronto.ca/explore-enjoy/parks-recreation/places-spaces/beaches-gardens-attractions/biidaasige-park/indigenous-placekeeping-at-biidaasige-park/ (⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people)). The same page lists design elements developed in consultation with Indigenous partners — Indigenous plantings, interpretive signage, dodem animal sculptures "designed by APE Richter/Christian Huba in consultation with Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation and MinoKamik Collective," sturgeon-stone path markers by artist Solomon King, and marker trees developed with Nikibii Dawadinna Giigwag and Two Row Architect — named here at the level the City's own page states them, without further elaboration of ceremonial use, per S4.

High Park: Indigenous land stewardship in a flagship park

High Park is one of Toronto's most-visited parks and the site of active Indigenous land-stewardship advocacy — carried at fuller length in this page’s nature-biodiversity-ravines companion overlay (not re-derived in full here). The core statement, cross-referenced because it bears directly on this page’s own "park management" and "community stewardship" themes: the Indigenous Land Stewardship Circle states its purpose as working "to re-Indigenize High Park as a sacred site to Indigenous peoples" (https://indigenouslandstewardshipto.wordpress.com/high-park/, Indigenous-authored), and Catherine Tamarro (Wyandot elder) and Doug Anderson (Métis earthworker) are quoted directly by CBC stating the savannah "is a remnant of Indigenous land stewardship that exists in the city. It's very precious" and calling to "have those rights of stewardship returned to us" (https://www.cbc.ca/news/indigenous/high-park-indigenous-land-stewardship-1.5456265, co-produced). See the H6 companion overlay for the full quotes and the honest gap regarding the Circle's separate pesticide-ban statement.

The Toronto Islands: a gathering place, in Indigenous-owned media's own words

The Bawaadan Collective, an Indigenous-owned production company, was commissioned by the Waterfront BIA (in partnership with the City) to produce a film centring the Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation's own perspective on the Toronto Islands. The Collective's own artists' statement, re-verified this review, states its method and aim directly:

"As with many projects, the process begins at the research. Our approach to the research, development and concept of the videos is led by our understanding of community engagement best practices with Indigenous partners and First Nations representatives. We believe this composition to be an accurate representation of the community 'voice', their stories, and their hopes for the future. Massive Miigwetch / Mikwec to Darin Wybenga and Margaret Sault for their supreme trust and generosity." — Bawaadan Collective, Artists Statement, quoted by Waterfront BIA, The Indigenous History Of The Tkaronto Islands, https://www.waterfrontbia.com/stories/learn-about-indigenous-historical-and-cultural-significance-of-the-toronto-islands (Indigenous-authored)

Heritage Toronto's own award-nominee page (re-verified this review) states the film's own stated purpose directly — an ask, quoted rather than paraphrased per this project's attribution protocol:

"This short film asks its viewers to listen for the calls to action that the Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation are asking for as part of the development of the Toronto Islands, providing context to the treaties that cover Toronto, and how they relate to and impact the Toronto Islands." — Heritage Toronto, Missisakis: On The Indigenous History Of The Tkaronto Islands, https://www.heritagetoronto.org/programs/heritage-toronto-awards/2022-public-history-award-nominees/missisakis-indigenous-history-tkaronto/ (non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) as to Heritage Toronto's own framing of the film's purpose; the film itself, produced by the Indigenous-owned Bawaadan Collective working with named MCFN knowledge keepers Darin Wybenga and Margaret Sault, is Indigenous-authored — no verbatim statement from Wybenga or Sault appears in either page's own text, so none is represented as quoted here; see Honest gaps)

Exhibition Place: Indigenous-led public art as placemaking

Mino Bimaadiziwin – Good Life (2024), a 50-metre permanent public art installation at Exhibition Place's Centennial Park, was developed in direct partnership with MCFN. Chief Claire Sault is quoted in Exhibition Place's own page, re-verified this review:

"This art piece is a beautiful representation of our community's culture and identity, and we're looking forward to sharing this powerful expression of our culture with Torontonians and visitors alike." — Ogimaa-Kwe (Chief) Claire Sault, Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation, quoted at Exhibition Place, Mino Bimaadiziwin, https://www.explace.on.ca/mino-bimaadiziwin/ (co-produced — co-developed installation, MCFN-endorsed; the artists, August and Luke Swinson, are named members of the Mississaugas of Scugog Island First Nation)

The installation's own text panel, reproduced on the same page, states the philosophy behind the work's design — cited at the general level the page itself publishes, without further interpretation:

"Mino Bimaadiziwin is an Indigenous philosophy and way of being that translates to living a 'Good Life,' one where we reach our fullest potential, are interconnected with the Earth and in relationship with Creation... The perforations in the wall capture the paradigms of the seven dodems and the four scared medicines." — Exhibition Place, same source (a sacred-medicines list follows in the source at the same general naming level — Tobacco, Sage, Cedar, Sweetgrass — and is not elaborated further here, per S4)

Sport and recreation as Indigenous-led programming

Indigenous Sport & Wellness Ontario (ISWO), Ontario's recognized Provincial/Territorial Aboriginal Sport Body, states its own mission on its homepage (re-verified this review):

"Indigenous Sport & Wellness Ontario (ISWO) is dedicated to empowering Indigenous Peoples across Ontario through the transformative power of sport, recreation, and cultural connection. By fostering active and healthy communities, we create opportunities for Indigenous youth and families to thrive—physically, emotionally, mentally, and spiritually." — Indigenous Sport & Wellness Ontario, homepage, https://www.iswo.ca/ (Indigenous-authored)

"When sport programming is rooted in Indigenous traditions, languages, and values, it becomes a place where young people feel seen, understood, and connected to something larger than themselves." — Indigenous Sport & Wellness Ontario, same source (news teaser, July 20, 2026 article).

ISWO's programming operates provincially, not specifically inside Toronto's park system by name in anything catalogued — see Honest gaps.

Toronto Island Park Master Plan engagement — handled with S4 restraint

The City's own 2022 engagement summary, Revealing an Indigenous Place, documents extensive Indigenous community input into the Toronto Island Park master plan, including proposals for ceremonial space, renaming, and Indigenous-led placekeeping. Re-fetched and re-read this review. This file does not quote or summarize the document's specific site-level proposals (which include naming a specific island area as a formal ceremonial space) for two compounding reasons disclosed rather than resolved: (1) as previously found in this project's own review (in_their_own_words/05), the document reports "what we heard" only as City-paraphrased synthesis, never attributed to a named speaker, organization, or nation — it fails this project's own no-generic-attribution sourcing bar and cannot be cited as an Indigenous position under S1; and (2) per S4's ceremonial restraint and this lane's build brief, a specific proposed ceremonial-site location is exactly the category of not-yet-finalized, sacred-adjacent information this project resolves toward pointing rather than excerpting. The honest, general statement this document supports: the City has conducted a multi-year Indigenous engagement process on the Toronto Island Park master plan oriented around the practice of "Placekeeping," and the document itself is public at https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/8d4e-toronto-island-park-indigenous-place-summary-mar-1-2022.pdf (⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people)/co-produced as a whole; no individually-attributed quotable statement was found in it this review or in the prior pass).

Honest gaps this overlay does not close

  1. No verbatim statement from Darin Wybenga or Margaret Sault (the named MCFN knowledge keepers interviewed in the Bawaadan Collective's Missisakis film) appears in either the Heritage Toronto or Waterfront BIA page's own text — only the Collective's statement about them, and the film's own described purpose. The film itself (hosted on YouTube, not re-fetched or transcribed this review) would be the source for their own words directly; not reproduced here.
  2. The Toronto Island Park Master Plan's specific proposals are deliberately not detailed here — see the S4 discussion above. A future pass with an Indigenous-governed review seat, per the charter's §3 "seat arc," is the appropriate route to determine whether and how those specific proposals should ever be summarized publicly by this project.
  3. No catalogued Indigenous-published position on the Parkland Strategy's equity-weighted Acquisition Priority Areas framework was found this review — the framework the FLAGSHIP treats as its central "tools that exist" case. Unsearched gap, not a confirmed absence.
  4. No catalogued Indigenous-published position on POPS regulation or hostile design — the FLAGSHIP's "quality of the public realm" theme — was found. Unsearched gap, not a confirmed absence.
  5. ISWO's own programming is not confirmed to operate inside any specific Toronto City park by name in anything catalogued this review; it is carried here as the clearest catalogued Indigenous-led sport/recreation organization active in Ontario, not as a Toronto-park-specific claim.
  6. Per S4: ceremonial and sacred-medicine specifics named on the Exhibition Place and Biidaasige Park pages (the four sacred medicines, the Fire Holder design element) are cited only at the general level those pages themselves publish; this file does not describe protocol, sourcing, or use beyond what the public pages themselves state.

  • Authority to control: every claim above is a direct attributed quotation from the organization's, Elder's, or Chief's own published statement (or, for City-authored process facts, flagged non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) and not used to state a position); the one deliberately unexcerpted document (the Island Park engagement summary) is named as such rather than quoted around.
  • Responsibility: no relationship exists between this project and MCFN, MinoKamik Collective, the Bawaadan Collective, or ISWO — the honest-limitation disclosure in an earlier internal research file §7 applies in full.
  • Ethics: this block keeps MCFN, MinoKamik, Bawaadan Collective, and ISWO distinct throughout; does not synthesize a single "Indigenous placemaking position"; states the co-produced nature of the Exhibition Place installation and the City-authored nature of the naming-process and placekeeping pages explicitly; and withholds the Toronto Island Park master plan's specific ceremonial-space proposals rather than amplifying not-yet-finalized, sacred-adjacent City- paraphrased content, consistent with S4's instruction to resolve toward the reading that takes less and points more (S10).
