Indigenous context: Housing Supply Affordability

No Indigenous person or organization has reviewed, endorsed, or participated in this work, and this page says so.

What the general supply-and-affordability finding misses

A word-boundary scan of all three carried-forward documents this review returns zero occurrences of indigenous / first nations / métis / inuit / aboriginal. The leaf argues Auckland-style upzoning, Minneapolis filtering chains, EHON multiplex permissions, the provincially-capped 5% inclusionary zoning rate, HAF milestones, and Canada's thin non-market sector (~3.5–4% of stock) without naming a single Indigenous housing provider, framework, or supply target — despite Ontario's urban Indigenous housing sector having published a full policy framework with its own numeric supply ask in September 2025, and despite Toronto Indigenous developers speaking publicly about the exact municipal approval machinery the page’s zoning document analyses.

Ontario's urban Indigenous housing framework, in its own words

The 2025 OUR Framework (Ontario Urban & Rural First Nations, Inuit & Métis Housing Policy Framework) is the first update since 2013. Ontario Aboriginal Housing Services states it was "developed collaboratively by the Housing Policy Team comprised of housing policy staff from Ontario Aboriginal Housing Services, Ontario Native Women's Association, Ontario Federation of Indigenous Friendship Centres, and the Métis Nation of Ontario," that "In total, 657 individuals' voices inform this report," and that engagement "concluded on October 25, 2024, in Tkoronto (Toronto)." https://www.ontarioaboriginalhousing.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/OAHS_OUR-Framework.pdf (Indigenous-authored)

Its headline recommendation is a supply number this page does not carry:

"Work collaboratively with urban Indigenous partners to add 22,000 subsidized Indigenous-led housing units across Ontario by 2035." — Ontario Aboriginal Housing Services, 2025 OUR Framework, Recommendations (the 22,000 figure is attributed in the report to ONPHA's 2020 cost-benefit analysis of urban Indigenous housing need). Paired with: "Prioritize investments in the preservation of all existing rent-geared-to-income Urban Native Housing (UNH) units across the sector."

On affordability definitions — the same terrain as the page’s inclusionary-zoning section — the report sets its own threshold: "Housing costs should not exceed 25% of a household's income - costs include rent, heat, hydro, water, sewer and other costs required to maintain housing," a standard the report says is "Often referred to as 'deeply affordable' housing – distinguishing it from market-based definitions of affordability." Its municipal ask is stated directly: "Municipalities must embed Indigenous housing supply targets into local planning, formalize Indigenous engagement, and ensure urban Indigenous communities directly shape housing policy" — and its one recommendation addressed to municipalities is to "Formalize and support engagement with local urban Indigenous communities to ensure that municipal planning and decision-making on housing priorities are directly informed by local urban Indigenous communities."

The report also reproduces community-engagement statements that speak to this page’s core supply-versus-affordability question. These are individual participant statements as OAHS published them, not a position of any nation or organization:

"This is the real answer to the housing crisis, rather than just adding more supply - it needs to taget deeply affordable." — participant, Ottawa community engagement session, reproduced in the 2025 OUR Framework (spelling "taget" preserved exactly as published; not a transcription error introduced here).

"The linear model doesn't make sense because it says there is a beginning and an end, that's not true, circle model is continuous, it takes you back and forth through life journey events. Any life crisis can take you to a different place along the continuum - it's not a neat and tidy line." — participant, Blind River community engagement session, reproduced in the same report's "Rethinking the Housing Continuum" section, which OAHS uses to replace the linear shelter-to-homeownership continuum with its web-based "OUR Housing Journey" model.

OAHS's own launch release states the framework's premise in its staff's words:

"Indigenous people continue to be overrepresented in precarious living conditions, yet our voices are too often left out of housing policy and funding decisions." — Peggy Rice, Senior Housing Policy Analyst, OAHS, September 19, 2025, https://www.ontarioaboriginalhousing.ca/news/new-report-calls-for-urgent-action-on-urban-indigenous-housing-crisis-in-ontario (Indigenous-authored). The same release states: "Across Ontario, Indigenous people represent only 3% of the population but account for up to 50% of the unhoused population, and as high as 88% in some urban centres."

Toronto Indigenous developers on the City's own supply levers

Na-Me-Res (Native Men's Residence) speaks directly to the approval machinery this issue's own promoted research document analyses:

"To get a building permit quickly, you're looking at maybe two years. That's how that process works. There's a lot of rhetoric about speeding up affordable housing. And that's all it amounts to is rhetoric because at the municipal level, usually at least in Toronto, all the hoops you have to jump through take a long time." — Steve Teekens, Executive Director, Na-Me-Res, in Na-Me-Res strives against all odds to house Indigenous men in Toronto, Windspeaker.com (Aboriginal Multi-Media Society), June 19, 2026, https://windspeaker.com/news/windspeaker-news/na-me-res-strives-against-all-odds-house-indigenous-men-toronto (Indigenous-authored — Indigenous-owned media, subject quoted directly)

The same piece reports that "Teekens believes the process could be partly sped up by eliminating statutory community consultations," and quotes his reason: "In my experience, every time we have to consult with neighbours when we're moving into a neighbourhood, I always experience overt racism in my face." He puts the unmet need at "about 700 to 800 additional units are required for Indigenous men in Toronto alone." On his organization's own pipeline: "Our new Carlton Street affordable housing project marks the first of three builds we are developing to help address the crisis of Indigenous people facing and experiencing homelessness in this city" (Anishinabek News, July 30, 2024, https://anishinabeknews.ca/2024/07/na-me-res-opens-non-profit-housing-in-toronto-providing-permanent-homes-for-indigenous-men-experiencing-homelessness/, Indigenous-authored). ⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people): the delivery mechanism for that building — a City Section 37 agreement with MOD Developments, conveying 218 Carlton Street to the City — is stated by Mayor Olivia Chow in the same release; cited here only as a government-response fact, supporting no claim about what Indigenous organizations need or want.

Wigwamen Incorporated, "Ontario's oldest and largest urban Indigenous housing provider" (founded 1972), states on its own site that it "owns and operates more than 865 units of affordable housing," and describes its supply activity in its own words: "With the ongoing dire shortage of affordable housing in the Province of Ontario, Wigwamen has been doing everything in its power to help create additional housing units." Its named Toronto-area project underway is a 62-unit building begun April 2024 at 515 Markham Road, Scarborough. https://www.wigwamen.com/ (Indigenous-authored)

Métis-specific supply, kept distinct

The Métis Nation of Ontario's 2024-2034 Housing and Homelessness Action Plan reports its own build and acquisition pipeline rather than an advocacy ask alone: "The MNO has also acquired two (2) building sites, one in Kenora and one in Midland, building a new supply of thirty (30) and fourteen (14) units of affordable housing, respectively," plus "twenty (20) units of affordable rental townhomes in Sault Ste. Marie, and six (6) units of affordable rentals in Weston in the GTA," against a stated target to "Build at least 9 affordable rental development pilots, 1 per MNO Region." https://www.metisnation.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/2024-2034-Housing-and-Homelessness-Action-Plan-web.pdf (Indigenous-authored). The plan's own framing of housing as a right is already quoted in an earlier internal research file and is not re-derived here.

Honest gaps this overlay does not close

  1. No catalogued Indigenous-authored or co-produced source addresses Toronto's named supply levers. Nothing in the catalog states a position on EHON, the 2025 sixplex vote, Major Transit Station Areas, the provincial 5% inclusionary-zoning cap, development charges, or the Housing Accelerator Fund by name. Teekens' permit-timeline and consultation statements are the closest catalogued material, and they are about Indigenous-led non-profit development specifically — not about as-of-right zoning reform in general.
  2. No published Indigenous assessment of the filtering/supply evidence itself. The one catalogued statement bearing on whether more supply helps is one OAHS engagement participant's words, Ontario-wide, reproduced above; it is not a Toronto finding and not an organizational position. The page’s Auckland/Minneapolis/Mast evidence base has no catalogued Indigenous-authored commentary.
  3. Urban Inuit housing in Toronto is uncovered here. Tungasuvvingat Inuit's own annual report and strategic plan are catalogued, but tiontario.ca (and ofifc.org) returned a SiteGround captcha challenge to fetch tools this review, so no quote from either lands in this file; the OFIFC material above is cited only as co-authorship of the OUR Framework, which is verified from the framework's own text.

CARE check (this library's Indigenous-sources framework, applied to this addition)

  • Collective benefit: places the urban Indigenous housing sector's own published supply target, affordability definition, and municipal ask beside a flagship that argues supply volumes without them, and names the Indigenous-led providers actually building units in Toronto.
  • Authority to control: every position, ask, and grievance above is a direct attributed quote or a pointer to the organization's own publication; the single government-sourced fact is flagged ⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) and supports no needs/wants claim.
  • Responsibility: no relationship exists between this project and OAHS, ONWA, OFIFC, MNO, Na-Me-Res, Wigwamen, or TICAB — the honest-limitation disclosure in an earlier internal research file §7 applies in full.
  • Ethics: this block does not present the OUR Framework (Ontario-wide, urban and rural) as a position on Toronto's zoning reforms; does not blend OAHS, MNO, Na-Me-Res and Wigwamen into a single synthesized Indigenous position on housing supply; and does not claim any of them has reviewed or endorsed this page.
