Indigenous context: Nature Biodiversity Ravines

No Indigenous person or organization has reviewed, endorsed, or participated in this work, and this page says so.

What the general finding misses

A word-boundary scan of the FLAGSHIP (\bIndigenous\b) returns 32 occurrences on 11 lines — matching the overlay audit's own count exactly, verified this review. Every single occurrence is general framing language: "in relationship with Indigenous land stewardship," "the forthcoming Indigenous suite," "in relationship with Indigenous nations," "two-eyed seeing." Zero of the 32 name a single nation, organization, or person; zero cite anything any Indigenous nation or organization has actually published about the ravines, the water, or biodiversity in Toronto. The FLAGSHIP repeatedly promises deferral to "the forthcoming Indigenous suite" — which already exists in part (that page's own internal records, per this library's Indigenous-sources framework but is never linked from this page. This overlay is that missing content: what the Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation, the Williams Treaties First Nations, and Indigenous-led land-stewardship organizations working inside Toronto's ravines and parks have actually published, in their own words, plus what Ontario First Nations Technical Services Corporation has published on water governance more broadly.

Treaty ground: what the treaty holder states about land and water

Toronto's ravines sit on Treaty 13 (the Toronto Purchase) lands; the Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation (MCFN) is the treaty holder (this library's Indigenous-sources framework names MCFN's own site as the Indigenous-authored anchor for this framing, not a third-party summary). MCFN's own site states its territory directly:

"The Mississaugas of the Credit occupied, controlled and exercised stewardship over approximately 3.9 million acres of lands, waters, and resources in Southern Ontario. Their territory extended from the Rouge River Valley westward across to the headwaters of the Thames River, down to Long Point on Lake Erie and then followed the shoreline of Lake Erie, the Niagara River, and Lake Ontario until arriving back at the Rouge River Valley." — Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation, homepage, https://mncfn.ca/ (Indigenous-authored)

On water specifically, MCFN's own title-claim page (filed September 2016) states:

"Our people continue to revere water as a spiritual being that must be accorded respect and dignity. Water is also vital to the survival of the Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation and all other forms of life." — Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation, Title Claim to Water within Traditional Lands of MCFN, https://mncfn.ca/treaty-lands-territory/title-claim-to-water-within-traditional-lands-of-mncfn/ (Indigenous-authored)

"As stewards of the lands and waters, we advocate for a healthy environment for the people and wildlife that live within our treaty lands and territory. The Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation remains committed, as we have been for generations, to utilizing, protecting and caring for the waters in a holistic way that promotes continued sustainability." — Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation, same source.

"The Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation asserts that we have unextinguished Aboriginal title to all water, beds of water, and floodplains contained in our 3.9 million acres of treaty lands and territory. There is no mention of water in any of the treaties between the Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation and the crown but for surrender #23 which we allege is invalid." — Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation, same source.

MCFN's Department of Consultation & Accommodation (DOCA) states its own mandate over development affecting land, water, and species at risk across its treaty territory — which spans the GTA, including Toronto's ravine system:

"To engage in consultation processes with the Crown (Federal and Provincial governments) and private sector proponents on actions and proposed development projects that may impact the rights and interests of MCFN and to ensure that environmentally sustainable practices are being implemented in response to any concerns about the land, water, air, species at risk, and the health of its people." — Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation, Department of Consultation & Accommodation (DOCA) — Mandate, https://mncfn.ca/doca-2/ (Indigenous-authored). Re-fetched this review: the DOCA page has grown substantially since original cataloguing — it now describes an Archaeology Unit, a Field Liaison Representative program, and a Burial Site Protocol not present in the row's original gist; the mandate quote above is unchanged and verified word-for-word against the live page.

MCFN's own account of the Rouge Tract Settlement (2025) — a $183.4 million settlement ($108.4M from Canada, $75M from Ontario) of MCFN's 2015 aboriginal-title claim to the Rouge River Valley — was re-fetched this review and remains, as previously catalogued, purely institutional/historical narrative with no first-person or attributable position statement on the page (https://mncfn.ca/treaty-lands-territory/rouge-claim/, Indigenous-authored; cited here as a factual anchor, not quoted).

Dish With One Spoon — the covenant, and the caution that travels with it

The Dish With One Spoon is invoked in this corpus's land-relationship content and is directly relevant to how "sharing the land" gets framed for the ravines. Per this library's Indigenous-sources framework's standing rule, carried forward here rather than re-derived: it is not a treaty with government and must never be cited as though it were the same kind of instrument as Treaty 13. Talking Treaties (a project of Jumblies Theatre + Arts, described on its own site as Indigenous-led) states:

"An agreement grounded in Indigenous legal traditions, the Dish with One Spoon is one of several Wampum that commemorate the relationship between the Anishinaabek and Haudenosaunee Confederacy. Today it is held up by Indigenous Peoples in Toronto as peace agreement between the Anishinaabek and Haudensaunee. It is also widely cited (and admired) for the reciprocal responsibilities with all of creation that it foregrounds, a reading that draws on the land as a dish to be shared and cared for to ensure ongoing sustenance and life - a metaphor that is part of many Indigenous knowledge systems." — Talking Treaties (Jumblies Theatre + Arts), The Dish with One Spoon, https://talkingtreaties.ca/treaties-for-torontonians/dish-with-one-spoon/ (Indigenous-authored; spelling "Haudensaunee" appears exactly this way in the live source and is preserved, not corrected)

Water governance beyond Toronto, in First Nations' own words

The Williams Treaties First Nations (whose treaty area reaches into Scarborough) state their own relationship to land and water directly:

"Williams Treaties First Nations have a special relationship with the lands, including the water and resources — not only in their traditional area, but throughout gichii mukinaak (Big Turtle). Protection, conservation and sustainable collaborative management are a priority for the Williams Treaties First Nations." — Williams Treaties First Nations, homepage, https://williamstreatiesfirstnations.ca/ (Indigenous-authored)

The Ontario First Nations Technical Services Corporation (OFNTSC)'s water-governance position paper states its argument for First Nations jurisdiction over water directly:

"As stated by Chief Del Riley, 'Indigenous people are at a crossroads… there is a resurgence of Indigenous sovereignty on unceded lands… operating on the basis of inherent Indigenous rights.'" — Ontario First Nations Technical Services Corporation, Decolonizing Water Governance: Addressing the Water Crisis in Ontario through Recognition of First Nations Jurisdiction, July 2022, https://ofntsc.org/sites/default/files/2022-11/2022_July_OFNTSC_Water_Position_Paper.pdf (Indigenous-authored; ellipses mark internal omissions as rendered in the source document itself)

"No longer can First Nations tolerate the status quo that, under Canada's watch, perpetuates oppression and denies fundamental Indigenous and human rights." — Ontario First Nations Technical Services Corporation, same source, Executive Summary.

OFNTSC's paper also quotes, at length, the Garma International Indigenous Water Declaration (endorsed 2008 by Indigenous peoples internationally, reproduced by OFNTSC as a guiding framework rather than an OFNTSC-authored statement):

"Nation-States have grossly mismanaged the lands and waters of Indigenous Peoples, causing ecosystem collapse, human induced climate change, severe water quality degradation, extreme stress upon ecologies and species extinction at a scale and rate which is unprecedented." — the Garma International Indigenous Water Declaration, quoted in Ontario First Nations Technical Services Corporation, same source.

(OFNTSC's circular-economy material — Heidi Manitowabi's "all things happen in cycles" framing — is already carried by the urban-agriculture-food-systems overlay's Honest gaps section and the in_their_own_words/10 theme file; not re-derived here.)

Land stewardship inside Toronto's ravines, in stewards' own words

High Park's black oak savannah — one of the ten Ravine Strategy Priority Investment Areas — is where the Indigenous Land Stewardship Circle (ILSC), a collective of Elders, knowledge holders, and urban Indigenous community members, has been most vocal. ILSC's own site states its purpose in one sentence:

"The Indigenous Land Stewardship Circle is working to re-Indigenize High Park as a sacred site to Indigenous peoples." — Indigenous Land Stewardship Circle, High Park, https://indigenouslandstewardshipto.wordpress.com/high-park/ (Indigenous-authored)

Catherine Tamarro (Wyandot elder, Wyandot of Anderdon Nation) and Doug Anderson (Métis earthworker), both members of the Circle, are quoted directly by CBC:

"What we're talking about is restoring the right relations with the land." ... "It is a remnant of Indigenous land stewardship that exists in the city. It's very precious." — Catherine Tamarro, quoted in CBC News, 'It's very precious': Indigenous collective wants input into managing High Park's oak savannah, https://www.cbc.ca/news/indigenous/high-park-indigenous-land-stewardship-1.5456265 (co-produced — CBC-hosted, built on direct named quotation)

"We need to teach our kids how to heal the land. We have to have relationships with the land and we have to know how things grow and balance and how you can get a lot of food off it. We need to actually live with all of these things and in balance." — Doug Anderson, same source.

This is a high-stakes ask, quoted rather than paraphrased per this project's own attribution protocol: the article reports the Circle opposes pesticide use for invasive-species management in the savannah and hopes the City will offer future restoration contracts to Indigenous stewards. ILSC's own site independently confirms it holds this position in the plainest terms available — its own navigation menu, verified live this review, names a standing page: "ILSC Calls for a complete ban on pesticide Use in High Park: Indigenous Stewardship is Climate Action and Harm Reduction" (https://indigenouslandstewardshipto.wordpress.com/ceremony-and-actions/). That specific page's body text was not fetched this review and no further content from it is quoted here — see Honest gaps.

MinoKamik Collective, an Elder-led circle providing Traditional Knowledge-based landscape restoration for Toronto's Port Lands Flood Protection project and other urban-nature sites, states its own composition and purpose:

"MinoKamik Collective includes a circle of elders and consultants from a wide range of backgrounds including: Indigenous Traditional Knowledge (TK), environmental stewardship, horticulture, species at risk, Indigenous plant restoration and water conservation. MinoKamik includes elders Shelley Charles (Georgina Island), Jim Dumont (Shawanaga), Melanie Sifton, Lynn Short, and Jonathon Araujo Redbird (Saugeen)." — MinoKamik Collective, homepage, https://www.minokamik.ca/ (Indigenous-authored)

"MinoKamik refers to the spring season; a time of renewal and celebration greeting the fresh new earth." — Mandakwe, MinoKamik Collective, same source.

Elder Shelley Charles, MinoKamik's lead, also spoke in her own words at the City's November 2024 naming announcement for the new Port Lands island and park (see parks-recreation-access's companion overlay for the fuller naming-process record; not re-derived here) — a direct link between this page’s ravine/biodiversity restoration content and D3's parks content that neither leaf should re-derive from the other.

City engagement records — non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) facts only

The City's own Ravine Strategy page (re-fetched this review, last modified July 21, 2026) states two facts about its own Indigenous engagement, cited here as non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) facts only — neither carries an Indigenous position, per S3:

"City Council has authorized staff to explore a new name, Wonscotonach Parklands, to create an identity for the network of interconnected parks and trails in the Lower Don Valley. Indigenous communities have been consulted on the name and further engagement and consultations are under way prior to a formal naming. An Indigenous name for the Lower Don parklands is just the beginning of working with Indigenous leadership on programming, place-making and identity projects." — City of Toronto, Ravine Strategy, https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/accountability-operations-customer-service/long-term-vision-plans-and-strategies/ravine-strategy/ (⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) — a City-authored fact about a consultation in progress, not a stated Indigenous position on the outcome)

"Sharing stories about special and historic spaces, including traditional Indigenous knowledge, will improve how we connect and relate to these natural spaces." — City of Toronto, same source (⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) — the City's own stated intention, not an Indigenous organization's position)

Honest gaps this overlay does not close

  1. No catalogued Indigenous-published position was found on Toronto's Biodiversity Strategy or Pollinator Protection Strategy by name. The FLAGSHIP names both strategies as City leadership; no Indigenous-authored or co-produced source in the catalog states a position on either specifically.
  2. No catalogued Indigenous-published position was found on human-wildlife coexistence (coyotes, raccoons, geese) — a tension the FLAGSHIP names explicitly. This is a real, unsearched gap, not a confirmed absence.
  3. The ILSC's pesticide-ban page's body text was not fetched this review — only its title, visible in the parent page's live navigation menu, is verified and quoted above. No further content from that specific page is represented as quoted here; a future pass should fetch it directly rather than relying on the CBC-reported summary of the Circle's position.
  4. MCFN's Rouge Tract Settlement page carries no quotable first-person statement, confirmed again this review — cited only as a factual anchor (the $183.4M settlement figure), not quoted.
  5. Toronto Nature Stewards, named in the FLAGSHIP as the community-stewardship model to power ravine restoration, was not checked this review for any Indigenous-specific angle or position — an unsearched gap, not a confirmed absence.
  6. Per S4 (ceremonial restraint): this file does not describe ILSC's ceremonial practices beyond the general "re-Indigenize... as a sacred site" framing ILSC itself publishes, and does not elaborate on what "restoring stewardship rights" or "Indigenous programming" at High Park would concretely involve beyond what Tamarro and Anderson themselves stated to CBC. Medicine plants and specific traditional-use practices at High Park (noted in the CBC article's own background reporting — the savannah "is a place for traditional medicines and berries to grow") are named at the general level the source itself uses and not elaborated further.
