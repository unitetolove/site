Indigenous context: Water Great Lakes Stewardship

No Indigenous person or organization has reviewed, endorsed, or participated in this work, and this page says so.

What the general finding misses

The FLAGSHIP treats water as excellent drinking water, a threatened living lake, and a "strategic, sovereignty-laden asset" to guard "as a commons, not a commodity" against a covetous United States. It never names that First Nations themselves already hold an asserted title claim to the water Toronto draws from, and that the "commons vs. commodity" question it poses as a future risk is one Ontario First Nations have already answered, in a standing position paper to the province. The flagship's own "shared, multi-jurisdictional" honesty stops one jurisdiction short.

MCFN's own asserted title to the water Toronto draws from

MCFN, the Treaty 13 holder, filed an Aboriginal title claim to waters within its traditional lands in September 2016 — re-verified live this review:

"Our people continue to revere water as a spiritual being that must be accorded respect and dignity. Water is also vital to the survival of the Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation and all other forms of life... The Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation asserts that we have unextinguished Aboriginal title to all water, beds of water, and floodplains contained in our 3.9 million acres of treaty lands and territory." — Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation, Title Claim to Water within Traditional Lands of MCFN, https://mncfn.ca/treaty-lands-territory/title-claim-to-water-within-traditional-lands-of-mncfn/ (Indigenous-authored). Ellipsis omits one sentence naming surrender #23 as the sole treaty water mention.

OFNTSC: "water should not solely be treated as a resource or a commodity"

OFNTSC states its position directly, in a July 2022 paper re-verified live via pdftotext:

"OFNTSC holds the position that water should not solely be treated as a resource or a commodity. Further, OFNTSC strongly holds that a new approach to water governance designed and led by First Nations is direly needed in Ontario... the water crisis is not a project management issue: it is an Indigenous rights, human rights, and a sustainability issue." — OFNTSC, Decolonizing Water Governance, July 2022, https://ofntsc.org/sites/default/files/2022-11/2022_July_OFNTSC_Water_Position_Paper.pdf (Indigenous-authored)

OFNTSC quotes the Nibi Declaration of Treaty #3 as a distinct guiding framework:

"Nibi (water) is alive and has a spirit... Nibi cannot be owned or controlled, it is shared across lands and territories, nations, and all other life forms." — Grand Council of Treaty #3, Nibi Declaration, quoted in OFNTSC, same source.

Anishinabek Nation: water as a treaty right and a gendered responsibility

"The citizens of the Anishinabek Nation have Treaty and Aboriginal Rights with respect to water... First Nation women (Anishinabe qwe) were given the role to be the traditional keepers and spiritual protectors of water." — Union of Ontario Indians / Anishinabek Nation, Anishinabek Regional Water Engagement Sessions, https://www.anishinabek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/Anishinabek-Regional-Water-Session-Report.pdf (Indigenous-authored). States 66 First Nation communities sit within Ontario's Great Lakes Basin Watershed, 40 of them Anishinabek Nation members — re-verified live this review.

Distinctness, kept exact

  • MCFN — Treaty 13 holder, a filed legal title claim over its own territory (incl. Toronto).
  • OFNTSC — a services body states plainly it "is not a rights-holder... has no mandate to speak on behalf of Ontario First Nations"; offers options, not jurisdiction, on any nation's behalf.
  • Anishinabek Nation — a political territorial body reporting a multi-community process.
  • Indigenous Climate Hub — checked; JavaScript-gated shell, no text accessible. Counted in the audited base; not quoted; no workaround attempted.

Honest gaps this overlay does not close

  1. No catalogued source addresses Toronto Water's own operations, sewage overflows, or its "Area of Concern" status — the flagship's strongest register. Grep-checked; zero matches.
  2. No named MCFN/Williams Treaties position on the binational Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement.
  3. Indigenous Climate Hub yielded no quotable text — inaccessible this review, not a confirmed silence.

CARE check (this library's Indigenous-sources framework

  • Collective benefit: surfaces a filed title claim over the water this city drinks, and First Nations' own rejection of the commodity framing the flagship raises only as a future risk.
  • Authority to control: every claim is a direct, attributed quotation, verified live.
  • Responsibility: no relationship exists between this project and MCFN, OFNTSC, or the Anishinabek Nation — this library's Indigenous-sources framework's disclosure applies in full.
  • Ethics: MCFN, OFNTSC, the Nibi Declaration (Treaty #3, not OFNTSC's own voice), and the Anishinabek Nation are kept as four separate voices, never blended into one water position.
