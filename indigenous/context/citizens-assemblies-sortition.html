Indigenous context: Citizens Assemblies Sortition

No Indigenous person or organization has reviewed, endorsed, or participated in this work, and this page says so.

What the general leaf misses entirely

Both citizens-assemblies-sortition's and participatory-budgeting-outcomes's promoted flagship documents are AI-authored expert briefings citing no Indigenous organization by name. The citizens'-assembly flagship is a partial exception in one respect: its own "Toronto-specific factors" section and its ninth policy recommendation both raise the question of how a sortition body relates to Indigenous governance —

"Second, and foundational, Indigenous governance and Treaty relationships: Toronto sits on Indigenous land with living Treaty obligations and pre-existing Indigenous governance and consultation rights. Any claim that a sortition body 'speaks for the community' has to reckon with this — a randomly-selected assembly does not displace Indigenous self-determination or duty-to-consult, and a credible Toronto design should define how deliberative democracy relates to Indigenous governance rather than silently overriding it." — this issue's own promoted research document, "Toronto-specific factors" (not an Indigenous source; the general page’s own AI-authored text, quoted here only to show what it raises)

"Define the relationship to Indigenous governance — set out, from the start, how a sortition body relates to Indigenous self-determination, Treaty obligations, and duty-to-consult; a randomly-selected assembly cannot claim to 'speak for the community' while overriding pre-existing Indigenous governance rights." — same document, Policy recommendation #8

The general leaf asks the right question and answers it with zero cited Indigenous voices. This overlay is the first document on this page to supply any. Neither of the two positions below resolves that open design question for the general leaf — they are offered as exactly what they are: named organizations' own published stances on a related but distinct question (settler electoral participation, not assembly design).

Two published positions, unadjudicated: an urban Indigenous organization that promotes voting, and a Confederacy that has physically stopped it

IMPORTANT — this overlay's core finding. This movement runs civic-participation campaigns. Per S1, this overlay does not enlist either position below for that agenda. It maps what these organizations have published, including a position that argues directly against participation in settler elections — carried exactly as its authors state it, not softened, not endorsed, not adjudicated against the position that favours it.

Position 1 — participation-encouraging: TASSC's Aboriginal Civic Literacy Initiative (Toronto)

The Toronto Aboriginal Support Services Council (TASSC — 24 Indigenous-led member agencies, already catalogued at an earlier internal research file row 16) publishes its own account of a civic-engagement program aimed at increasing Toronto's urban Aboriginal community's participation in settler government:

"ACLI seeks to strengthen the Toronto Aboriginal community's desire to engage with all levels of government ultimately aiming to increase governmental attention and resources to effectively meet the needs and aspirations of the urban Aboriginal community at large." — Toronto Aboriginal Support Services Council (Indigenous-authored), Aboriginal Civic Literacy Initiative (ACLI), https://tassc.ca/?page_id=34

"ACLI promotes civic literacy by enhancing basic knowledge of the respective roles and responsibilities that are assigned to the municipal, provincial, and federal government. Consequently, resources were developed to raise awareness of the reasons and options for effective civic engagement, including a series of four informational pamphlets, a resource booklet, and a promotional video, which was delivered to the Toronto urban Aboriginal community at four knowledge sharing sessions in March 2014." — same source.

The same page hosts 14 "Voting Fact Sheets" (federal-election-themed: housing, health care, jobs, poverty reduction, public transit, climate change, seniors' income security) and four "Civic Engagement Pamphlets" — Our Numbers Can Make a Difference, Our Government Briefly Explained, Our Motivations and Issues, Our Options for Action — TASSC's own materials, still hosted live on its own site this review. Freshness caveat, stated plainly: the program dates to March 2014; the page shows no indication of activity since, and this review found no evidence TASSC has repeated or updated ACLI for a more recent election cycle. It is carried here as TASSC's own standing publication, not as a claim that the program is current or ongoing.

Position 2 — sovereignty-based non-participation: the Haudenosaunee Confederacy Chiefs Council

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy Chiefs Council (HCCC) — whose traditional territory, per this lane's own standing Toronto-grounding reference (this library's Indigenous-sources framework, the Dish With One Spoon covenant), extends to the wider region this project operates in, though Six Nations of the Grand River itself sits roughly 100 km southwest of Toronto — has published and acted on the opposite position: that voting in Canadian elections is itself incompatible with Haudenosaunee sovereignty. In September 2021, HCCC published its own account of enforcing that position against a federal election:

"On Wednesday, Sept. 15, 2021, the Haudenosaunee Confederacy Chiefs and Clanmothers released a statement regarding federal elections being held on Haudenosaunee territory... On election day, total regard for the statement of our Confederacy Chiefs and Clanmothers had been ignored and the polling stations were being prepared at the Gathering Place. All entrances to the polling station on the territory were blocked by Confederacy members which resulted in many police officers attending the site of polling. This was a peaceful protest in support of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy Chiefs and Clanmothers and remained peaceful until the polls were removed." — Haudenosaunee Confederacy (Indigenous-authored), Federal Election Removed From Haudenosaunee Territory, September 21, 2021, https://www.haudenosauneeconfederacy.com/2021/09/federal-election-removed-from-haudenosaunee-territory/

"The people have decided to do their responsibility, the Haudenosaunee people, we are here backing up the statement of Confederacy Council that went out this past Wednesday. The peoples responsibility is to enforce the will of the Council, that is what we are doing. As Haudenosaunee men and women, we know our roles. Our great peace, that is what we are exercising here." — Colin Martin, quoted in the same HCCC-published article.

APTN — Indigenous-owned national news media, classed Indigenous-authored per this library's Indigenous-sources framework's own listed example — covered the same event and is the only source in this review to carry the Confederacy Council's own statement text directly, alongside a second, distinct Six Nations voice:

"It has been brought to our attention that the Canadian government has placed polling stations on Haudenosaunee Territory... We urge those involved to immediately remove the polling stations and elections materials from the territory. This is a violation of not only treaty rights, but our human rights to exist as distinct people." — Haudenosaunee Confederacy Chiefs Council statement, quoted in APTN National News (Indigenous-authored), Polling station moved off Six Nations after demand from confederacy, September 20, 2021, https://www.aptnnews.ca/national-news/elections-canada-polling-station-on-six-nations-territory-forced-to-move-to-nearby-town/

"Chiefs and clan mothers contend Haudenosaunee voting in Canadian elections is 'a violation of treaties and commitments our ancestors made', referencing the Two Row Wampum agreement made between the Haudenosaunee and Dutch settlers more than 400 years ago 'to never interfere in one another's government, laws and ways.' 'We have never relinquished our sovereignty and we view the actions of all involved in Canada's elections as doing such,' the Confederacy statement said." — same APTN article.

The two bodies are not the same, and this overlay does not resolve their disagreement

APTN's article is explicit that the Confederacy and the elected band government are two distinct bodies with two distinct, opposed published positions — and quotes both, which this overlay carries exactly as printed, unadjudicated per S1:

"The Confederacy is distinct from Six Nations elected council, an Indian Act government led by Chief Mark Hill. In an emailed statement, elected council spokesperson Katie Montour said band members 'have a free choice as to whether or not they decide to vote in any election.' 'Having a polling location in our community is a way of respecting the right of our community members to exercise that free choice if they wish,' Montour said." — same APTN article — Six Nations of the Grand River Elected Council (Indigenous-authored, a named body's own spokesperson statement), distinct from the Confederacy Chiefs Council quoted above.

This is not a settled internal debate this overlay is positioned to adjudicate, and it does not try to. Two named Haudenosaunee/Six Nations bodies — the traditional Confederacy Chiefs Council and the Indian-Act-created elected band council — hold and have publicly acted on opposite positions about whether participating in Canada's electoral system is compatible with Haudenosaunee sovereignty. Both are carried here exactly as their own spokespeople state them.

A statistic APTN itself flags as government data, not an Indigenous-published finding (carried here per this library's Indigenous-sources framework's non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) rule — admissible as a contextual fact, never as a position): "According to data from Elections Canada, 51.8 per cent of Indigenous people nationwide voted in the 2019 federal election, compared to overall voter turnout of 67 per cent." ⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) — a Statistics Canada/Elections Canada figure reported by an Indigenous outlet, not itself an Indigenous organization's stated finding or position.

Honest scope limit: this is Six Nations territory, not a catalogued Toronto position

Stated plainly, not blurred: every source in the section above concerns Haudenosaunee/Six Nations territory and federal elections, not a Toronto municipal election specifically, and not a Toronto-headquartered Indigenous organization's own account of participating or declining to participate in a City of Toronto election. This review grep-checked an earlier internal research file for "municipal election," "mayoral election," "Toronto election," and "city council election" in combination with any Indigenous-organization row: no matches. No catalogued source states a Toronto-specific sovereignty-based non-participation position, and none states a Toronto-specific voter-turnout or ballot-access finding for a City of Toronto election. This is a genuine catalogue gap, consistent with the dispatch's own expectation that Toronto-specific Indigenous voter-experience material may be thin — carried here as the finding it is, not papered over with the Six Nations material above standing in for it.

Friendship Centres' own account of their internal democratic structure

Distinct from the settler-electoral question above, the Ontario Federation of Indigenous Friendship Centres (OFIFC) publishes its own description of how the Friendship Centre Movement — "the country's most significant urban Indigenous service delivery infrastructure," per OFIFC's own framing — governs itself, a form of urban Indigenous civic participation that is neither settler electoral participation nor traditional Confederacy governance:

"Friendship Centres are governed by a volunteer Board of Directors consisting of elected members. They are membership driven organizations in urban communities that serve all urban Indigenous people, regardless of status." — Ontario Federation of Indigenous Friendship Centres (Indigenous-authored), Friendship Centres, https://ofifc.org/friendship-centres/

"Have a mandate to serve all urban Indigenous people regardless of status (Status, Non-status, Métis, Inuit)." — same source, membership requirement for Friendship Centre status.

Toronto's own Friendship Centre — the Native Canadian Centre of Toronto (NCCT, est. 1962, already catalogued at this library's Indigenous-sources catalogue) — sits inside this same OFIFC network and structural description; this review found no NCCT-specific page elaborating its own board-election process beyond what OFIFC's general Friendship Centre description states, so NCCT's own account is not separately quoted here.

What the governance overlay already carries — pointed to, not repeated

Per this lane's pointer discipline and the boundary stated at the top of this file, an earlier internal research file already carries: the OFIFC/AMO Declaration of Mutual Commitment and Friendship and Toronto's absence from its 10 signatory pairs; Yellowhead Institute's Tomiak, Horn, and Wale on municipal advisory bodies that "lack the type of political power that reflects the nationhood of Indigenous peoples" and are "often framed and interpreted as 'special interest' groups"; the TICAB-versus-AAAC advisory/binding contrast; and the EX10.16→EX14.1 TRC-Calls record. Readers looking for Toronto's municipal consultation architecture, duty-to-consult mechanics, or advisory-body structure should start there. This file's own contribution is narrower and different in kind: not who holds decision power, but whether and how Indigenous people and organizations engage with the electoral/civic processes those structures sit inside.

Kuokkanen & Maddison — re-attempted, still inaccessible

The peer-reviewed article the audit names as directly on point (this library's Indigenous-sources catalogue, co-produced — Sámi scholar Rauna Kuokkanen co-writing with a non-Indigenous settler-colonial-studies scholar, arguing Indigenous self-determination "reclaims and pluralizes democracy rather than threatening it") was re-fetched live this review at its publisher URL (tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2201473X.2025.2495459) and returned an empty page, consistent with the 2026-07-20 and 2026-08-17 (E1) findings. No quotable text obtained; not cited from an unverified secondary description.

A document found and deliberately not used

This session located two further Haudenosaunee Confederacy documents concerning Toronto directly — a September 2025 letter from HCCC to Mayor Olivia Chow, and a September 2025 exchange of letters between HCCC and the Six Nations of the Grand River Elected Council — via the Confederacy's own website. Both were downloaded and OCR'd (scanned PDFs, no text layer) to check their relevance. Neither is used in this overlay. The Mayor Chow letter concerns a burial-site investigation and ancestral remains — content that one of this library's internal records (S4) routes to the future Indigenous-governed review seat, never resolved internally, and that also falls inside the sealed governance overlay's duty-to-consult territory, not this file's. The HCCC/Elected-Council exchange concerns an active, specific 2025 dispute over which body legitimately represents Six Nations, including litigation and named-individual allegations from only one side of the exchange (this review obtained HCCC's letters but not a full SNGREC rebuttal) — using it here risked amplifying an unresolved, one-sided account of a live inter-governmental conflict, which S1's "never speak for" and this project's identity-firewall caution against. The APTN-sourced, calmer, two-sided 2021 polling-station account above establishes the same underlying elected-versus-traditional-governance tension on cleaner, fully-verified, two-sided sourcing, so it was preferred and the 2025 letters were set aside.

Distinctness, kept exact

  • TASSC — Toronto Aboriginal Support Services Council; a Toronto-based coalition of 24 Indigenous-led member agencies; publisher of the Aboriginal Civic Literacy Initiative.
  • Haudenosaunee Confederacy Chiefs Council (HCCC) — the traditional government of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, based on the Great Law of Peace; publisher of the 2021 election-removal statement.
  • Six Nations of the Grand River Elected Council — an Indian-Act-created band government, distinct from HCCC; its spokesperson stated a "free choice" position on voting, the opposite framing from HCCC's.
  • OFIFC — the Ontario Federation of Indigenous Friendship Centres; a provincial federation, not itself a Friendship Centre.
  • NCCT — the Native Canadian Centre of Toronto; one member Friendship Centre within the OFIFC network, Toronto's own.
  • APTN — Aboriginal Peoples Television Network's news division; Indigenous-owned national media, the collection vehicle for both Six Nations bodies' quoted statements above, not itself a party to either position.

None of the above are interchangeable, and none is blended into a single "Indigenous position on voting."

Honest gaps this overlay carries rather than closes

  1. No catalogued Toronto-municipal-specific source states a position on participating in a City of Toronto election, a TICAB/AAAC appointment process, or any other municipal (as opposed to federal) civic mechanism — see "Honest scope limit" above. Grep-checked this review; zero matches.
  2. No catalogued Indigenous-published source addresses participatory budgeting — not Toronto's 2015–17 pilot, not Toronto Community Housing's tenant PB program, not any Indigenous housing provider's (Wigwamen's, Na-Me-Res's) experience of either. This is why this overlay lands on citizens-assemblies-sortition alone rather than also building a participatory-budgeting- outcomes companion this review.
  3. TASSC's ACLI program is dated (March 2014) with no evidence of renewal found this review — carried as TASSC's standing publication, not as a current program claim.
  4. The Kuokkanen & Maddison article remains inaccessible on a third fetch attempt across two passes and two lanes — flagged, not guessed at.
  5. This overlay does not, and by design cannot, state which Six Nations body — HCCC or the Elected Council — "actually" represents Haudenosaunee political authority. That is a live, named, unresolved dispute between two Indigenous bodies; this overlay carries both stated positions on the narrower voting question and stops there.
  6. Freshness is unverifiable from the repo alone beyond this review's live re-fetches — anything published on this subject since 2026-08-17 is invisible here.

CARE check (this library's Indigenous-sources framework, applied to this addition)

  • Collective benefit: surfaces a genuine, unresolved disagreement among Toronto-region Indigenous organizations about settler-electoral participation that this movement's own civic-participation framing would otherwise never encounter, plus a concrete example (TASSC's ACLI) of Indigenous-led civic-literacy programming a reader could otherwise miss entirely.
  • Authority to control: every claim above is a direct, attributed pointer to a named organization's own published statement, or (for the APTN-carried quotes) a named body's own statement as directly quoted by an Indigenous-owned outlet; the one non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people) figure (Elections Canada turnout data) is flagged inline and used only as a contextual fact, never as a position.
  • Responsibility: no relationship exists between this project and TASSC, HCCC, Six Nations of the Grand River Elected Council, OFIFC, NCCT, or APTN — the honest-limitation disclosure in an earlier internal research file §7 applies in full.
  • Ethics: the two opposed Six Nations positions are carried side by side, attributed by name, with neither smoothed into the other nor silently preferred — including the position (HCCC's) that runs directly counter to this movement's own civic-participation orientation, per S1 and the dispatch's explicit instruction not to enlist Indigenous voices for that agenda.
