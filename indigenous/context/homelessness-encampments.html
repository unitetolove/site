Indigenous context: Homelessness Encampments

No Indigenous person or organization has reviewed, endorsed, or participated in this work, and this page says so.

What the general finding misses

Checked this review by word-boundary scan: the two carried-forward documents in encampment-clearance-policy-costs — Encampment clearance: policy, votes, and costs in Toronto and Encampment clearances: past litigation and the City's own response infrastructure — contain zero occurrences of Indigenous, First Nations, Métis, Inuit or Aboriginal. So does an earlier synthesis document, this library's encampment-cost research. Between them those documents carry the Ombudsman's "significant unfairness" finding and 23 recommendations, Council's November 13 2025 three-shelter-offer enforcement motion (MM34.4, 16-3, with a 50-metre buffer and 24-hour notice), a $2M itemized 2021 clearance, four non-comparable encampment counts, and a still-undecided lawsuit by five plaintiffs over the July 2021 Lamport Stadium clearance — and not one Indigenous-authored source anywhere in them, on a subject where Indigenous organizations have published a great deal.

homelessness-legal-rights-frameworks (an earlier synthesis document) is the one C2-adjacent document that already noticed this: its own open-items list flags "the Yellowhead Institute's Toronto Indigenous outdoor-homelessness statistic (3% of population, 31% of outdoor homeless)" as an integration candidate "not yet added." This overlay lands the Yellowhead brief that figure came from, in its authors' own words.

The Yellowhead Institute brief on Bill 6 — an Indigenous-authored analysis of encampment enforcement

Yellowhead Institute (Indigenous-led, Faculty of Arts, Toronto Metropolitan University) published a brief specifically on Ontario's Safer Municipalities Act (Bill 6) and encampment removal, by three named Indigenous authors — Brianna Olson Pitawanakwat (Anishinaabekwe, Wiikwemkoong Unceded First Nation), Mskwaasin Agnew (Dene, Salt River First Nation) and Kelsi Balaban (Métis Nation of Alberta):

"In the fatal absence of effective, research-backed government initiatives, people who use drugs create their own mutual aid solutions, such as encampments, to keep each other safe. But the passage of Bill 6 threatens these community-based, life-saving initiatives, and further endangers those navigating the interlocking realities of Canada's toxic drug supply and houselessness."

"Bill 6 effectively dismantles pre-existing due process around encampment removal, including the city's previous notification process, which prepared folks for the violence of displacement."

"High rates of Indigenous outdoor houselessness is a direct result of generations of Indigenous displacement enabled through the process of settler colonialism. In Toronto, Indigenous people make up 3 percent of the overall population, but represent 31 percent of people experiencing houselessness outdoors."

"Indeed, many of these same people on the streets are language speakers, ceremonial knowledge holders, and Elders who are consistently refused dignity and support."

— Brianna Olson Pitawanakwat, Mskwaasin Agnew and Kelsi Balaban, Ontario's "Safer Municipalities Act" (Bill 6) and the Criminalization of Indigenous People in the City, Yellowhead Institute, September 25 2025, https://yellowheadinstitute.org/2025/ontarios-safer-municipalities-act-bill-6-and-the-criminalization-of-indigenous-people-in-the-city/ (Indigenous-authored). The same brief reports research by the Centre for Drug Policy and Evaluation, made available to Toronto Indigenous Harm Reduction, finding that "Indigenous participants had 98% increased prevalence of violent encounters with police compared to white participants" — cited here as the brief's own reporting of that research, not as an independent finding of this library.

Toronto Indigenous Harm Reduction, in its own words, on the 2021 clearances

TIHR is a grassroots, entirely queer and Two-Spirit Indigenous collective working with unhoused Indigenous people in Toronto since April 2020 — a street-level organization, structurally distinct from both TICAB (a governance body) and TASSC (an umbrella research and coordinating council). Its own statement, published five days before the April 2021 park evictions:

"It was also medicine to strengthen people against the coming displacement. On April 6, mass evictions are set to unfold against encampment residents at Moss Park, Scadding Court, Trinity Bellwoods, and other parks throughout the city — part of a new campaign of displacement."

"As the third wave of COVID-19 emerges, Indigenous folks who find themselves unhoused are now being threatened with violence to clear encampments, with no promise of safe, permanent housing."

"But as we said at a recent rally in support of encampment residents, the situation that is happening here will be the criminalization of Indigenous place-making and care. And everyone's going to watch it."

"The state is going to criminalize people from not having homes. And the reason we come out here to do what we do — we drum and we sing and we practice our cultures— is because many many of the people in these camps are Indigenous. They speak their languages, they know their songs, they are brilliant knowledge holders. Many are elders themselves."

— Toronto Indigenous Harm Reduction, Toronto Indigenous Harm Reduction in Solidarity with Indigenous Encampment Residents, Yellowhead Institute, April 1 2021, https://yellowheadinstitute.org/2021/toronto-indigenous-harm-reduction-in-solidarity-with-indigenous-encampment-residents/ (Indigenous-authored). The statement describes raising a tipi on March 27 2021: "While it was up, it created a space of ceremony, healing, and community at Allen Gardens Park, a gathering place for many houseless Indigenous people in the city." (TIHR's spelling "Allen Gardens" is preserved as published; City documents spell it "Allan Gardens." Punctuation irregularities in the fourth quotation — the unspaced em dash after "cultures" — are also as published.)

On its own site, TIHR states its standing position rather than a dated event:

"TIHR has been on the frontlines of encampment support and the devastating overdose crisis that is currently taking place across Turtle Island."

"Toronto has much less shelter and housing space than is needed, despite it's enormous wealth, leaving thousands of people outside routinely, especially those Indigenous people with disabilities, mental health, trauma and those who use substances."

— Toronto Indigenous Harm Reduction, "About," https://www.torontoindigenoushr.com/ (Indigenous-authored; verified live 2026-08-17. The irregularity "despite it's" is preserved as published.)

TICAB and Toronto Shelter and Support Services, on encampment response

Toronto's Indigenous Community Advisory Board holds final decision-making authority over Coordinated Access policy — documented in depth in `that page's own internal recordsthis issue's own promoted research document and not re-derived here. What an earlier synthesis document records on encampments specifically is a single line from the 2023 Meeting in the Middle report; the 2024 report is more detailed, and is the only catalogued source in which a Toronto Indigenous governance body's positions on encampment response appear at all:

"TSSS and TICAB discussed how they can collaboratively strengthen best practices to support Indigenous clients living in encampments."

"TICAB members also highlighted the collaborative efforts involved in the co-creation and implementation of the Interdivisional Protocol for Encampments (IDP), which has acted as a guide for the City's approach to encampment response. However, TICAB members also pointed out the challenges in finding support pathways for Indigenous clients, particularly due to encampment concerns."

"One TICAB member acknowledged the community working group, which was established to advise on approaches to supporting people living in the Allan Gardens encampment. Both TSSS and TICAB acknowledged the success of the approach with 98 individuals referred to housing."

"TICAB and TSSS noted a positive shift in how sacred fires are being addressed by the City as separate from encampment response."

Two of the report's stated Opportunities for Collaboration, reproduced verbatim:

Ensure investments focused on encampments are supporting Indigenous clients experiencing unsheltered homelessness.

Support Indigenous partners on federal encampment funding implementation.

— Meeting in the Middle: Sixth Annual Gathering Summary Report 2024, "Commitment 4: Meaningful Engagement" and "Opportunities for Collaboration", https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/887b-MITM-Five-Year-Annual-Gathering-Summary-Report-2024AODA.pdf (co-produced — authored and published by Toronto Shelter and Support Services in partnership with TICAB). Two fidelity flags, not smoothed over: every TICAB position above reaches the page as the City's indirect summary of what TICAB members said, not as TICAB's own direct quotation; and individual speakers are identified only as "One TICAB member." Both are recorded here rather than upgraded. The prior year's report lists, under "Commitment 3: Capacity Building," the action "Co-develop Indigenous community outreach and cultural needs assessments at encampments" — Meeting in the Middle Fifth Annual Gathering Summary Report 2023, https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/98a4-MITM-Five-Year-Annual-Gathering-Summary-Report-2023AODA.pdf (co-produced, same note).

OFIFC, on how municipalities have responded

The Ontario Federation of Indigenous Friendship Centres — which represents 29 Friendship Centres, many of them running the frontline homelessness outreach the City's encampment response depends on — stated a position on municipal encampment conduct in a written submission to a House of Commons standing committee:

"When it comes to homelessness programming, outreach, encampment supports, and wraparound services, Friendship Centres have reported approaches taken by municipalities that range from inappropriate and insufficient to significant human rights violations."

"Instances of Indigenous people experiencing homelessness who have been denied shelter, refused service, and left without supports have increased, as reported by Friendship Centres."

"Across the Friendship Centres, resources allocated for homelessness supports do not line up with the data and the results have been lethal."

— OFIFC, Written Submission to the Standing Committee on Indigenous and Northern Affairs on the Effects of the Housing Shortage on Indigenous Peoples Across Canada, March 25 2022, https://www.ourcommons.ca/Content/Committee/441/INAN/Brief/BR11678666/br-external/OntarioFederationOfIndigenousFriendshipCentres-Updated-e.pdf (Indigenous-authored — OFIFC-authored; published by the House of Commons, which is why it is reachable when ofifc.org is not). (The first quotation spans a page break with an intervening footnote block; both page boundaries were inspected directly to confirm the sentence is continuous in the document's own reading order — see the close record.) The submission is Ontario-wide and pre-dates Bill 6; it is not a statement about Toronto's 2025 enforcement motion, and is not presented as one.

An encampment death, in the words of the networks who spoke to it

The estate already carries the fact of a January 2026 encampment death — an earlier synthesis document records, from the Canadian Human Rights Commission, that "a 38-year-old mother of three from Wiikwemkoong First Nation died in a tent fire in Sudbury in January 2026," and flags it as a death "this library does not currently document." That source is ⚠️ non-Indigenous-authored (about Indigenous people). Two Indigenous-led networks published their own statement about her, six days later, jointly with a third network:

"Amanda Nahwegahbow was a mother of three, a beloved daughter, parent, and member of the community whose life was cut tragically short at the Energy Court tent community in Sudbury, Ontario on January 12. The 38-year-old Wiikwemkoong woman was surviving in an encampment simply because her basic needs for safe, stable housing were unmet—a situation no one in our community should ever have to face."

"This loss was not inevitable. It was a result of a systemic failure to realize the human right to housing, which guarantees that every person has access to safe, adequate, affordable, and culturally appropriate shelter. When people are forced to pitch a tent, because there are no other viable housing options, we set the stage for preventable tragedies."

"Amanda was not an anonymous statistic, she was our neighbour, a mother, and someone who deserved dignity, care, and support."

"No one should have to die because they had nowhere safe to lay their head. Homelessness leaves people vulnerable to a death that is completely avoidable."

— Her Life Mattered: A Preventable Death in Sudbury Demands Housing Justice, January 19 2026, signed by Denise Baldwin (Co-Founder and Network Director, Indigenous Harm Reduction Network), Marie McGregor Pitawanakwat (Chair, National Indigenous Women's Housing Network; Co-Chair, Women's National Housing & Homelessness Network) and Stefania Seccia (Executive Director, Advocacy & Public Affairs, WNHHN), https://womenshomelessness.ca/sudburystatement/ (co-produced — co-signed by two Indigenous-led networks and one network that is not Indigenous-specific, and hosted on the third's site; the Indigenous signatories' authorship is on the face of the document). This is Sudbury, not Toronto, and it is carried here because it is the only Indigenous-published statement on an encampment death found this review — not as a stand-in for anything Toronto's own Indigenous organizations have or have not said. Amanda Nahwegahbow's name appears here only because her community's own networks published it in her honour; nothing is added to their words.

Who counts encampment residents, and on whose data

Every Indigenous-published Toronto encampment figure found this review rests on the City's own Street Needs Assessment rather than on Indigenous-governed data collection:

  • Native Child and Family Services of Toronto, in a study it published: "The 2021 Street Need Assessment also surveyed encampment residents between March 3 and March 21, 2021 and found 27% of respondents identifying as Indigenous." — "I Want To Be Able To Smudge", March 31 2023, https://nativechild.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Culturally-Responsive-Study-to-Address-Aboriginal-Homelessness-in-Toronto_FINAL.pdf (co-produced — NCFST / Dixon Hall / Catherine Donnelly Foundation partnership, published by NCFST; its author, NCFST Senior Analyst Dr. Judith Mintz, states her own positionality in the report as a non-Indigenous researcher).
  • Ontario Aboriginal Housing Services, province-wide, lists among what the housing crisis has meant for urban Indigenous communities: "Overrepresentation within encampments for those experiencing homelessness across the province." — 2025 OUR Framework, "The Current Context," https://www.ontarioaboriginalhousing.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/OAHS_OUR-Framework.pdf (Indigenous-authored).
  • TASSC, from its own community survey rather than a City count: "13% of those surveyed do not have a fixed address (i.e., live in shelters, are staying with friends most nights, live in an encampment/outdoor dwelling, or are not currently housed)." — To Higher Ground, ch. 2, October 2024, https://tassc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/To-Higher-Ground-TARP-10-Year-Legacy-Review-Indigenous-Prosperity-Action-Plan-Oct-2024.pdf (Indigenous-authored).

Na-Me-Res runs the Indigenous-specific outreach that meets people where the counts find them: "This service provides indigenous-specific outreach services using a mobile approach with support workers and Elders. They engage with male and female Indigenous homeless clients to secure housing, develop a crisis plan, access services, and foster a greater sense of cultural awareness." https://www.nameres.org/programs/ (Indigenous-authored).

Where these published positions do not agree — carried, not reconciled

Three catalogued Indigenous voices address encampment response and do not say the same thing. Per one of this library's internal records §E2 they are listed as three named voices, never blended into one "Indigenous position on encampments," and this overlay does not adjudicate between them:

  1. TICAB, through the co-produced Meeting in the Middle reports (2023, 2024), is recorded as co-creating the Interdivisional Protocol for Encampments, collaborating with TSSS on best practices for Indigenous clients living in encampments, acknowledging "the success of the approach" at Allan Gardens, and asking that encampment investments and federal encampment funding reach Indigenous clients.
  2. TIHR (2021) and the Yellowhead authors (2025), both Indigenous-authored, describe encampments as mutual-aid and place-making that clearance and enforcement destroy, and describe removal as displacement and criminalization.
  3. OFIFC (2022, Indigenous-authored) describes municipal approaches to encampment supports as ranging "from inappropriate and insufficient to significant human rights violations."

These are different bodies, addressing different governments, in different years, at different scopes — which is itself part of why they have not been reconciled by anyone, and they are not reconciled here. Treating any one of them as the Indigenous position would be exactly what §E2 forbids.

Honest gaps this overlay carries rather than closes

  1. No catalogued Indigenous-published response to Toronto's own current enforcement policy was found — nothing from any Indigenous organization on Council's November 13 2025 three-shelter-offer motion (MM34.4), the 50-metre buffer, the 24-hour notice period, or the Ombudsman's "significant unfairness" finding and 23 recommendations. Searched this review, on each organization's own site search for "encampment": Na-Me-Res returns We could not find any results for your search, TASSC 0 results found for: encampment, Nishnawbe Aski Nation We found 0 results containing "encampment", Toronto Council Fire It seems we can't find what you're looking for; Chiefs of Ontario returns its standard no-match message followed by unrelated recent posts, i.e. no on-point item. A statement about this repo's shelf and about these five searches, never about Indigenous organizations' priorities.
  2. No Indigenous-published statement on the Lamport Stadium litigation — the estate's litigation document names five plaintiffs and an undecided claim over the July 2021 clearance; no Indigenous-authored analysis of that case was found.
  3. No Indigenous-published statement on a Toronto encampment death was found this review. The one Indigenous-published encampment-death statement located is about Sudbury (above). Toronto's own homelessness mortality data (an earlier synthesis document) is City-published throughout.
  4. Every Indigenous-published Toronto encampment number rests on City data. No Indigenous-governed encampment enumeration for Toronto exists in this catalog — a data-sovereignty gap that belongs beside the OCAP®/FNIGC material in the data-privacy-municipal-info-governance overlay, not resolved here.
  5. TIHR's dated encampment statement is from 2021; nothing more recent from TIHR beyond its standing site text was located this review, and no TIHR deputation record was retrieved (the City's agenda-item endpoint returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch this review).
  6. ofifc.org, homelesshub.ca and tiontario.ca are bot-blocked to direct fetch. No curl-around was attempted. The OFIFC material above comes from a genuinely different primary artifact — OFIFC's own submission as published by the House of Commons — and the Reaching Home TICAB quotations remain pointed to via an earlier synthesis document and an earlier internal research file, not re-derived.
  7. Freshness is unverifiable from the repo alone — this library's Indigenous-sources catalogue carries no per-row date column.

CARE check (this library's Indigenous-sources framework, applied to this addition)

  • Collective benefit: puts Indigenous-authored analysis of encampment enforcement, and Indigenous organizations' own accounts of clearance and of an encampment death, beside a set of estate documents on encampments that currently contain no Indigenous source at all.
  • Authority to control: every position, grievance and ask above is the named source's own published wording; the TICAB material is flagged as the City's indirect summary and as speaker-anonymized; the Yellowhead police-contact statistic is flagged as the brief's own reporting of third-party research.
  • Responsibility: no relationship exists between this project and Yellowhead Institute, Toronto Indigenous Harm Reduction, TICAB, OFIFC, the Indigenous Harm Reduction Network, the National Indigenous Women's Housing Network, NCFST, OAHS, TASSC or Na-Me-Res — the honest-limitation disclosure in an earlier internal research file §7 applies in full.
  • Ethics: no organization above is presented as endorsing another's position, this page, or any City policy. The one named death is reported only in the words of the networks who published it, with nothing added; that passage is flagged as protocol-sensitive and routed upward in the checklist below rather than resolved inside this lane.
