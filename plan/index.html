One year, all-out

What could Toronto do in one year, all-out? This page is the answer, told honestly: a scenario of what the year could hold — labeled a scenario, start and finish — the calendar the season is actually running on, the five goals with their numbers, every one labeled a target and never claimed as a count, and the ladder any organization can climb at its own pace, up to the covenant framework. Nothing here is a promise about what will happen. It is a working answer to what could — written to be checked, argued with, and made yours.

The five goals — targets, labeled targets

Everything below is measured in aggregate only — never a roster, never a tracked individual. And every number in this section is a target: an invitation, not a claim. Where a real counter reports a real number, that number is what publishes — if the counter says forty, the page says forty.

  • G1 · The conversations — 10,000 in three months.

    Rooms, kits, circles, online threads. A conversation counts only when it returns a harvest — a vision, a complaint, or a nomination — so no gathering is ever only an audience. Not 10,000 new events: the city already holds thousands of gatherings weekly, and the engine is conversion — kits everywhere, the counter visible, the harvest just one QR code away.

  • G2 · One Big Conversation — the city-scale synthesis moment.

    Opinion mapping over the whole harvest uses proven open tools, already used by Taiwan, Barcelona, and hundreds of governments — independent voices first, deliberation second. The result is the State of the Conversation: what Toronto said it wants, in its own words, weighted by how many said it. How the week actually works.

  • G3 · The plan — collectively agreed, receipts attached.

    A plan for the city’s future worked on by tens of thousands — signers, submitters, rooms, mappers, counted in aggregate only — and discussed by a million or more, two million the stretch. Reach publishes as an invitation (“help us get to…”), never as a claim.

  • G4 · The candidates — adoption, never endorsement.

    Every candidate in every race receives the people’s plan identically, with one equal question: will you commit to implementing it — and which parts? Adoption is public, candidate by candidate, in their own words — never a ranking, never an endorsement. The suite of candidates forms itself: whoever adopts, is the suite.

  • G5 · The coalition — a thousand-plus organizations, one year.

    Organizations agreeing to sprint the next year together, climbing one rung at a time — pin, host, quest, covenant — each rung one ask with one receipt, each rung’s count published in aggregate.

The calendar the season runs on

These are the season’s working dates. Two of them belong to the city — nominations close and voting day are the election calendar’s own. The rest are ours, set and published in advance so anyone can hold us to them.

  • August 21 — nominations close at 2 p.m. The city knows who is asking to lead it; every registrant in every race receives the same welcome package, identically, forever.
  • First week of September — ignition. Schools, campuses, congregations, leagues, and workplaces all restart in the same five days; the invitation lands everywhere at once.
  • September into October — the six weeks of wins: conversations at scale, one city-visible proof each week, the counter public.
  • October 7–14 — the One Big Conversation window: the harvest goes through opinion mapping, the whole city invited.
  • October 15–18 — synthesis, in the open.
  • Monday, October 19 — the people’s plan publishes, carrying the adoption question to every candidate on every ballot, identically.
  • Friday, October 23 — candidate answers close.
  • Saturday, October 24 — the adoption record publishes: candidate by candidate, in their own words, beside every silence, never ranked.
  • Monday, October 26 — voting day. Not the starting gun, not the finish line: the mid-sprint scoreboard.
  • November — the new council is sworn in; the coalition signs the covenant its own organizations finished writing (the framework is on the table now); the year’s real work begins.
  • September to September — the sprint year runs school-year-shaped. The 10,000 conversations complete across the full year, not by election day.

The year, told month by month

A scenario What follows is the year’s story as one honest telling — sealed, fact-checked line by line, and labeled exactly what it is: a scenario, not a prediction. Every number in it is an illustration or a receipted public fact; every ingredient in it is real.

What could Toronto do in one year, all-out?

Here is one honest answer. Not a prediction — a scenario: a picture of a year that breaks no law of physics, requires no new invention, and waits on no permission. Not even on an election. Why would a city wait for a ballot to start taking care of itself?

Start with what is actually true. Toronto’s problem was never knowledge — the solutions to nearly every problem on our map already exist somewhere on earth, cataloged, graded, with receipts. It was never people — this city holds the world: every profession, every language, every governance system and health system and school system on the planet, resident, a transit ride away. It was never money — the capital that could fund any solution the city chooses is managed within walking distance of the problems themselves. And it was never will — our own city council declared the emergencies years ago, on the record, in recorded votes: climate, twenty-five to nothing; homelessness, twenty-four to one.

The missing ingredient was always coordination — thousands of organizations and a million people pointing at the same goals at the same time. And coordination is not a resource you wait for. It is a choice a city makes. This is the story of the year Toronto makes it — told month by month, because that is how a year actually gets made: a crowd first, then the crowd’s decisions, then the crowd’s work.

This is the work for us to do, we the people, not some amorphous they or them. Join us.

AUGUST 2026 — the setup

Great years are won before they start, and this one starts quietly, in August, with homework.

The library opens its shelves: hundreds of researched issue briefs, the peer-city proofs — how Vienna plans, how Barcelona reclaimed its streets with published results, how Helsinki lets residents budget, how Wales wrote future generations into law. The futures corpus connects it all to the decade ahead, every claim carrying its receipt. A citizen question bank, sourced and sealed. Day-one packages: worked drafts of what a new council member could actually table in their first weeks — because no candidate should have to build a research department from a kitchen table, and no voter should have to guess. All of it free, public, serving every side identically. Nobody will be able to say we weren’t ready. We checked.

The field sets: nominations close August 21 at 2 p.m. In the days before the deadline, the quiet question gets asked out loud — why do so many of this city’s extraordinary people never even consider running? — and the crowd’s own answer runs: recommend a leader you trust, and we’ll bring them the homework. Some of the recommended file. Most don’t. But every single registrant, in every race, incumbent or challenger, receives the same welcome package: the library, the question bank, the day-one scaffolding. Identical for all, no exceptions, forever. When the deadline passes, the city knows exactly who is asking to lead it — and for the first time, every one of them is holding the same homework.

And the door opens: one link, everywhere. Not ten asks — one. Sign up for the conversation. Thirty seconds, a name and a way to reach you, and a public counter the whole city can watch. Voters, yes — and equally the permanent resident who can’t vote, the student on a permit, the commuter from Brampton, the cousin abroad watching home. The ballot has a citizenship requirement; the conversation does not. Late August is festival season, and the festivals become the first harvest: visions collected at a hundred stages, the first organizations pinning themselves to the map, the first conversation kits — a dinner kit, a debate-in-a-box, an assembly-in-a-box — moving from print shop to trunk to hall. By Labour Day, the machine that September needs is standing: kits printed, rooms booked, facilitators briefed, counter live, and a city about to change rhythm all at once.

SEPTEMBER 2026 — the crowd assembles

The week of September 7 — ignition

The first week of September is the closest thing this city has to a collective deep breath: a couple of million routines change in the same five days. Schools and campuses come back — the year’s single biggest mobilization moment, and the sprint rides it — but so does everything else: congregations return from summer, leagues restart, workplaces refill, book clubs and run crews and union halls resume. Every network in the city is, for one week, simultaneously open. So that is the week the invitation lands everywhere at once — the one link on ten thousand posters and orientation tables and group chats, and a simple message behind it: you are needed. Not as an activist. As a neighbour with twenty minutes a week and something nobody else has: your read on this city.

The counter starts climbing in public. That number — Torontonians first, then the region, then the world’s welcome — is the proof the crowd exists, updated daily, and it is the only scoreboard that matters this month.

The week of September 14 — ten thousand rooms

The conversations begin at scale — not ten thousand new events, but the city’s existing gatherings, converted: the dinner that adds one question, the halaqa and the Bible study and the sangha night that take up the kits, the team that talks after practice, the union local, the seniors’ lunch, the classroom, the group chat. A conversation counts only when it returns a harvest — a vision, a complaint, or a nomination — so no room is ever only an audience. The arithmetic was always the point: a thousand organizations hosting ten conversations each is ten thousand conversations. The organizations pin the map; the counter splits into two public numbers — people signed, rooms held — and both climb.

The first city-visible proof lands that Saturday — a clean-up morning, a block-circle count, pick your instrument — because each of the six weeks to voting day will land one proof the crowd exists. The proofs are the drumbeat. The conversations are the song.

The week of September 21 — the tribes convene

The city’s strongest coordination assets have been organized for decades; nobody had asked them to point the same direction. This is the week the doors start opening — some this week, some in October, some never, and that is every community’s own right, because the oldest rule this movement has binds before any calendar does: nothing is convened in any community without an invitation from inside it. But where the invitation comes, it comes from everywhere at once: the gurdwaras and mosques and temples and churches and synagogues, the hometown associations and diaspora chambers, the student unions and faculty clubs, the nurses and teachers and tradespeople, the artists and the civic-tech circles, the mutual-aid networks. The rooms open with the oldest connection technologies there are: food, music, and working alongside. Kids welcome, nobody pays, ever.

And the coordination gets a spine: the coalition’s own emergency-operations rooms open — virtual and physical, the movement’s, not the City’s — not because anything new went wrong, but because our governments declared these emergencies years ago and an emergency deserves an emergency response: sense-making, decision-making, coordinated implementation. Ours is love, organized.

The week of September 28 — the questions open every race

Seven in ten of us stayed home last time. The races were landslides; barely half of the mayoral and council candidates have a findable platform; almost none carry a date. The crowd’s answer is not anger — it’s homework, deployed. The citizen question bank goes to every candidate in every race, identically: the city’s real questions, sourced from the conversations, published with every answer — and every silence — rendered candidate by candidate, never as a ranking. The two-horse story breaks, not by attacking anyone, but by making the crowd the story: the city is actually choosing this time. Candidates discover the strangest thing about this election: a crowd that shows up prepared, asks fair questions, and defends the dignity of every single person on every ballot. Some of them start answering. The answers are news. The news brings more signers. The counter climbs.

OCTOBER 2026 — the crowd decides

The week of October 5 — the harvest closes, and the standard rises

The ten-thousandth conversation — in this telling; remember, every number here is an illustration — is counted, and the harvest window closes: tens of thousands of visions, complaints, and nominations, gathered from every corner of the city, every one traceable, every one counted in aggregate — the city measuring itself without surveilling a single person. The synthesis work begins in the open.

Meanwhile the day-one standard does its work. Candidates who accept it publish exactly what they will table in their first weeks — motions, budget lines, staff directions — with the library’s worked packages standing beneath anyone who wants the floor raised. The commitments that matter most land now, while the weather is kind: winter is coming, our research priced what the homelessness crisis costs to maintain, and the city’s own auditor found people unmatched to shelter beds on nights beds sat empty. The asks are specific — warm rooms named now, not scrambled in January; every person known by name; every bed visible — because the crowd did its reading, and a crowd that has done its reading is very hard to refuse.

The week of October 12 — the One Big Conversation

Mid-October, the synthesis moment the whole season built toward: the harvest goes through opinion mapping — proven open tools used by Taiwan, Barcelona, and hundreds of governments; independent voices first, deliberation second, dissent recorded beside consensus — and out of it comes the State of the Conversation: here is what Toronto said it wants, in its own words, weighted by how many said it. Not a manifesto from a back room. A mirror, held up by ten thousand rooms.

The same week, the plan born from it goes to every candidate on every ballot with one identical question: will you commit to implementing the people’s plan — and which parts?

The week of October 19 — Answer Day

The answers come back and are published the same way everything here is published: candidate by candidate, in their own words, beside every silence, never ranked, never endorsed — a commons endorses no one and serves all identically. Whoever adopts, is the suite; nobody anoints anyone. An endorsement would be our opinion. An adoption record is their commitment — on the record, weighable at the ballot box, reviewable all term.

The city walks toward the booth knowing, for the first time in its modern history, exactly what its choices propose to do — and knowing something better: whatever the count says, the crowd on the other side of the ballot box is not going anywhere.

October 26 — the mid-sprint scoreboard

Voting day. And here is the thing to understand about it: it is not the starting gun and it is not the finish line — it is the mid-sprint scoreboard. The city walks into the booth six weeks into taking care of itself: a public plan, a signed-up crowd, rooms that harvested, proofs landed every week, commitments locked before the cold. Whoever wins inherits a city in motion, and a published record of what they committed to do about it. Last time, seven in ten stayed home and whoever showed up decided. This time, showing up was redefined: the conversation never had a citizenship requirement, and the crowd it built is now a public fact — counted, published, and still growing.

— Half the year told, so say it again plainly: this is a scenario, not a promise and not a poll. Nobody here was elected, and nothing here is imposed — everything is offered, and every decision in it belongs to the crowd and the council the city actually chooses. What follows is what the ingredients already on the table allow. —

NOVEMBER 2026 — the decisions meet the leaders

The counting continues past the count — the plan was never “a crowd by election day”; it was a city that talks to itself as a habit. But November is where the year is truly won or lost, because November is where crowd decisions meet the machinery.

Mid-month, the new council is sworn in. On every desk: the people’s plan, and beside it the adoption record — who committed to what, before the vote, in their own words. The first motions are already drafted, because the day-one packages exist: the crowd doesn’t arrive at the new term with demands, it arrives with paperwork — table-ready, costed, receipted. A councillor who wants to move fast has never had it easier — and either way, the record is public from the first day: what was tabled, what moved, what didn’t, and why.

In a borrowed hall, the coalition holds the year’s most important meeting. The organizations that hosted the fall’s conversations — the congregations and unions, the chambers and student bodies, the run crews and hackerspaces, hundreds strong and climbing toward a thousand — sign the covenant: same goals, same time, own names, shown work. This is G5, the quiet engine of everything: each organization names what it will carry through the year, publicly, and the coalition’s count publishes in aggregate, one more number the city watches itself grow. The six citywide quests are offered to the assembled coalition as instruments — proposed, never decreed; every organization adopts what fits its gifts — and the scoreboards that will carry the year go live under the coalition’s own names.

And the operating system stands up for the long run: the coalition’s own coordination rooms move from campaign rhythm to standing rhythm — sense-making (the commons and its gardeners), decision-making (the assemblies now being designed), implementation (the coalition’s working groups). The first fully self-convened rooms appear — conversations nobody staffed, run by people who were participants in September and are hosts by November. That metric was always the one that mattered.

DECEMBER 2026 — first motions, first frost

Early December is where years are made right — not in the spotlight, in the committee rooms, while the election’s adrenaline is still in the building.

The council’s first working weeks meet a crowd that shows up prepared: deputations drawn from the people’s plan, each carrying its research under its arm; the budget process opening with the plan already on the table, and the deputants making the argument out loud: what do people want? was answered in October, in writing — the question now is which line does it land on? The first day-one motions get tabled, and the record starts: tabled, passed, stalled, abandoned — receipts from day one, because accountability was never anger; it’s a filing system and a city that reads.

The winter commitments hold their schedule: warm rooms open on the dates named in October; the by-name push runs before deep cold — every person known, every bed visible — the auditor’s finding attacked as what it always was, a coordination failure in a city that has now spent three months proving it can coordinate. Block circles take their winter shape: the check-in knock, the shoveled steps, the holiday tables where kids are underfoot and nobody pays. The guides to everything already free in this city get their winter edition, translated by the volunteers who made translation a first-class civic act.

This is a working December, and everyone can feel it. The rest comes later, and it will be earned.

JANUARY 2027 — open books

The city opens its books, because that was the covenant’s price of entry: every sprint project publishes what it did and what it spent, to the dollar. Not a press release — a claims register. The skeptics are invited to audit and thanked when they find errors; a movement that can’t survive its fact-checkers doesn’t deserve to.

The first citizens’ assembly of the term is seated — ordinary Torontonians, drawn by lot, briefed from the same public shelf everyone reads. This is not an experiment; it’s a homecoming: Ireland handed an assembly its hardest constitutional question and the country followed; the field counts six hundred such processes worldwide; Toronto ran its own panel for four years — and then ran none. The capacity was never absent, only idle. The assembly takes up the plan’s hardest question, and the council answers in public, on the record, within a stated time — the pattern our research documented in Ostbelgien and Paris and Brussels, adapted home.

And budget season becomes the plan’s first true test: line by line, the people’s plan against the city’s money, with tens of thousands of co-authors watching their own words move — or not — and noting which.

FEBRUARY 2027 — the deep cold, held

The coldest month is the proudest. The warm rooms are full and working; the frontline workers are partners with a plan, not scapegoats with a crisis; the by-name board shrinks name by name — each one a person, housed. The block circles born in the fall are the city’s winter nervous system now: the eye on the neighbour, the tower floor that eats together. The assembly deliberates; the coalition’s working groups grind; the counter still climbs, slower, steadier. Nothing about February photographs well. Everything about it compounds. The city notices something it has never once seen in living memory: a February in which nobody stopped.

MARCH 2027 — the city learns itself

The commons becomes what it was always meant to be: not a website — a guild. The gardeners’ network takes shape in public: the fact-checkers keeping the claims register honest, the librarians keeping it findable, the translators keeping it universal, the elders paired with youth all winter watching their stories become the city’s own record. The classrooms and campuses that harvested futures in the fall run their own assemblies now; the apprenticeship isn’t civics class — it’s the real thing, practiced. Expert submissions stack up in the truth layer: the nurse’s honest read on the ER, the engineer’s on the intersection, the tenant’s on the building — the city’s professional knowledge, finally with somewhere public to land.

APRIL 2027 — the visible spring

The year’s work surfaces where everyone can see it. The Great Toronto Clean-Up runs at full scale — ravines, shorelines, laneways — powered by crews that have been practicing since September, not strangers meeting for the first time. The retrofit crews trained through the winter take their first buildings. Tool libraries have waiting lists, which is the best problem this city has ever had. And the deputations keep coming: spring is construction season, and the crowd’s paperwork is already in the pipeline.

MAY 2027 — paint season

The transit commitments adopted in October and moved through the winter hit the street. Our own research put the receipt on the table long ago: give a streetcar its own lane and its on-time rate roughly doubles on average — on the TTC’s own standard. Paint, posts, and priority — not tunnels, not generations: street-level fixes on a clock the City itself controls, the first corridors moving inside the year and the gains compounding for decades. The data showed the crisis was never generosity; it was scale — and a crowd that did its reading knows the difference between a budget problem and a courage problem. On the corridors the plan named, the difference gets painted onto the asphalt. Meanwhile the leagues fill the parks — ten thousand pickup games, nobody picked last — and the counter quietly passes another line nobody thought plausible in August.

JUNE 2027 — the festivals are the tables

Festival season — but this year the movement doesn’t set up tables at the festivals. The festivals ARE the tables, because their organizers signed the covenant in the fall and planned it that way. A hundred stages harvest the next round of visions; the second year’s question — what do we want now? — starts collecting its answers where the first year’s did: in public, at full volume, with music.

JULY 2027 — ribbons

High summer is for finishing and showing. The visible works get their ribbons: the benches, murals, shade and gardens permitted in winter and built in spring — artists paid where money exists and credited everywhere, because that was the rule from day one. Progress is the news now — not because anyone captured a newsroom, but because a city visibly fixing itself is a better story than a city arguing about whether it could.

AUGUST 2027 — the honest month

The scoreboards get their audits, and the sprint’s most important habit fires: what failed is named in public, with reasons, in the same typeface as what worked. Some commitments slipped. Some working groups stalled. Some numbers disappointed. The skeptics who spent the year fact-checking are thanked and asked to go harder. A movement that can’t publish its failures is just another brand; this one publishes them in August, when everyone’s watching the totals.

And the proof begins to travel. Diaspora summer: visitors from every homeland this city holds walk through a year of receipts, and some leave carrying the blueprints — free, forkable, translated. Solutions flow out; gratitude and improvements flow back. Designed in from the start; running at scale by August.

SEPTEMBER 2027 — the second ignition, which nobody has to light

One year after ignition, the city’s rhythm changes again — and this time nobody has to light anything. The kits are in the culture. The circles know their streets. The organizations run the civic tables without being asked, because last year’s participants are this year’s hosts. The scoreboards reset for year two on their own, in ten thousand rooms, under ten thousand names that were never ours.

Year two’s work isn’t inherited — it’s chosen. The coalition, thousands of organizations strong, collaborates on what the year holds next: keep what’s working, retire what finished, take up what the conversations kept asking for. The crowd decides. That was always the point of the practice.

OCTOBER 2027 — the reckoning

One year since the ballot. The adoption record — who committed to what, in their own words, before the vote — comes off the shelf, and beside it goes the year’s receipts: what was tabled, what passed, what stalled, what was quietly abandoned. Candidate by candidate, promise by promise, never a ranking — just the record, doing what records do.

The assemblies convene to read it. The second State of the Conversation is published: what the city said it wanted last year, what it got, what it wants now. Some office-holders have a very good week. Some have questions to answer in public, on the record — which is exactly the covenant they signed. Accountability, it turns out, doesn’t need anger. It needs a filing system and a city that reads.

NOVEMBER 2027 — packing the seeds

The export month. Everything the year proved gets packaged the way the blueprints were always meant to travel: versioned, translated, documented, forkable — the kits rewritten so a city that isn’t Toronto can run them, the frameworks with their Toronto-specific parts clearly marked swap this for your own. The diaspora networks that watched all year start writing home in earnest: come see it, then take it.

And the invitations go out — to the sister cities, the peer networks, the mayors and ministries and mutual-aid crews who spent the year asking “is that real?” A date is named in the new year. The world is invited to come and check the receipts in person.

DECEMBER 2027 — the rest

And then — nothing. On purpose.

December is the earned month: no scoreboards, no drops, no asks. The doctrine said it from the first page: rest is a legitimate rung nobody has to justify, burnout is the death that kills movements, and a fully-instrumented year needs its corner nobody manages. The gatherings don’t stop; they just stop counting. Holiday tables, warm halls, kids underfoot, nobody pays. The block circles do what they were always really for.

A movement that can rest without dissolving is the proof of regeneration. Anyone can sprint; the city that learned to breathe is the one worth copying.

JANUARY 2028 — the sporing

Rested, the city opens its doors the other way.

The visitors arrive — the delegations, the skeptics, the builders from every homeland this city holds and a hundred it doesn’t. They walk the receipts: the year of scoreboards, the open books, the published failures beside the published wins. And they leave carrying the cargo this whole thing was always building toward: the blueprints, free forever — the design grammar, the toolkits, the civic operating patterns a city of nearly three million just spent sixteen months testing on itself.

The problem-servicers become the cure-teachers — the promise kept. Solutions spread the way mycelium spreads: quietly, underground, through the diaspora bridges, every proof carried by someone who loves both ends of the journey. Toronto took care of its own backyard first — the credibility gate — and now the demonstration IS the export.

And the sporing has its own calendar, because the world already wrote it: a year of new years. January 1 is only the first door. Every few weeks, somewhere on Earth — and therefore somewhere in Toronto — a year turns: Lunar New Year across East and Southeast Asia; Nowruz at the spring equinox, from Tehran to Kabul to the Kurdish highlands; the April solar new years from Punjab to Tamil Nadu to Bangkok; the turnings the Islamic calendar carries through the seasons; Enkutatash and Rosh Hashanah in September; the ceremonies Indigenous nations keep on this land’s own clock — on their terms, at their invitation, always. Each turning becomes that region’s launch: the blueprints arriving as a new-year’s gift, carried by the Toronto community that actually celebrates it, timed to the moment their own calendar says begin again. Nobody is asked to adopt anyone else’s new year — the spore travels on the calendar of the people carrying it. Which is how you know it was never an export campaign at all: it’s the family, going home for the holidays, with something extraordinary to show.

The gardener who helped light it has spent sixteen months doing what was promised on day one: inspire, inform, connect, equip, guide, hand off — and now the last word, the one that was always a promise and never a flourish. What remains doesn’t need the gardener. That was the whole design.

The honest coda

Could it really go like this? Not exactly — nothing ever does. Some months will be swallowed by events nobody scheduled; some commitments will fail in public, and the publishing of that failure will be the system working. The scenario’s claim was never smoothness. And its authorship was never authority: nobody here was elected, nothing here is imposed, and every decision in this story belongs to the crowd and the council the city actually chooses.

The claim is this: every ingredient above already exists. The solutions are researched, cataloged, receipted. The organizations are real and counted. The facilitators are trained and resident. The methods are proven on three continents. The emergencies are declared — by our own governments, on the record. The calendar is set: nominations close in August, the city changes rhythm in September, the ballot falls in October, and the year belongs to whoever organizes it. The only missing ingredient was always coordination — and coordination is a choice. A city can make it in a single season. This one, for instance.

We could talk about it from a hundred lenses. Or we could take one collective deep breath, and push forward.

How to take part — the ladder

For a person, the doors are on the Now page — thirty seconds to a room of your own. For an organization, the ladder has four rungs. Each rung is one ask with one receipt; no rung requires any other; nobody is hurried.

  • PIN — put your organization on the city’s map.

    Presence, zero cost, your name public only because you put it there. The public org map is being built now; until it opens, say hello and you’ll be on it from day one.

  • HOST — hold one conversation in your own room.

    Under your own name, with a ready-made kit — a dinner, a debate-in-a-box, an assembly-in-a-box, and more. Free, printable, yours.

  • QUEST — adopt one citywide quest with a public scoreboard.

    Offered, never assigned. The quests are offered to the coalition in the fall; scoreboards publish under the coalition’s own names.

  • SPRINT COVENANT — the year, whole.

    One signable page: same goals, same time, own names, shown work. Read it, print it, sign it in your own room.
