Leaving Jail

Hard selection rule: an entry belongs here only if it serves someone who is in, leaving, or has left custody — or their family — because of that, and is free or explicitly labelled subsidized, in Toronto.

Release is a cliff, not a slope. Someone walking out of Toronto South Detention Centre this morning with no money, no ID and nowhere booked to sleep tonight needs a completely different page than someone six months out who is ready to apply for a record suspension. This guide is built around the first 72 hours first, then moves outward — housing beyond the first nights, income and ID, work, and the record. It also treats families of people still inside as a second, separate reader, with their own section, because until this wave the atlas had no entry for them at all.

Find where you are: - Being released today, or in the next few days → The first 72 hours, below. - Still in custody, waiting on bail → If you're not out yet. - Need somewhere to live beyond the first nights → Where to sleep beyond the first few nights. - Looking for income, ID, or a job → Money and ID, and Work with a record. - Ready to deal with a record → The record: suspension, its $50 fee, and free help applying. - You're the family of someone inside → If you're the family of someone inside.

The first 72 hours after release

Where to sleep tonight and what to eat aren't answered in this guide — they're answered better elsewhere, and this guide points rather than duplicates. For a shelter bed tonight, see Guide 01 In Crisis Now and Guide 07 Housing. For food today, see Guide 03 Food Today and Groceries. What belongs here is the support that exists because you're leaving custody — the discharge-day contact and the weeks of follow-up after it.

John Howard Society of Toronto — Reintegration Services Trailer (RST) A trailer parked outside the discharge doors of Toronto South Detention Centre, staffed for anyone walking out or picking up property after release: overdose-prevention information, harm-reduction supplies and naloxone, clothing, food and snacks, TTC tokens, phone/email/charging access, and warm referrals into JHS Toronto's other programs and citywide services. Peer support workers can go with you to appointments. johnhoward.on.ca/toronto/rst — 416-524-2816, 160 Horner Ave (Etobicoke), in the parking lot outside TSDC's discharge doors. Anyone released from, or picking up property at, Toronto South Detention Centre. No documents required. Free.

John Howard Society of Toronto — System Navigator Program (SNP) Intensive, one-on-one case management that starts before release where possible and continues through the weeks after it — then a planned "warm transition" to ongoing community services around the 4–6 week mark. Covers housing, ID replacement, income and money management, addictions and mental-health counselling, employment, food, cultural supports, legal help, life-skills programming, and accompaniment to appointments. This is the row that answers "ID after release" and "income in the first weeks" specifically because you're leaving custody — general-population ID and income help lives in Guide 02, but this program exists to do that work with you during reentry. johnhoward.on.ca/toronto/snp — 416-925-4386 ext. 775. People being released specifically from Toronto South Detention Centre. Does not serve people released from other facilities. No documents required to start. Free.

If you're not out yet — bail and support before trial

Toronto Bail Program — Criminal Division Community-based bail verification and supervision, funded by Ontario's Ministry of the Attorney General at no cost to the accused: verifies background information for the court, helps identify whether a surety is available, and — where the court orders it — supervises someone in the community (reporting, court-date reminders, referrals to addictions, mental-health and housing support) instead of holding them on remand. Covers the Ontario Court of Justice Toronto, the Toronto Regional Bail Centre, and the Oshawa and Newmarket courthouses. torontobailprogram.com/criminal-division — 416-314-3765. Anyone facing bail in these courts. Referral comes through your defence lawyer or duty counsel — not something you request directly yourself. Free.

Aboriginal Legal Services Free community legal clinic for Indigenous people in the GTA: courtworkers, Gladue report writing, referral to lawyers, victim-rights advocacy, and a Community Council restorative-justice program that can offer an alternative path through the justice system. Also handles landlord/tenant, social assistance, and child-protection representation. aboriginallegal.ca — 416-408-3967 or 416-408-4041, toll-free 1-844-633-2886, 211 Yonge St Suite 500. Legal Clinic intake (corrected this verification pass): Mon/Wed 10am–3pm in person or by phone; Fri 10am–1pm, phone only — not 3pm, as v0.1 stated. Two dedicated intake days not previously listed here: Tue 10am–3pm for Criminal Matters and Thu 10am–3pm for Child Protection Matters, both in person or by phone. Call 416-408-4041 to reach an intake worker. Community-specific — this door is for Indigenous people in the Greater Toronto Area. No documents required. Free.

Where to sleep beyond the first few nights

Halfway housing and post-release housing support split by who's eligible — read the eligibility line on each carefully before calling, since several of these are gated by gender or by which system (provincial or federal) you're leaving.

John Howard Society of Toronto — Homes for Good (H4G) Housing-search help and intensive follow-up support, specifically for people leaving provincial custody with nowhere to go: either (a) currently homeless and, in the past six months, spent 30 cumulative nights in a provincial institution, or (b) currently in a provincial institution for 30+ days, homeless for six months before entering it, and likely to be homeless on discharge. johnhoward.on.ca/toronto/h4g — homesforgood@jhst.ca. Men and male-identifying individuals only — not available to couples or to people with dependants living with them. High demand; intake runs through a waiting-list sign-up form, not same-day placement. Free.

John Howard Society of Toronto — Crossroads Program, Donald G. Evans Residence & Crossroads Day Reporting Centre Two co-located federal halfway houses (Crossroads Program and Donald G. Evans Residence) for men on supervised release from a federal institution, with individual counselling, a graduated-leave system, employment/vocational/educational programming, a financial program, and substance-use programming. A separate day-reporting service (Crossroads Day Reporting Centre) provides intensive case management in the community for higher-risk people on parole who aren't living in a residential facility. johnhoward.on.ca/toronto/reintegration-programs — DGER ext. 767, Crossroads Program ext. 766, CDRC ext. 765 (main line 416-925-4386). Men on federal supervised release. Placement is arranged through Correctional Service Canada or the Parole Board of Canada — not a door you can self-refer into. Subsidized (no resident fee schedule is published on the provider's own pages).

Salvation Army Correctional and Justice Services — Archibald Centre, Bunton Lodge & Toronto Harbour Light CRF Three federal halfway houses (Community Residential Facilities) in Toronto for men approved by the Parole Board of Canada for community supervision: the 18-bed Archibald Centre and 43-bed Bunton Lodge, and the 26-bed, addictions-focused Toronto Harbour Light CRF. Programming includes counselling, goal planning, a job-readiness Employment Readiness Program, housing assistance, a Victim Impact Program, and an Emotions Management and Violence Prevention Program. salvationarmyjustice.ca/residential-facilities — 416-964-6316, toll-free 1-888-834-8483. Men on federal parole. Application is only through Correctional Service Canada and Ontario federal institutions — not by self-referral. Subsidized (no fee schedule is published).

Elizabeth Fry Toronto — Phyllis Haslam Community Residential Program A supportive residential placement for women on parole from provincial or federal prison, with staff support to build a release plan covering education, employment and rebuilding family ties. efrytoronto.org/programs-services/housing-supports — toll-free 1-855-924-3708. Women on parole. Any woman in custody can ask to speak with an Elizabeth Fry Toronto worker to be assessed for the residence. Subsidized (no rent-geared-to-income structure is published — confirm directly).

Fred Victor — Bridging Housing & Community Stabilization Housing workers who bridge people experiencing homelessness — including a specific post- incarceration housing support stream — to affordable housing and landlords, then provide at least a full year of follow-up support to help the tenancy last. Program is funded by the Province of Ontario. fredvictor.org — Housing Access and Support Services — 416-364-8228 ext. 3361. Anyone experiencing homelessness; referral-required. Free.

Na-Me-Res — Programs Beyond Shelter Beyond emergency shelter, Na-Me-Res runs Native Inmate Liaison Officers (NILO) supporting incarcerated Indigenous men's access to sweat lodge ceremonies, healing circles, smudging and drumming; a year-long Housing Aftercare Program; Street Outreach & Support Services that helps Indigenous clients secure housing, develop a crisis plan and connect to other services; and life-skills, cultural and bursary programs for people already connected to Na-Me-Res. Corrected this verification pass: the provider's own current page no longer describes Street Outreach as including help finding an ID clinic for a SIN, birth certificate or OHIP card specifically — that detail is not repeated here. nameres.org/programs Mixed access, not one gender line: Street Outreach & Support Services is open to Indigenous men and women; NILO and the Espaniel/Menzies Education Fund bursary remain specific to Indigenous men. Most access runs through Na-Me-Res's own outreach team or an existing program relationship, not open walk-in. Free.

Honest gap: Streethaven's Grant House, a women's community residence funded by the Ministry of the Solicitor General for provincial parolees, is confirmed to exist on the City of Toronto's own policy page but its own program page could not be read this wave — see Honest Gaps, below, rather than a guess at what it offers.

Money and ID in the first weeks

General income support (Ontario Works, ODSP) and general ID-replacement help are not repeated here — see Guide 02 Claim Everything for both. What's specific to leaving custody:

  • The System Navigator Program (cited above, ) is the row built to help specifically because you're leaving custody — ID replacement and income/money management are named parts of its case management, not something you have to separately track down.
  • If you're on Ontario Works and applying for a record suspension, ask your caseworker about discretionary benefits — see the record section below.
  • Na-Me-Res (cited above, ) connects Indigenous clients — men and women — to housing, a crisis plan and other services through its Street Outreach team. Corrected this verification pass: the provider's own page no longer states specific ID-clinic help for a SIN, birth certificate or OHIP card, so that detail is not repeated here.

Work with a record

Working For Change — Out of This World Cafe, The Raging Spoon, Parkdale Green-Thumb Three Toronto social enterprises — a cafe, a catering company, and a horticulture/landscaping business — that name criminal justice history explicitly as one of several life experiences (along with mental-health and addictions challenges, poverty, homelessness, gender-based violence, and refugee/newcomer status) they build paid, supported employment around. About 55–60 people are currently employed across the three, with ongoing mentoring and peer support built into the job. Say plainly which part is open and which isn't: the organization's own page states its separate 12-week pre-employment training program is currently paused "as we try to raise the funds we need to support this important work." The three social enterprises themselves are described as actively operating and hiring — the training pathway is not. Refer yourself, or someone else, by email. workingforchange.ca — 416-504-1693. Anyone in Toronto whose employment has been affected by criminal justice involvement or other marginalization — not an exclusive door for people with a record, but one of very few Toronto employers naming one as an explicit non-barrier. Free to apply — this is paid work, not a program you pay to join.

General job search and training support, not specific to a record, is covered in full in Guide 12 Jobs and Employment.

The record: suspension, its $50 fee, and free help applying

Both of these are true, and this guide says both rather than picking one: a criminal record is the largest single barrier this domain found to work and housing after release — and the fee to clear that record is itself a barrier.

A record suspension (formerly called a pardon) asks the Parole Board of Canada to seal a criminal record so it stops showing on most background checks. The Parole Board's own application fee is $50, in effect since January 1, 2022 (down from a former $657.77) — confirmed on the Parole Board's own page. On top of that fee, expect roughly $70–90 or more for fingerprinting and criminal-record fees, plus a $45 local record-processing fee charged by Toronto Police Service specifically (figures as stated on Peacebuilders' own page, read 2026-08-01). None of these are waived automatically — but free help applying exists, in three places:

Elizabeth Fry Toronto — Record Suspension (Pardon) Program Free, one-on-one help completing and submitting your application — eligibility screening and document help — and, once your application is successfully submitted, Elizabeth Fry covers the cost of the $50 application fee itself. efrytoronto.org — Record Suspension Program — 416-924-3708 ext. 400, recordsuspensions@efrytoronto.org, 215 Wellesley St E. Anyone in Toronto. No documents required to start. Free, including the $50 fee once your application is accepted.

Peacebuilders — Record Suspension Support Free help clearing a criminal record after conviction. Peacebuilders' current site lists "Record suspension support — Help clearing records and rebuilding futures after conviction" among its live programs. When last verified in full (2026-08-01, on the program's own since-removed page) the program offered people aged 18–25 eligibility assessment, document gathering, guidance through the Parole Board application, and connections to employment, education, mental-health, housing and food-security support, with possible case-by-case fee help — you're still responsible for the fees above unless that help applies to you. Confirm those details when you call — the redesigned site no longer publishes them. peacebuilders.ca — info@peacebuilders.ca, (416) 960-0105 — ask about record suspension support. People with a criminal record, in Toronto. Free. Peacebuilders names Black, Indigenous and racialized youth in the justice system among who it has served for over 20 years. (2026-08-04 link-rot correction: the dedicated program page, the 18–25 age band, records@peacebuilders.ca, (437) 217-2328 and the 585 Dundas St E address are no longer published — details above dated accordingly.)

Ontario Works discretionary benefits — worth asking about Not a dedicated record-suspension program, but John Howard Society of Ontario's own province-wide guidance states that "some Ontario Works offices will pay for part or all of the cost of obtaining a record suspension." If you're on OW, ask your caseworker directly. Full detail on discretionary benefits generally is in Guide 02 Claim Everything.

A note on where not to look: John Howard Society of Toronto's own former Record Suspension program has closed — its current page now refers people to Elizabeth Fry and Peacebuilders directly, which is exactly what this guide does above. If you come across an older or province-wide listing naming a John Howard Society Toronto contact for record-suspension help specifically, treat it as out of date; use the two doors above instead. Reconfirmed this verification pass: the province-wide "Police Record Hub" listing (policerecordhub.ca) still names a specific JHS Toronto contact for record-suspension help even though JHS Toronto's own page confirms the program closed — the listing is still stale as of 2026-08-01.

Counselling, health and cultural support

Elizabeth Fry Toronto — Counselling Services (General, Reintegration & Standing Strong) Free one-on-one and group counselling: General Counselling (abuse and trauma, parenting, anger management, theft/fraud, domestic violence, substance use); Reintegration Counselling specifically for women on provincial probation or parole; and Standing Strong (TREM), a free 16-session trauma-recovery program for women and gender-diverse young adults 16–29 who have experienced family or gender-based violence. Free interpretation is available via the Barbra Schlifer Clinic. efrytoronto.org — Counselling Services — 416-924-3708, efrycounselling@efrytoronto.org. The organization's overall mandate centres women and non-binary people in conflict with, or at risk of, the law, though this program page doesn't explicitly restrict intake — confirm by phone. Free.

PASAN (Prisoners' HIV/AIDS Support Action Network) Support, education, harm reduction and advocacy specifically for people who are currently or formerly incarcerated and living with, or at risk of, HIV or Hepatitis C — including the Cell Count newsletter (written for and by current and former prisoners) and practical support for people leaving custody. Full detail on HIV care generally lives in fleet #21B Living with HIV — this entry exists here because PASAN's mandate is defined by incarceration status, not only by HIV status. pasan.org — 416-920-9567, toll-free 1-866-224-9978, 526 Richmond St E. People who are currently or formerly incarcerated and living with, or at risk of, HIV or Hepatitis C, across Ontario. Free.

If you're the family of someone inside

Families of people in custody are a separate reader with their own needs, and until this wave the atlas had no entry for them at all. A parent trying to explain a father's absence to a seven-year- old is not served by a reentry page built for the person coming home.

Canadian Families and Corrections Network (CFCN) A bilingual, confidential, free toll-free support line for families of incarcerated people — questions about visiting forms, phone calls, and mailing items to a loved one — plus programs including the Dad HERO Project (parenting education for incarcerated fathers), the Let's Read a Book Project (helps an incarcerated parent stay connected to their kids through reading), a Family Liaison Worker, Family Support Groups, Prison Letter-Writing Kits, Strengthening Families Events, a Resiliency Project, and a CFCN Scholarship for students with a current or formerly incarcerated parent or caregiver entering post-secondary study. cfcn-rcafd.org/programs-services — toll-free 1-888-371-2326. Families of incarcerated people, reachable from Toronto (a national charity based in Kingston, ON — not a Toronto-specific organization, but its toll-free line and programs are open to Toronto families). No documents required. Free.

Visiting, calls, and staying in contact

If you don't know where to start with visiting, phone calls, or mailing something to someone inside, call CFCN's toll-free line first — this is exactly the question it exists to answer.

What a phone call from provincial custody actually costs. Every call from someone in Ontario provincial custody is billed as a collect call — the person receiving it must accept the charge and be able to be billed for it. Ontario's own current pages (Ministry of the Solicitor General, still showing Updated: May 15, 2023 when re-checked this verification pass, 2026-08-01) publish two limits: up to five hours of phone access a day, and each call on a pay phone capped at 20 minutes. Phones are switched off from 11 p.m. to 6 a.m. A call made because of a serious family illness, injury or death is free — the institution pays.

What no government page states, as of this verification pass, is the per-minute rate or who currently runs the phone system. Families budget around this cost, so here is the honest state of it rather than a guess: the best-documented figure comes from an earlier era, under a Bell Canada contract that ran roughly 2013–2021 — a flat $1 for a local call, and about $1 a minute plus a $2.50 connection fee for a long-distance or collect call. Those numbers are old — do not budget as if they are today's price. CBC and the Globe and Mail reported family phone bills running into the hundreds or thousands of dollars a month under that pricing, and a proposed class action followed. Corrected this verification pass — the review's own dating: the CRTC did not simply "open a review" in 2025. The record traces to Telecom Decision CRTC 2024-324 (December 2024), in which the Commission found it lacked jurisdiction over the specific Ontario complaint but flagged broader national concerns about telecommunications service in correctional facilities; CRTC staff then sent every major phone provider and every provincial and territorial corrections ministry a formal information request dated 29 September 2025, with responses due 29 October 2025. As of this verification pass (2026-08-01), no further public ruling or rate decision has followed that request — the review remains in the information-gathering stage, not resolved. If the actual current cost matters to you right now, call CFCN's toll-free line above. (Policy fact, not a service row — sourced to Ontario's own inmate-telephone-communication pages and the CRTC's own public record, re-verified 2026-08-01; see this wave's research lane report for the full source trail.)

If there's a child in the picture

CFCN's Dad HERO Project (parenting education for incarcerated fathers) and Let's Read a Book Project (helps an incarcerated parent stay connected to a child through reading together) are built specifically for this — a reentry plan is not the same task as helping a child understand where a parent is, and this guide doesn't pretend it is. Call the same toll-free line above.

Honest gaps

  • Streethaven's Grant House, a women's community residence funded by the Ministry of the Solicitor General for provincial parolees, is confirmed to exist — re-verified this verification pass on the City of Toronto's own Ontario Works policy page, which lists it by name (87 Pembroke St) alongside Elizabeth Fry's Phyllis Haslam House as the city's two MCSCS-funded community residences for provincial parolees. Its own program page (streethaven.org) returned only a JavaScript-redirect wall on every attempt this verification pass, including a distinct Grant House subpage — three different attempts, all unfetchable. Directory listings elsewhere describe a roughly 6-month residential addiction-treatment program for women 24+, but that detail could not be confirmed on the provider's own page and is not printed here on that basis — a real gap in women's halfway-house coverage, not an oversight.
  • No Canadian federal bonding program (a fidelity-bond incentive for employers hiring people with a record) was found — this appears to be a U.S.-only mechanism with no domestic equivalent.
  • The current per-minute cost of a call from Ontario provincial custody is still not published anywhere a government page states, re-confirmed this verification pass (2026-08-01). The CRTC's information-gathering review (Telecom Decision 2024-324, December 2024, plus a 29 September 2025 follow-up information request to providers and every provincial/territorial corrections ministry) has not yet produced a public rate decision. This guide states the published rules plainly and states just as plainly that the price itself is not published, rather than presenting the last documented (2013–2021) rate as if it were current.
  • CoSA Toronto (Circles of Support and Accountability) was not checked this wave — worth a future pass, particularly for higher-risk releases.
  • No Black-specific reentry organization for adults (distinct from Peacebuilders' youth model, ages 18–25) was confirmed for Toronto.
  • General-population ID clinics (for anyone lacking ID, not specifically gated by custody status) exist but are out of scope for this guide per its own hard selection rule — see Guide 02 instead.
  • Working For Change's pre-employment training program is paused pending funding, in the organization's own words, reconfirmed this verification pass — its social enterprises remain open and hiring; this guide presents them as two different things on purpose.
  • John Howard Society of Ontario's province-wide "Police Record Hub" page currently lists a stale Toronto contact for a record-suspension service that JHS Toronto's own page says has closed — reconfirmed still stale this verification pass. This guide does not cite that listing.
