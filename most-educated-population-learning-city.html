Scope

This leaf's neutral scope question, drawn from its promoted document: could Toronto plausibly build a genuine lifelong-learning culture — using behavioural-economics design (nudges, defaults, social proof, habit) combined with structural access (libraries, learning accounts, AI tutoring) — and what does the evidence say about which levers actually work? This document covers: adult lifelong-learning participation and credential-attainment data for Canada/Ontario/Toronto; the behavioural-economics evidence base for learning interventions (nudges, gamification, commitment devices) and its honest limits; the "learning city" framework as an international policy concept, including the live-verified UNESCO Global Network of Learning Cities membership question; structural funding models for lifelong learning (Singapore's SkillsFuture as the leading comparator); and the equity/Matthew-effect risk of an undesigned learning agenda. It hands off, rather than duplicates: the library institution itself — TPL's operations, budget, hours, ROI, and its role as social infrastructure — to libraries-civic-commons (see research/backgrounders/libraries-civic-commons.md, already covering TPL's 2026 hours-expansion data and the unrefreshed 2013 ROI study in depth); and newcomer-specific credential recognition, foreign-credential underemployment, and the provincial/federal fair-access and bridge-training policy detail to newcomer-success-credential-recognition (see research/backgrounders/newcomer-success-credential-recognition.md, already covering Ontario's Bill 2/O.Reg 199/25 deemed-certification framework, the federal Foreign Credential Recognition Program, and Ontario Bridge Training funding status in depth). This leaf's own contribution is adult lifelong learning broadly, credential-attainment data at the population level, and the learning-city/behavioural-economics framework — not library operations specifically, not newcomer-specific policy in depth.

Current state

UNESCO's Global Network of Learning Cities: Toronto is not a member — verified directly, not assumed

The inherited master briefing lists "UNESCO Global Network of Learning Cities" among its "Real-world precedents" as "an international framework and peer network for lifelong-learning cities — a model and membership Toronto could lead" [inherited from the master briefing], without asserting that Toronto is actually a member. This pass's live-discovery task was to check that directly rather than let the ambiguous framing stand unverified. The verified finding: Toronto is not a member of UNESCO's Global Network of Learning Cities (GNLC). The Canadian Commission for UNESCO's own current page states plainly: "In 2022, Edmonton became the first Canadian city to join UNESCO's Global Network of Learning Cities. The Canadian Commission for UNESCO looks forward to further building this network in Canada" [NEW-2026-1] — language that only makes sense if Edmonton remains, as of this pass's search, the sole Canadian member. This is independently corroborated by UNESCO's own Institute for Lifelong Learning (UIL) member profile page for Edmonton, which records Edmonton's status as "Member since 2022" and describes Edmonton's own stated ambition as aiming "to become the first Canadian member of UNESCO GNLC and to encourage other cities to develop learning strategies" and to "serve as a catalyst for a Canadian learning network" [NEW-2026-2] — again, framing that presumes no other Canadian city, including Toronto, had joined as of this pass's check. The GNLC itself is confirmed live and active: as of this pass's search, the Canadian Commission for UNESCO describes it as consisting of "356 cities across 79 countries" [NEW-2026-1]. This pass did not locate any Toronto application, candidacy, or council motion toward GNLC membership — a gap stated plainly below rather than assumed either way.

Credential attainment: Canada, and by extension Toronto, is highly credentialed and still rising

Fresh 2025 Statistics Canada data updates and sharpens the inherited briefing's "Toronto is already among the most credentialed cities anywhere" framing [inherited from the master briefing] at the national level. Statistics Canada's March 2026 release states that "in 2025, nearly two-thirds of Canadian adults aged 25 to 64 (64%) held a college or university credential, up from 55% in 2015," with the increase "largely the result of growth in the number of adults with a bachelor's degree or a master's or doctoral degree" [NEW-2026-3]. The same release places this in international context: "In 2025, 64% of Canadians aged 25 to 64 held a college or university credential, above the average of 41% across member countries of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)... This gap is driven largely by Canada's college system. In 2025, 25% of Canadians aged 25 to 64 held a college credential — more than three times the OECD average of 7%" [NEW-2026-3]. A gender gap is also documented and widening: "In 2025, 71% of women aged 25 to 64 had attained a college or university credential, up from 61% in 2015... The share remained lower among men, but also increased over the same period, from 49% to 57%" [NEW-2026-3]. This is a national, not Toronto-specific, figure — this pass did not locate a Toronto- or Ontario-specific breakdown of the same 2025 data — stated as a gap rather than assumed proportional, consistent with this leaf's own citation discipline. It corroborates, at the national level with current data, the inherited briefing's core premise that credential attainment is high and rising, while doing nothing to address the inherited briefing's separate and more load-bearing claim — credential underutilization and underemployment — which this leaf explicitly hands off to newcomer-success-credential-recognition for the newcomer-specific case, per this document's own scope discipline above.

The behavioural-economics evidence base, re-checked: nudges remain modest, not transformative

This pass's live discovery did not surface new large-scale nudge-effectiveness studies superseding the inherited briefing's account of FAFSA-nudge nulls, mixed attendance-versus-attainment findings, and Philadelphia absenteeism-letter results [inherited from the master briefing] — the inherited briefing's characterization of the behavioural-nudge literature as "modest, mixed, and oversold" is not contradicted by anything found in this pass, and this document does not re-litigate that literature, consistent with the inherited-citation discipline. What this pass adds is current confirmation that the single most load-bearing structural comparator in the inherited briefing — Singapore's SkillsFuture universal learning credit — still shows the same participation gap the master briefing already flagged as "subpar participation," now with a specific, current, sourced figure.

Singapore's SkillsFuture, refreshed: still real, still underused

The inherited briefing cites SkillsFuture as the leading structural-funding comparator, noting "subpar participation" despite universal eligibility, attributed to time and cost-of-living barriers rather than lack of a voucher [inherited from the master briefing]. This pass's live discovery confirms the participation gap persists into the program's most recent reporting period: per SkillsFuture Singapore's own 2025 Year-in-Review reporting (as summarized in current 2026 program-update coverage), "only 1 in 2 Singaporeans have used their SkillsFuture Credit since its launch" [NEW-2026-4]. On the enterprise side, a parallel gap is reported: "close to 40,000 businesses have used the SkillsFuture Enterprise Credit, but that's only about half of those eligible" [NEW-2026-4]. The program itself continues and has been refreshed for 2026: "all Singaporeans aged 25 and above will receive an opening SFC of $500 which will not expire," with "eligible individuals aged 40 and above" continuing "to have access to additional credits of up to $4,000, supporting longer-term learning and career transition plans" [NEW-2026-4], while a separate one-time top-up tranche issued in 2020 expired December 31, 2025 [NEW-2026-4]. This is a direct, current-dated confirmation — not a re-assertion from memory — that the inherited briefing's central honest caution (money alone does not produce learning participation; roughly half of a universally-eligible population still has not used a non-expiring benefit years after launch) remains accurate as of this pass.

Toronto's actual municipal learning infrastructure: the Adult Literacy Program, confirmed directly

Independent of the library-operations material this leaf hands off to libraries-civic-commons, this pass directly fetched Toronto Public Library's own current Adult Literacy Program page to confirm what municipal-level adult-learning infrastructure concretely exists and how accessible it actually is, since the inherited briefing's "library as the engine" framing [inherited from the master briefing] is a claim about capacity and role, not a claim this leaf should take on faith without checking eligibility and access mechanics. The program "offers free, one-on-one tutoring in reading, writing, and math for English-speaking adults aged 19 and older," delivered by volunteer tutors, with eligibility restricted to adults who "can speak English," "live in Ontario," are "not enrolled in a high school or academic credit program," and with "preference... given to those who are grade level 3 and below in reading, writing or math" [NEW-2026-5]. Access is not immediate or guaranteed: "Enrollment is limited and not guaranteed. Depending on the assessment results, individuals may be placed on a waiting list or referred to a more suitable agency/organization" [NEW-2026-5], and the intake assessment itself "will take approximately 1.5-2 hours long and must be attended in-person" [NEW-2026-5]. The program operates from ten named library branches with partial weekly hours at each, funded in part by Employment Ontario and a "Get SET" partner logo displayed on the page as of this pass's fetch [NEW-2026-5]. This is directly relevant to the inherited briefing's own "friction reduction" behavioural-design thesis [inherited from the master briefing]: an English-language-only, in-person-assessment, waitlist-gated program is a real and valuable service, but it is not, on the evidence of its own eligibility and intake mechanics, a low-friction, one-tap-access model — a tension named explicitly below rather than left implicit.

Key tensions / tradeoffs

The inherited briefing's central honest caution — nudges plus access plus culture, not nudges alone — is reinforced rather than complicated by this pass's fresh SkillsFuture data, but the reinforcement cuts against the briefing's own optimistic framing of "structural access" as a sufficient complement to behavioural design. SkillsFuture already combines universal, non-expiring, no-cost access with periodic top-ups and enterprise-side incentives — a structural-access design about as generous as any real-world comparator offers — and still shows roughly 50% uptake since launch [NEW-2026-4]. This does not contradict the inherited briefing's own "time and money, not motivation, are the real barriers" diagnosis [inherited from the master briefing]; if anything it sharpens it: even removing the money barrier fully leaves the time/life-circumstance barrier largely intact, years into the program's life. A city-level learning strategy that reads "give people access and credits" as the solution, rather than as one necessary-but-insufficient piece, would be repeating a documented shortfall rather than improving on it.

Toronto's own credential attainment is rising nationally in step with the country as a whole, which sits in tension with the inherited briefing's "Toronto's problem is underemployment, not scarcity" framing. The fresh StatsCan attainment data [NEW-2026-3] documents rising credentials nationally; it says nothing about whether those credentials are being used — the inherited briefing's actual diagnosis is credential waste and underemployment, a claim this leaf does not itself re-verify and instead hands off to newcomer-success-credential-recognition, which independently confirmed via direct StatsCan fetch that recent immigrants with postsecondary credentials report meaningfully higher overqualification rates than Canadian-born workers (32.6% vs. 19.1% across a broader postsecondary population; 30.3% vs. 20.2% at the bachelor's-degree level specifically) [cited via that sibling leaf's own NEW-1 finding, not independently re-fetched here per this leaf's own scope discipline]. Rising aggregate attainment and persistent underutilization are not contradictory facts, but a backgrounder that cited only the rising-attainment figure without naming this tension would overstate progress toward the inherited briefing's actual goal.

The UNESCO GNLC finding is a genuine, disclosed correction to the inherited briefing's ambiguous framing, not a manufactured tension — stated plainly rather than softened. The master briefing's "Real-world precedents" section lists the GNLC in a way a reader could plausibly take as implying Toronto participation or eligibility already in motion [inherited from the master briefing]; this pass's direct verification found no such membership, application, or candidacy [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2]. This document corrects the ambiguity going forward rather than repeating it, consistent with this project's standing discipline of flagging inherited-document ambiguities without hand-editing the inherited document itself.

International context

1. Treaties/frameworks touched. UNESCO's 1976/2015 Recommendation on Adult Learning and Education (RALE) is the standing international instrument most directly on point for this leaf's own subject matter — it is the framework document underlying UNESCO's global adult-learning and lifelong-learning policy work, of which the GNLC (verified above as not including Toronto) is one operational network. This pass's search did not independently fetch and directly quote RALE's own text within this pass's budget — stated as a gap rather than a citation, since the discipline this template requires is a real pinned instrument/article citation, not a vague gesture; a future pass should fetch RALE directly before citing specific article language. Separately, the general right to education under Article 26 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and Article 13 of the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR) is a plausible broader connection, consistent with the same general provision the sibling libraries-civic-commons and arts-culture-creative-economy-spaces backgrounders name for adjacent access-to-learning/culture questions — named here for consistency across the corpus rather than independently re-verified in this pass.

2. 2-3 best global comparators. (a) Singapore's SkillsFuture, the inherited briefing's own leading comparator, refreshed in this pass with current 2025-2026 participation data showing continued "subpar" (roughly 50%) uptake despite a universal, non-expiring credit and targeted top-ups for those 40 and older [NEW-2026-4] — the clearest available real-world test of "does removing the money barrier alone solve participation," and the honest answer, still, is no. (b) Edmonton, Canada's only current UNESCO GNLC member (joined 2022) [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2] — a genuinely closer and more directly comparable Canadian precedent than any international city, not previously in this leaf's corpus, and one Toronto could examine directly rather than look only overseas; this pass did not independently fetch Edmonton's own "ConnectEdmonton" learning-city strategy documents or outcome data beyond what UNESCO's own member-profile page states, which is itself thin (a description of Edmonton's Senior Advisory Committee, the Edmonton Public Library's "City of Learners" initiative, and general SDG 4/11 framing, with no quantified outcome figures) [NEW-2026-2] — flagged as a gap for a future pass rather than oversold as a proven model. (c) The inherited briefing's own AI-tutoring comparator (Khanmigo and similar) [inherited from the master briefing] is carried forward as-is; this pass did not independently re-verify or refresh it.

3. What Toronto/Ontario can steal shamelessly. Two concrete, transferable pieces emerge from this pass's own findings rather than from the inherited briefing alone. First, Edmonton's own stated GNLC ambition — explicitly framed by Edmonton itself as wanting to "serve as a catalyst for a Canadian learning network" [NEW-2026-2] — is a live, open invitation for other Canadian cities to join the same network rather than build a parallel framework from scratch; GNLC membership itself is a concrete, nameable, joinable mechanism (an application/candidacy process through UNESCO's Institute for Lifelong Learning), not merely an abstract "Toronto should be a learning city" aspiration. Whether Toronto should pursue that membership is a recommendation-shaped judgment outside this document's own voice, per the neutrality firewall. Second, SkillsFuture's own 2026 program redesign — targeted top-ups for older/mid-career learners ($4,000 for those 40+) layered on top of a smaller universal base credit ($500) [NEW-2026-4] — is a concrete, nameable design choice (age/career-stage-targeted top-ups rather than flat universal amounts) that speaks directly to the inherited briefing's own equity/Matthew-effect concern [inherited from the master briefing]: a tiered structural-access design is one concrete way other jurisdictions have already tried to counter the "already-advantaged benefit most" dynamic, independent of whether it has fully succeeded (uptake data above suggests it has not solved the deeper time/circumstance barrier).

NEW-2026-1 — Edmonton as Canada's sole (and first) GNLC member; network scale.

"UNESCO's Global Network of Learning Cities consists of 356 cities across 79 countries that foster an inclusive culture of learning to empower citizens of all ages and enhance social inclusion, economic development and cultural prosperity... In 2022, Edmonton became the first Canadian city to join UNESCO's Global Network of Learning Cities. The Canadian Commission for UNESCO looks forward to further building this network in Canada, as part of its commitment to education for sustainable development."

"Edmonton, Canada... Member since: 2022... Edmonton aims to be a city built to connect and continuously learn. It aims to become the first Canadian member of UNESCO GNLC and to encourage other cities to develop learning strategies. The city hopes to serve as a catalyst for a Canadian learning network in line with the UIL framework."

Source: UNESCO Institute for Lifelong Learning, "Edmonton - UNESCO Global Network of Learning cities," https://www.uil.unesco.org/en/learning-cities/edmonton. Accessed via direct fetch 2026-07-14.

NEW-2026-3 — Statistics Canada, 2025 postsecondary credential attainment, national.

"In 2025, nearly two-thirds of Canadian adults aged 25 to 64 (64%) held a college or university credential, up from 55% in 2015. This increase was largely the result of growth in the number of adults with a bachelor's degree or a master's or doctoral degree... In 2025, 64% of Canadians aged 25 to 64 held a college or university credential, above the average of 41% across member countries of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)... In 2025, 25% of Canadians aged 25 to 64 held a college credential — more than three times the OECD average of 7%... In 2025, 71% of women aged 25 to 64 had attained a college or university credential, up from 61% in 2015... The share remained lower among men, but also increased over the same period, from 49% to 57%."

Source: Statistics Canada, "The Daily — Educational attainment in Canada has continued to rise over the past decade, 2025," released 2026-03-25, https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260325/dq260325c-eng.htm. Accessed via direct fetch 2026-07-14.

NEW-2026-4 — SkillsFuture Singapore, 2025 Year-in-Review participation and 2026 credit structure.

"According to SkillsFuture Singapore 2025 Year-in-Review, only 1 in 2 Singaporeans have used their SkillsFuture Credit since its launch. Additionally, close to 40,000 businesses have used the SkillsFuture Enterprise Credit, but that's only about half of those eligible... The one-time $500 top-up issued in 2020 expired on 31 December 2025... all Singaporeans aged 25 and above will receive an opening SFC of $500 which will not expire... Eligible individuals aged 40 and above will continue to have access to additional credits of up to $4,000, supporting longer-term learning and career transition plans."

Source: reported via SkillsFuture Singapore 2025 Year-in-Review, as summarized in Well Coaches SG, "Singapore Skills Future 2026: New Educator Standards And Expanded Mid-Career Support," https://wellcoachessingapore.com/singapore-skills-future-2026-update/, and corroborating current program pages at https://www.myskillsfuture.gov.sg/content/portal/en/header/faqs/skillsfuture-credit.html. NEW-2026-5 — Toronto Public Library Adult Literacy Program: eligibility and access mechanics.

"The Adult Literacy Program offers free, one-on-one tutoring in reading, writing, and math for English-speaking adults aged 19 and older (exceptions may apply)... Adults who can speak English, Live in Ontario, Not enrolled in a high school or academic credit program. Preference will be given to those who are grade level 3 and below in reading, writing or math... The assessment will take approximately 1.5-2 hours long and must be attended in-person by the individual applying for the program. Enrollment is limited and not guaranteed. Depending on the assessment results, individuals may be placed on a waiting list or referred to a more suitable agency/organization."

Source: Toronto Public Library, "Adult Literacy," https://tpl.ca/using-the-library/services/adult-literacy/. Accessed via direct fetch 2026-07-14.
