Who is the Unknown Soldier — the founding letter

You want to know who I am. That’s fair. Someone is asking for your trust, and trust usually starts with a name and a face.

I’m not going to give you either one yet, and I owe you the reason.

Here is what I can tell you. I’m a neighbour. I ride the same transit you do, wait in the same lines, love the same skyline from the same crowded ferry. I’ve been lucky in some years and not in others. I’ve seen this city be astonishingly good to people, and I’ve seen it look away. I am not a politician, and I’m not auditioning to become one. Nothing here asks you to vote for anyone. It asks you to look at your city, and then to help.

What do I actually want? You can read it in full — every plan, every source, every number traced to where it came from — but it fits in four lines: a floor under everyone, so no one in this city falls further than a life can recover from. A commons around everything we share, owned by us, walled against capture. A practice for anyone — things we do together, weekly, that make a city a city. And receipts on all of it: every claim checkable, every promise carrying its own test, in public, where you can watch us be wrong and watch us fix it.

It isn’t fear, and it isn’t a game. It’s this: the moment you know a name, you start judging the name instead of the work. You look up where I went to school. You find the photo you don’t like. You place me in a box — this neighbourhood, that generation, this accent, that job — and then everything I say arrives pre-sorted, half of it dismissed before it’s read. You’ve done it. I’ve done it. Everyone does it. It’s not a flaw in you; it’s just how names work.

So I took the name away, for now, to leave you with nothing to judge but the work itself.

The name I’ve taken has a history. The unknown soldier is the one whose name we never learn — honoured not for who they were, but for what they gave. I don’t claim that kind of sacrifice. I claim the other half of the idea: that a contribution can matter without a face attached, and that sometimes it matters more, because no one can make it about the person.

And there’s a second meaning, and it’s the real one: if the soldier has no name, the soldier can be anyone. Including you. This was never going to be one person’s work. The name is a door left open on purpose. When you put up your hand — when you check a claim, join a project, teach what you know, show up for your block — you’re not following the Unknown Soldier. You’re being one. That’s the whole design.

That last part is why anonymity is safe to offer you. You don’t have to trust me, and I’d rather you didn’t. Trust the receipts. Check the sources. Find the mistake — there will be mistakes — and tell us, loudly. A movement you have to take on faith is just another thing that can betray you. This one is built so it can’t ask for faith even when it wants to.

One day, probably sooner than you’d guess, I’ll put my name on all of this and you can judge me then — fairly or not, that’s the deal with names. Nothing will change on that day. The work will read the same, because it was written to be judged without me.

Until then: judge the work. And if any of it strikes you as worth doing — the floor, the commons, the practice, the receipts, or just one small piece of one of them —

then you already know who the Unknown Soldier is.

— The Unknown Soldier · uniteTOlove · Toronto
