State of the Election — Toronto 2026

The standing baseline of the biweekly record. Edition of August 5, 2026 — a complete refresh: every candidate re-pulled from the City Clerk's official feeds today, and every one individually verified or searched. The receipts are at the bottom. Previous baseline: July 23.

Reading this in real time: every field figure below — candidate counts, filings, web presence, incumbency — is a snapshot as of August 5, 2026, taken directly from the City Clerk's candidate feeds and re-verified candidate by candidate the same day. Nominations close Friday, August 21, 2026, at 2:00 p.m. — the field is still moving (five people filed today), and every number here will be recomputed and re-stamped after close.

The whole story in one paragraph

Sixteen days before nominations close, Toronto's election is moving: 223 people have filed for the city's 55 offices — five of them today — and for the first time this cycle every council ward has a contest on paper. The ward this page reported last month as having exactly one candidate now has two. But the deeper shape has not moved: twenty-one of twenty-five sitting councillors have already re-filed atop 2022's landslide margins, five wards still hold only a two-way race, one school-board seat has attracted zero candidates, and — now measured against every single candidate rather than a sample — about one in four candidates has no findable web presence at all, while fewer than half have published anything resembling a platform. Around the 1.9 million people who may vote stand roughly 900,000 residents who legally cannot, a daily region of millions more, and a scattered online conversation with no common room. Every one of those facts, read correctly, is an opening — and the first organized act is free, takes thirty seconds, and is open to every person in this paragraph including the ones who can't vote: register for the conversation. If you've ever considered the larger act, the papers take one afternoon, and the window closes August 21 at 2 p.m.

1. The moving field — what sixteen days can still change

All figures as of 2026-08-05, from the City Clerk's official candidate feeds:

  • 223 registered candidates: mayor 37 · council 132 across 25 wards · TDSB trustee 34 · TCDSB trustee 15 · French-board Toronto seats 5. Since July 23: mayor 32→37, council 123→132, TDSB 29→34, TCDSB 13→15. 23 filings since July 23 — 17 in the last seven days, 5 today. The field is accelerating into the deadline.
  • The acclamation watch is over. Last month this page reported a ward where, if nominations had closed that day, the seat would have been filled without a single vote. Someone filed. Zero council wards now stand at one candidate. A month ago this page said the difference between an empty contest and a real one "is measured in weeks." It was measured in twelve days.
  • The thin edge is still thin: five wards sit at exactly two candidates (Etobicoke Centre, York Centre, Toronto–Danforth, Don Valley West, Scarborough–Guildwood). The deepest fields — University–Rosedale and Beaches–East York at ten each, Parkdale–High Park at nine — include the city's two confirmed open seats.
  • One seat has attracted nobody at all: TCDSB trustee Ward 8. Zero candidates, sixteen days to close. And 12 of 29 school-trustee races carry exactly one candidate — on track to be filled without a vote if nothing changes.

Why this matters to you: the field is provably movable, this month, by ordinary people — the emptiest race on last month's map has a contest today because one person filed a form. In a two-candidate ward, a handful of organized neighbours is still the whole game. If you're eligible for that empty trustee seat, the papers cost $100 and an afternoon — and as of today the contest is literally nobody. Here's what running takes. The clock says August 21, 2:00 p.m.

2. The entrenchment — deeper than last month, and nearly complete

  • 21 of Toronto's 25 sitting councillors have re-registered — up from 17 on July 23. Two have confirmed they are stepping aside (Parkdale–High Park, Beaches–East York); only two wards' incumbents remain undeclared — the last genuinely open questions on the council map.
  • The default future is now almost fully filed: nearly every incumbent who can run again is running again, atop 2022 margins whose median was 32 points — 15 of 25 wards decided by more than 30 points, ten by more than 50, only three by under five (City of Toronto 2022 official results). The two razor-edge wards of 2022 — York South–Weston at 0.4 points, University–Rosedale at 0.5 — remain the proof that Toronto races can come down to dozens of votes.
  • The challenger field — 111 council non-incumbents across 25 wards — is larger than July's but no less alone: mostly unstaffed, mostly unfunded, and often without a findable page (§3).

Why this matters to you: unless something changes, your 2026 councillor is very probably your 2022 councillor, chosen by a fraction of your neighbours, by a wide margin. That is not an accusation of any person — it is a system running on defaults, and defaults are exactly what organized neighbours and real contests disrupt. Sixteen days remain to widen the field; four years of receipts-keeping begin the day after it closes.

3. The presence and platform layer — now measured wall to wall

What changed since July is not just the numbers — it's the completeness of the measurement. In July we reported our research coverage honestly but partially. Today, after re-pulling the Clerk's feeds and running per-candidate verification and search across the entire field, every one of the 223 candidates has been individually researched: every held website fetched and checked against the candidate's name, race and year today; every apparently-absent candidate actually searched for. These figures describe candidates, not our coverage:

  • 141 of 223 (63%) have a campaign website or platform page; 163 (73%) have at least one findable channel of any kind; 60 (27%) have none — and in nearly every case that absence is backed by a real search performed today, not by silence.
  • 99 websites were fetched and verified live today with the candidate's name, race and year on the page. Twenty more are built on scripts a plain page-fetch can't read — held as unverifiable, never counted as absent. A handful came back parked, stale, or pointing somewhere unexpected; every such case is flagged in the registry rather than silently kept.
  • Platform pages: 103 of 223 (46%). Among mayor-and-council candidates — July's comparable universe — 53%, up from 39% two weeks ago. Candidates are publishing as the deadline approaches. The school boards remain the dark zone: 15 of 34 TDSB registrants have no findable channel at all.
  • Where candidates are reachable, web-plus-Instagram is where Toronto's candidates actually live: websites 141 · Instagram 105 · Facebook 79 · X 54.

None of this is contempt. Candidates are running largely alone, unstaffed and unfunded — the vacuum is the shape of the invitation, and it is also the standard question this project puts to every candidate identically: by voting day the city should know exactly what each candidate proposes to do, written down, costed, dated. The full measurement — every published platform assessed for breadth, depth and quality under a public rubric — is its own page: the Platform Baseline.

Why this matters to you: you are hiring 26 executives for a multi-billion-dollar organization plus 29 school trustees, and as of today more than half have published no platform and a quarter cannot be looked up at all — a fact we can now state about every candidate, not a sample. You would not hire a contractor this way. The library's honest briefs and question banks exist so that any candidate — including one who files next Tuesday — can meet a professional standard within weeks, and so voters can demand it.

4. The turnout floor — and live-verified doors

  • Turnout in Toronto's 2022 municipal election was 29.7% — 563,124 ballots among 1,898,750 electors. The whole government was chosen by fewer than one in three of the people who could have chosen it. (Ward-by-ward turnout is not yet compiled in our registry — we say so rather than guess; see §7.)
  • The how-to-vote layer of this site was re-verified against thirteen official City and provincial pages on August 5: core dates unchanged — advance voting October 6–11, election day Monday, October 26 — while the City restructured its voter-information pages this week: proxy-voting rules are now published, the acceptable-ID list narrowed, and the province's online voter-registration tool closes August 12 — one week away. Details, every claim sourced and dated: how to vote.

Why this matters to you: at 29.7%, a municipal ballot is one of the highest-leverage acts a citizen has — not because any ballot weighs more, but because the deciders are three times fewer. And one of the easiest ways to be ready closes this coming week: checking your registration takes two minutes today.

5. Who can participate — the claims register holds

July's row-by-row reconciliation stands, unchanged by today's field refresh:

  • 1,898,750 electors on the 2022 voters' list; 2,794,356 residents at the 2021 Census. The honest sentence: roughly nine hundred thousand people inside Toronto's city limits — about one in three — were outside the 2022 franchise: children, permanent residents, temporary residents, and the uncounted. The exact split is a named open lane, not a guess (§7).
  • Immigrant Toronto is not voteless Toronto. Since the 2022 voters' list exceeds Toronto's entire Canadian-born population of every age, at least roughly 400,000 Toronto electors are foreign-born citizens — a decisive voting bloc plus an excluded remainder ("hundreds of thousands" remains the verified-safe phrase). Nearly half the city was born elsewhere; a large share of them vote; the ones who can't are excluded by citizenship law, not by immigration itself.
  • Around the city stands the rest of the decision's real constituency: the millions in the GTA outside the ballot, the daily commuter inflow, the international students and workers the census itself undercounts, and diasporas on five continents for whom Toronto is the proof-of-concept. The franchise is municipal; the consequences are metropolitan and global.

Why this matters to you: if you can vote, you are one of 1.9 million holding a proxy for nearly a million neighbours who can't. Your ballot is not only yours. If you can't vote, you are not voiceless: every other instrument in this project — the conversation, the vision bank, the volunteer bank — is open to you on identical terms, and the count of non-voter participants is a number this movement reports out loud.

6. The fragmented conversation — the thesis, sharpened

Toronto's civic conversation still happens in a thousand disconnected rooms — neighbourhood Facebook groups, language-community WhatsApp and WeChat circles, Reddit, X, campus Discords, congregation newsletters — each partial, ephemeral, unaccountable, and walled off from the rest. A false claim refuted in one room lives on in forty others; a thousand rooms each concluding "someone should do something" sum to nothing. Today's complete census adds the sharpest version of the supporting fact: the conversation is so scattered that one in four of the people asking to lead it cannot be found in it — and our verification pass found live candidate pages still carrying ward numbers that no longer exist and campaign sites pointing somewhere other than the race on file, each case flagged in the registry. No common room exists in which Toronto can hear itself. The answer is not "everyone leave your rooms" — the rooms are assets. The answer is one thin, common layer over many rooms: a single registration — register for the conversation — that any resident of the city, the region, or the world can complete in thirty seconds, that returns receipts instead of newsletters, and that carries what each room concludes into one shared, archived record. Your room stays yours. The city finally gets one.

7. What we still don't know — on purpose, out loud

Honesty gaps are listed, not hidden; nothing below is asserted anywhere above. Open verification lanes (each has an owner and a next step): per-ward and poll-level 2022 turnout (blocks any "your ward stayed home" claim — the single most motivating stat this page could hold); age-cohort turnout; the exact count of municipally excluded adult residents (blocks upgrading "hundreds of thousands"); the current city population ("a city of three million" is common usage, not a held figure); Toronto's non-permanent-resident count; the naturalization share of immigrant Toronto; one ward's 2022 baseline discrepancy (that ward is cited nowhere on this page by name); and trustee incumbency, which the TDSB's 22-to-12 ward restructure broke the records for. New honest classes from today's pass: 20 candidate sites unverifiable by plain fetch (held, neither present nor absent), one flagged registry residual (excluded from every count above), and the standing certainty that a late-filing wave will move every field figure between now and August 21 — which is why every number carries today's date, and why this page returns every two weeks until voting day.

8. The leverage math — unchanged, only more so

No formula converts electors into a victory number, and we don't publish one. What the honest math says: where seven in ten stay home, a crowd the size of a good summer festival is decisive — and Toronto produces those every summer weekend. Every section above shrinks the crowd it takes: a 29.7% floor (§4), five two-way wards and an empty trustee seat (§1), defaults nearly fully filed (§2), a platform vacuum any prepared candidate or informed voter instantly stands out in (§3). Apathy is not our obstacle. Apathy is our opportunity — and the pool of potential participants is not 1.9 million electors but everyone the decision touches.

You are not one voice in three million

You are one of a festival-sized crowd it would actually take — and if you can't vote, you are one conversation registration, one problem card, one vision statement away from counting anyway. Here's the one act every reader of this page can take today, whether you can vote in this election or not: register for the conversation. The same thirty-second act for the voter, the permanent resident, the commuter from Brampton, the international student, and the diaspora cousin watching from abroad.

The signature rail is being built and switches on when its custodian line is real; until then, start at the door — and if the larger act is calling you, so is the deadline.

Receipts — where every number comes from

Every figure on this page traces to one of these sources, each checkable without asking our permission:

  • City of Toronto Clerk's official candidate lists (mayor, council, trustee), re-pulled in full on August 5, 2026 — candidate counts, filing dates, and the candidate-submitted contact and web channels; archived at capture. Field figures re-stamp after nominations close August 21, 2:00 p.m.
  • Our per-candidate verification pass, August 5, 2026 — every held website fetched and right-person-checked the same day; every apparently-absent candidate individually searched; every anomaly flagged in the registry rather than silently kept. Method: how the research is made.
  • City of Toronto 2022 Official Declaration of Results — turnout (29.7%; 563,124 of 1,898,750), per-ward margins (median 32.0 points), and the 2022 baselines.
  • Statistics Canada, 2021 Census of Canada (Toronto CSD profile) and its published coverage studies — population (2,794,356), foreign-born share (46.6%), and the computed participation claims register; computations shown in the deep edition, illustrative rate-transfers excluded by rule.
  • Official election-authority pages (toronto.ca/elections, MyVote, Elections Ontario) — voting dates and rules, each live-verified August 5 and sourced line-by-line on the how-to-vote page.

Per-candidate assessments are never published; aggregates only — committed before publication, held privately by design. No aggregate on this page is attached to a group so small it would describe one or two identifiable people.
