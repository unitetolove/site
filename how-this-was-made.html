Written With Machines — the authorship disclosure

Yes. AI helped build this. A lot of it. Let’s talk about it plainly, because you deserve better than a disclosure buried in a footer.

Here is honestly how this work gets made.

A small number of humans — neighbours of yours — decide what this project cares about, what it will never do, and what gets published. Every value in it, every red line, every “we won’t touch that” came from a person. The questions came from people. The stubbornness came from people.

The machines do what machines are good at: they read faster than we ever could. They sweep thousands of documents, draft summaries, cross-check claims against sources, find the study we’d never have found, and argue with us at three in the morning. They also draft prose — including, we’ll say it before you do, early versions of pages like this one.

Now the part that actually matters, because “humans direct, AI drafts” is what everyone says. The question isn’t who typed it. The question is: why should you trust any of it?

Our answer is the same answer we give for everything, and it’s the reason this project can use these tools honestly while others can’t: nothing here asks to be trusted because of who — or what — wrote it. Every load-bearing claim carries a receipt that leads to a source that existed before any machine summarized it. AI systems are famous for confidently making things up. We know. That failure mode is precisely why the receipts architecture exists: a claim either traces to a real, archived, checkable source, or it’s labeled unverified, or it doesn’t ship. The pipeline was designed by people who assume the machine is sometimes wrong — the same way this project assumes we are sometimes wrong. Distrust, verified, all the way down.

Think of it this way: if a human researcher with a brilliant reading speed and an occasional habit of misremembering worked here, you’d want every one of their claims checked against sources before publication. That is literally our process. The species of the researcher was never the load-bearing part.

Could we have done this without AI? A version of it — smaller, slower, and honestly, years too late to matter. A handful of unpaid neighbours cannot read a hundred thousand pages of municipal reports, audits, and studies. With these tools, they can — and the whole point of this project is that the tools of institutional-scale knowledge finally fit in a community’s hands. It would be strange to build a movement about that and be embarrassed by it. We’re not embarrassed. We’re careful — which is different, and harder, and documented.

What the machines don’t do: they don’t decide our values. They don’t approve publication. They don’t choose what the movement asks for or who it stands with. And they don’t get blamed — if you find an error, it’s ours, the humans’, because signing the work means owning its mistakes. The corrections page doesn’t care where a mistake was born; neither should we.

You’ll meet people who say AI involvement discredits all of this automatically. Ask them the only question that matters: is the claim true, and how would we know? We’ve made that checkable. Most of what you’re asked to believe every day — by institutions with far bigger budgets than ours — has not.

Judge the work. Check the receipts. It’s the same invitation as ever. It doesn’t change with the typist.

— The Unknown Soldier · uniteTOlove · Toronto

The whole story — who runs this, how, and what it actually cost

Updated July 28, 2026. An earlier version of this page held a visible placeholder here, noting we hadn’t yet decided whether to name the specific AI systems and vendors used. That call is made, in three words: be completely honest. What follows is one story, told straight — the person, the controls, the money, and the month. All figures in Canadian dollars.

Start with the person, because everything else hangs off him. One human being directs, orchestrates, and controls all of it. To borrow from a certain gravel-voiced movie dad: he has a very particular set of skills. Skills acquired over a very long career. Not those skills — his are three-plus decades of living on the internet: finding things, reading things, checking things, building things, and refusing to log off. An elite operator of the information superhighway since before it had guardrails. If a forgotten municipal report exists, he will find it, and he will cite it.

And the machines answer to him — not loosely, but by written constitution. Every instruction he gives is captured word-for-word in a permanent log, and an AI session’s first duty is to read those words back correctly before acting on them. Every ruling he makes becomes a numbered decision on a permanent record. The machines propose; he ratifies — and where he delegates, the delegation itself is a numbered, revocable decision with his redline rights standing. Every substantive edit preserves a snapshot of the version before it, so the full history of every document stays inspectable forever. Every load-bearing claim needs a receipt. Every page faces adversarial review — hostile-reader personas hunting for the ways it could mislead — before it’s called ready. Automated checks sweep every batch of work for leaks and drift. And nothing goes public without his hand on the button. The machines here are brilliant and fast, and they are on a leash held by someone who has been on the internet longer than some of their training data.

Now the money, because it is the most instructive part. This person had not paid for a single AI tool in over two years. Then, on June 27, 2026, a friend lent him a Claude account — and bought him his own month of it as a gift. Everything on this site happened in the thirty-one days that followed. Committed in month one: about CAD $1,030, of which $140 was the gift. But here is the number that matters more, because most of those payments landed late in the month: pro-rate each subscription by the days actually used by July 28 — twenty-six days of the 5x, eleven of the first 20x, five of the second, one of the third, three weeks of Kimi — and the AI actually consumed so far comes to roughly CAD $330. A few hundred dollars. The rest is capacity for the month ahead, already bought. Everything you can read on this site — every page, every receipt, every guide — cost a few hundred dollars of machine time, directed by one unpaid human.

Around the paid seats, a rotating free bench did the bulk reading and cross-checking (free tiers as checked July 28, 2026 — they change often; each link is the living source): Qwen (free chat app, Qwen3.7-Max/Plus previews), Z.ai (GLM-4.7-Flash), DeepSeek (V4-family chat), ChatGPT (GPT-5.5 Instant), Gemini (3.6 Flash, with a daily taste of 3.1 Pro), Perplexity (Sonar), Google’s Antigravity preview (rate-limited Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Sonnet/Opus 4.6, GPT-OSS 120B), Vibe by Mistral (Medium 3.5), Meta AI (Llama 4), Grok (light free tier), Suno (v4.5-all, for music), and Microsoft Copilot (GPT-5.1 Smart mode) — plus one already-owned gaming PC running free open-weight models around the clock as a tireless local research assistant. The paid Claude seats do the judgment-heavy work: drafting, verification passes, and orchestration. Kimi is the deliberately different outside eye. The cheap and free tiers haul the bulk. Every tool’s output passes through the same receipts discipline — the vendor was never the load-bearing part either.

What did thirty-one days and a few hundred consumed dollars produce? A civic research library of 666 live pages — backgrounders, briefs, cards, and day-one implementation pages across every major issue, each carrying its verification status on its face. A living atlas of 765 free-help entries across 54 categories, from crisis lines to free skating, with audience guides being sealed one by one. A plain-language how-to-vote guide checked against the election regulation itself — a check that caught the City’s own published ID list omitting a document the regulation accepts, now flagged publicly. A candidate’s handbook for anyone deciding whether to stand in October. And the verification machinery under all of it. If you like numbers, the auto-generated tally that day: 5,950 files holding just over a million lines of text and code, 3,386 research and strategy documents, more than 150,000 rows and entries of structured data, 41,167 distinct external sources consulted, 93 numbered decisions, 112 logged working sessions, 74 written work orders between AI sessions — counted by a script precisely so the legend can’t outgrow the ledger, and re-run at launch.

The honest asterisks, before anyone else finds them: a friend’s gift started it; the free tiers are subsidized by their vendors; the gaming PC was already owned; quality varies by page and every page says so on its face — that is what the statuses are for. And none of it counts the human’s own time, which was enormous and unpaid.

Here is the message inside the numbers. One skilled person — unpaid, anonymous, directing machines with a written constitution and a few hundred dollars of compute — can now stand up a receipted civic research library at a scale that used to need an institute and a budget with commas in it. The machines didn’t bring the values, the questions, or the stubbornness. They made one stubborn person’s month go institutionally far. Now imagine a thousand of us, in every city, at this standard, with receipts. That is the actual invitation of this page. The door is on the home page. It takes thirty seconds.
