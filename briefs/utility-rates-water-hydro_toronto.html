Municipal Water and Hydro Rates — Backgrounder — Toronto

DRAFT v1.0 Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Issue: utility-rates-water-hydro · Data as of: 2026-07-14

Backgrounder: utility-rates-water-hydro · Issue: utility-rates-water-hydro Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Status: DRAFT v1.0 Data as of: 2026-07-14

Toronto is a single-tier city; its CD (3520) and CSD (3520005) codes both identify the same municipality.

TL;DR

This brief localizes the utility-rates-water-hydro backgrounder for Toronto — the jurisdiction the entire backgrounder was researched against, since both Toronto Water and Toronto Hydro are Toronto-specific bodies. Toronto's water rate rose 3.75% for 2025 and a further 3.75% (interim) for 2026, adding roughly $40/year to the average household's bill for a total of about $1,118 in 2026. Toronto Hydro's electricity distribution rate, unlike water, is set by the Ontario Energy Board rather than City Council, under an approved 2025–2029 plan with its own year-by-year bill-impact schedule. Toronto Hydro paid the City $60 million in dividends in 2025 as its sole shareholder — a documented structural fact sitting alongside the ratepayer-funded rate increases, not itself established as improper. The upstream backgrounder is marked DRAFT v1.0.

FOR

Evidence supporting the current rate-setting/investment approach:

  • Toronto Water's rate increases are tied to a stated, quantified infrastructure need: the 2026–2035 Capital Budget and Plan allocates $9.284 billion of Toronto Water's 10-year capital plan to renewal, projected to cut the state-of-good-repair backlog more than 55% over that period [backgrounder, "Infrastructure investment," ⚠️ still being checked — search-surfaced figures].
  • Toronto Hydro's rate-setting runs through an independent, evidence-based regulatory process (OEB) rather than unilateral utility or City discretion, which is designed to prevent either over-charging ratepayers or under-investing in reliability [backgrounder, "Two structurally distinct rate-setting regimes"].
  • Both rate streams have dedicated low-income relief mechanisms already in place: the City's Water Rebate Program and the province's Ontario Electricity Support Program, both confirmed directly from their own program pages [backgrounder, "Affordability and low-income relief programs"].

AGAINST

Evidence complicating the "rates are justified and well-managed" reading:

  • Both rate streams have risen at a multi-percent annual pace in the periods documented (3.75% water in both 2025 and 2026; a five-year OEB-approved hydro distribution-rate glide path), and no source in this page’s evidence base establishes what share of income-eligible households actually receive either relief program — a real, unresolved gap [backgrounder, "Open questions / data gaps"].
  • The jurisdictional split itself (City sets water rates; the OEB, not the City, sets Toronto Hydro's distribution rates despite the City owning Toronto Hydro) means no single accountable body owns the combined water-plus-hydro affordability picture for a Toronto household [backgrounder, "Key tensions / tradeoffs"].
  • Toronto Hydro paid the City $60 million in dividends in 2025 while its ratepayers face OEB-approved distribution-rate increases justified by capital investment need — a documented tension the backgrounder states plainly without asserting a causal or improper relationship between the two facts [backgrounder, "The Toronto Hydro dividend," ⚠️ still being checked — search-summarized figures].
  • Neither relief program (Water Rebate Program, OESP) is structured as a direct percentage-of-income bill cap; Philadelphia's Tiered Assistance Program offers a differently-designed comparator worth naming, though its own figures are flagged ⚠️ still being checked in this review [backgrounder, "International context"].

Precedents

  • Philadelphia, USA (Tiered Assistance Program, established 2016): caps eligible households' water bills at 2–3% of income rather than offering a flat rebate; reported serving roughly 60,000 households as of 2026, with a legislative proposal to expand eligibility further — a structurally different affordability-program design from Toronto's current flat-rebate model [backgrounder, "International context," ⚠️ still being checked].

Bottom line

For Toronto, water rates are set directly by the body residents can hold accountable (City Council) and rose 3.75% in both 2025 and 2026, while Toronto Hydro's electricity distribution rates — despite Toronto Hydro being wholly City-owned — are set by a provincial regulator under a multi-year plan running through 2029, with the City receiving $60 million/year in dividends from the same utility along the way. Existing relief programs exist on both sides but their actual reach into the eligible low-income population is not established in this page’s evidence base — the clearest locally-true synthesis is that Toronto residents face two structurally different, both multi-percent-annually-rising utility cost streams, governed by two different accountable bodies, with an affordability safety net whose real-world coverage remains an open question.
