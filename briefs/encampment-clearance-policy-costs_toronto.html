Encampment Clearance: Policy, Litigation, and Costs — Toronto

DRAFT Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Issue: encampment-clearance-policy-costs · Data as of: 2026-07-13 (backgrounder synthesis) + inherited leaf citations dated through 2026-07-13

Backgrounder: encampment-clearance-policy-costs · Issue: encampment-clearance-policy-costs Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Status: DRAFT · v1.1 · 2026-07-13 Data as of: 2026-07-13 (backgrounder synthesis) + inherited leaf citations dated through 2026-07-13

This is a CONTESTED topic. FOR and AGAINST below represent clearance-policy advocates' and encampment-rights advocates' strongest cases respectively, drawn only from claim-anchored evidence — neither section is this document's own position.

TL;DR

Toronto's encampment clearance policy sits between a 2021 Ombudsman finding of "significant unfairness" [CL-099] and a November 2025 Council vote (MM34.4, 16-3) tightening enforcement to a three-shelter-offer/50-metre-buffer/24-hour-notice model [CL-572][CL-713]. Clearance costs, itemized in detail only once, ran to roughly $2 million for a single 2021 operation [CL-354]. Toronto's own tent counts have fallen from 539 people (Nov. 2024) to 196 (Dec. 2025) [CL-90684], read by the City's CFO as genuine progress and by at least one community worker as progress accompanied by real data-transparency limits (Global News, Jan. 14, 2026; accessed 2026-07-13). A 2021 clearance-related lawsuit (Lamport Stadium) remains unresolved as of this writing, and a comparator Charter ruling against a similar bylaw in the Region of Waterloo — which found the bylaw violated both section 7 (security of the person) and section 15(1) (equality, including a discriminatory-impact finding against Indigenous, disabled, and women/gender-diverse residents) — is under active appeal as of June 2026 (2026 ONSC 2971; JURIST, May 23, 2026; CTV/CP24, June 16, 2026; both accessed 2026-07-13).

FOR (clearance-policy advocates' strongest case)

  • Toronto's current enforcement model offers residents three shelter placements before enforcement, with a 24-hour notice period and 50-metre buffer — materially more process than a bare removal, and passed by a decisive 16-3 Council margin [CL-572][CL-713].
  • The City's own count data shows a real, sustained decline in people living in tents — from 539 in November 2024 to 196 in December 2025 — which the City's Chief Financial Officer frames as a genuine, calculable improvement rather than a coincidence: "you can do the math on the reduction" (Global News, Jan. 14, 2026; accessed 2026-07-13).
  • The City has paired enforcement with real investment: $3.698 million in one-time federal Parks funding plus a dedicated 5-person cleaning crew [CL-406], and an $8.807 million 2025 TSSS encampment-response budget [CL-437] — clearance is not, on this framing, an unfunded mandate.
  • Toronto Fire Services runs a standing Encampment Strategy Team doing daily fire-risk site visits independent of any clearance decision — an ongoing safety function, not a one-off enforcement action [CL-356].
  • In the comparator Waterloo Region case, the Province and Regional Chair frame clearance-adjacent action around protecting major public infrastructure delivery (the Kitchener Central Transit Hub) for "hundreds of thousands of residents and visitors" (CTV/CP24, June 16, 2026; accessed 2026-07-13) — a named, sourced infrastructure-delivery argument specific to Kitchener's transit hub, not shown in this review's sources to have been made by any named party about Toronto's own infrastructure or public-space maintenance specifically; Toronto's own model already differs structurally from Waterloo's original no-alternative-site approach, and the ruling against that approach rested on both s.7 and s.15(1) Charter grounds, not s.7 alone.

AGAINST (encampment-rights advocates' strongest case)

  • The City's own Ombudsman found "significant unfairness" in 2021 clearance practices and issued 23 recommendations — the most recent formal fairness review on the books for this page, with no subsequent independent review of the 2025 enforcement framework identified [CL-099].
  • A single itemized 2021 clearance cost roughly $2 million, including $792,668 for turf restoration alone [CL-354] — money advocates can frame as better spent on housing than on enforcement and grounds repair.
  • The City's own count metrics are not reconciled: total sites, parks-with-tents, street sites, City-property counts, and people-in-tents are four or more genuinely different, non-comparable figures [CL-470], and even this review's own live-discovery fetch could not cleanly reconcile a 48-vs-49 parks-with-tents variance between two City-sourced figures. Community worker Diana Chan McNally, while crediting real improvement, still cautions that "without all the resources and without all the data available, it is hard... to even understand entirely what the situation looks like" (Global News, Jan. 14, 2026; accessed 2026-07-13) — a materially different, more qualified position than a flat "no real change" claim, and worth citing precisely rather than flattened.
  • Five plaintiffs allege traumatic brain injuries, lacerations, police baton injuries, and PTSD from the 2021 Lamport Stadium clearance — a case that remains unresolved, with no settlement or judgment reported as of a fresh 2026-07-13 discovery check [CL-355].
  • The clearest AGAINST-side precedent found this review is external to Toronto but structurally on point: an Ontario Superior Court found, in the Region of Waterloo's case (2026 ONSC 2971), that a clearance without any offered alternative shelter site violates the Charter's section 7 protection of security of the person, because "the region has clearly stated that if the encampment is cleared, it is not prepared to allow homeless people to shelter outdoors anywhere on region property" (CTV/CP24, June 16, 2026, quoting Justice Michael Gibson; accessed 2026-07-13) — a live judicial articulation of the human-rights floor clearance policy must clear, even though it has not (yet) been tested against Toronto's own three-shelter-offer model specifically. The same ruling went further on a second, independent ground: Justice Gibson also found the bylaw violated section 15(1) equality rights, recognizing homelessness itself as an analogous ground of discrimination and finding the bylaw's enforcement would fall disproportionately on Indigenous residents and residents with disabilities (both "over-represented in the unhoused population") and on women and gender-diverse residents (who "need to resort to unsafe forms of shelters, given the disproportionate lack of municipal shelter spaces" for them) (JURIST, May 23, 2026; accessed 2026-07-13) — a distinct, additional Charter argument against clearance-without-alternative that the s.7 framing alone does not capture.

Precedents

  • Region of Waterloo (Kitchener), 2025–2026: a regional bylaw clearing an encampment with no offered alternative site was found unconstitutional by the Ontario Superior Court (2026 ONSC 2971, Gibson J., decision dated May 21, 2026, reported May 22-23, 2026) on two Charter grounds: section 7 (security of the person, no alternative site) and section 15(1) (equality — homelessness recognized as an analogous ground, with a specific discriminatory-impact finding against Indigenous, disabled, and women/gender-diverse residents), neither saved by section 1 (JURIST, May 23, 2026; ULLAW, May 26, 2026; both accessed 2026-07-13). The Region and Province are appealing as of June 16, 2026, with Regional Council voting 10-5 in favour of the appeal (CTV/CP24, June 16, 2026; accessed 2026-07-13). Outcome: unresolved, active appeal — not a settled precedent either way, and Ontario's Premier has publicly floated, but not yet invoked, the Charter's notwithstanding clause as an alternative route (same source).
  • Toronto, Sanctuary et al v. Toronto (City) et al, 2020 ONSC 6207: a narrower, COVID-era shelter physical-distancing case, not a clearance case directly; the City conceded 32 of 7,152 beds across 7 sites failed distancing standards under an Interim Settlement Agreement [CL-347]. Outcome: settled by concession on the narrower point, with broader allegations from other parties left unresolved.

Bottom line

Toronto's clearance policy has moved toward more process (three shelter offers, notice, buffer) since the 2021 Ombudsman finding, and the City's own count data shows a real decline in people living in tents through 2025 — but no independent fairness review of the current 2025 enforcement framework has been identified, a 2021 clearance lawsuit remains unresolved, and a live Ontario Charter ruling in a comparable municipality (2026 ONSC 2971) has just found that clearance without any offered alternative site fails constitutional scrutiny on two independent grounds — section 7 security of the person, and section 15(1) equality (including a specific discriminatory-impact finding against Indigenous, disabled, and women/gender-diverse residents) — a floor Toronto's own model has not yet been tested against in court.

Uncertainties

  • ⚠️ Still being checked: the 48-vs-49 parks-with-tents discrepancy between two City-sourced figures, not reconciled.
  • ⚠️ Still being checked: a reported Toronto Council motion narrowing a 200-metre school/daycare/playground buffer to 50 metres, and an associated "46 encampments resolved" figure — surfaced in this review's search results but not independently fetched/quoted live, so not asserted as fact in this brief.
  • No independent fairness review of the November 2025 MM34.4 enforcement framework has been identified in this page’s sources.
  • The Lamport Stadium lawsuit's damages figures differ between the Globe and Mail and NOW Toronto and are not reconciled here [CL-355].
  • The Waterloo Region Charter appeal's outcome, and whether the notwithstanding clause is ever invoked, are both genuinely open as of 2026-07-13 — this brief does not predict either.
  • Toronto's own clearance costs have been itemized in comparable detail only once (2021); no equivalent itemization for any subsequent operation was located.
