Development Charges & Building Permits — Toronto

DRAFT v1.0 Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Issue: development-charges-building-permits · Data as of: 2026-07-14

Backgrounder: development-charges-building-permits · File: research/backgrounders/development-charges-building-permits.md · Issue: development-charges-building-permits Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Status: DRAFT v1.0 Data as of: 2026-07-14

Toronto is a single-tier city; its CD (3520) and CSD (3520005) codes both identify the same municipality.

TL;DR

Toronto's development-charge framework has changed at least five times since 2022 across both municipal and provincial action — Bill 23 (2022, reducing DC-recoverable costs), the City's 2022 DC Background Study, Bill 185 (2024, reversing several Bill 23 mechanisms), the City's June 2025 rate update and Council exemption changes, and Bill 17 (2025, provisions not yet characterized in this leaf's evidence base). Meanwhile, Ontario's own government documents — internal ministry notes and the 2026 Budget itself — now describe the province's 1.5-million-homes-by-2031 target as unreachable at current pace, with the gap widening across successive government reports rather than narrowing.

FOR

Evidence supporting the case that DC-framework and permit-approval reform is actively responsive to the housing-supply challenge:

  • Bill 185 (2024) explicitly reversed Bill 23's own DC-recovery restrictions "to enable municipalities to recover more of the costs to build critical infrastructure that is needed to service new homes" toward the province's own 1.5-million-homes goal [backgrounder §"What DCs are and Ontario's repeated legislative amendments"].
  • Toronto's own 2025 Council changes (EX24.2, MM32.5) added DC exemptions for developments up to six units and removed indexing for 2025-2026 — a specific, targeted supply-side lever the City controls directly.
  • The federal Housing Accelerator Fund rewards municipalities for faster permitting specifically, with 32 municipalities nationally already receiving over $42 million in additional funding for demonstrated progress, and HAF-funded municipalities collectively issuing over 334,000 residential building permits — a national, not Toronto-specific, figure but evidence the incentive structure exists and has produced measurable results elsewhere.

AGAINST

Evidence complicating the case that reform to date has been sufficient or well-coordinated:

  • Ontario's own internal government documents (October-December 2024 ministry notes obtained by Global News; the 2026 Budget itself) independently conclude the 1.5-million-homes target will not be met, with the shortfall assessed as larger in each successive document — at roughly 175,000 annual starts needed and the 2026 Budget projecting only 64,800 for 2026, the target is characterized in this leaf's inherited source as "mathematically unachievable, not just behind schedule."
  • AMO's own cost estimate for Bill 23's DC-revenue impact required substantial revision after passage (from a preliminary $5.1 billion over nine years to roughly $1 billion annually) — evidence that even close observers found the initial policy's fiscal effect difficult to estimate accurately, a governance-quality concern independent of which direction the policy moved revenue.
  • An industry-commissioned benchmarking study (BILD/Altus) found average GTA development-approval timelines of 20.3 months per application type, with wide inter-municipal variation and only marginal system-wide improvement (2.4 months) since the prior study — though this figure comes from a developer-industry association, not an independent government source, a provenance distinction this document does not paper over.
  • Ottawa's own Auditor General found process delays attributable to multiple parties (incomplete applicant submissions, late internal legal consultation, inconsistent inter-departmental conditions, and "aggressive developer attempts to 'skip the line'") — a useful comparator showing delay is not attributable to one side alone even within a single credible audit.

Precedents

  • British Columbia's development-cost-charge system: per a C.D. Howe Institute comparative analysis, BC municipalities collected roughly $9 billion in development charges since 2009 and spent about $8.5 billion of that on capital-related infrastructure — a materially higher, faster capital-deployment ratio than the same analysis found for Ontario municipalities over a comparable period [backgrounder §"International context"; staged discovery, not independently re-fetched this pass].
  • City of Ottawa's Auditor General audit of development-application review: an intra-Canadian comparator showing multi-party delay attribution within a single credible audit process, potentially instructive for a Toronto-specific audit of the same kind.

Bottom line

For Toronto, the clearest locally-true synthesis is that the City has been an active, if reactive, participant in a rapidly shifting DC-framework environment largely driven by provincial legislative volatility (three DC Act amendments in three years) — and that whatever the net local fiscal effect of that volatility turns out to be, it is occurring against the backdrop of a provincial housing-supply target the government's own documents now describe as unreachable at current pace, a fact independent of, though clearly relevant context for, any Toronto-specific DC or permitting reform.

Uncertainties

  • Bill 17's (2025) specific provisions and their dollar effect on Toronto's DC revenue are not yet characterized in this leaf's evidence base — a genuine, named gap.
  • AMO's restated ~$1 billion/year Bill 23 cost figure's primary publication was not independently located or fetched this pass.
  • The BILD/Altus 20.3-month approval-timeline figure comes from a developer-industry-association-commissioned study, not an independent source — a provenance distinction worth weighing in any downstream use.
  • No source in this leaf's evidence base directly connects DC-framework changes causally to the widening 1.5-million-homes gap; both sides of evidence exist without a claim bridging them.
