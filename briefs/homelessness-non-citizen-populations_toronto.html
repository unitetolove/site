Non-Citizen Populations: Population Data and Service Eligibility — Toronto

DRAFT Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; CD and CSD codes coincide, sgc_code 3520005/3520) · Data as of: 2026-07-14 (National Shelter Study 2024 Update, published September 2025, live-fetched)

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 Backgrounder: research/backgrounders/homelessness-non-citizen-populations.md · Data as of: 2026-07-14 (National Shelter Study 2024 Update, published September 2025, live-fetched) Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; CD and CSD codes coincide, sgc_code 3520005/3520) · Trust classes: INHERITED (leaf-promoted docs) + NEW-LOAD-BEARING (this pass's quote-context findings, cited NEW-2026-NC-#, pending formal ledger promotion)

TL;DR

Nationally, 85.7% of Canadian shelter users are citizens, with resettled refugees and asylum seekers rising to 6.6% of shelter users in 2024 (up from 5.1% in 2023 and 0.9% in 2021) [NEW-2026-NC-2]. Toronto's own refugee-claimant share reached 53% in October 2024 — roughly eight times the newly-confirmed 2024 national rate — before falling to roughly 3,420-3,500 nightly by August-September 2025, tracking a 34% national decline in asylum claims over the same window [NEW-2026-NC-3, NEW-2026-NC-4, NEW-2026-NC-5]. Toronto's sanctuary-city policy (Access T.O.) means the City cannot and does not track undocumented/without-status shelter users by design, and no federal funding mechanism exists for that population at all — a structural gap, not a funding-level shortfall [NEW-2026-NC-6, NEW-2026-NC-7].

FOR (the current funding/data architecture is coherent, if incomplete)

  • Refugee claimants — the population Toronto's shelter data tracks with genuine precision — are matched to a real, dedicated federal funding stream (IHAP), because they are definitionally in an active federal determination process [NEW-2026-NC-7].
  • Toronto's own refugee-claimant shelter population has fallen substantially and consistently across two independently-sourced City documents (from a 6,600-6,490 peak in August 2024 to roughly 3,420-3,500 by August-September 2025), tracking a genuine national decline in asylum claims rather than reflecting an uncontrolled local trend [NEW-2026-NC-4, NEW-2026-NC-5].
  • Federal policy is not static or indifferent to housing pressure: IRCC's own stated rationale for cutting international-student and temporary-worker permit numbers explicitly cites "managing pressures on housing, health care, and local infrastructure" [NEW-2026-NC-8], and a May 2026 policy update reduces, rather than increases, the number of people falling into undocumented status at permit expiry [NEW-2026-NC-9].
  • Access T.O.'s no-status-inquiry design serves a genuine equity goal (universal shelter access regardless of immigration status) that this leaf's own promoted source frames as a deliberate policy choice, not an oversight.

AGAINST (a real, structural gap remains, and a new risk is emerging)

  • There is no federal funding mechanism at all — not merely an underfunded one — for the without-status population Toronto's own sanctuary policy commits it to serving [NEW-2026-NC-7]. This is a structural claim independent of any specific dollar figure, and it is not addressed by any of the mitigating federal trends above.
  • Bill C-12 (Royal Assent March 26, 2026) creates a direct, mechanical pathway for refugee claimants — currently in the funded IHAP category — to be pushed into the entirely-unfunded without-status category, with up to 30,000 people nationally already sent procedural fairness letters as of April 2026 [NEW-2026-NC-10]. No Toronto-specific impact has been quantified, but the mechanism is real and dated.
  • The same policy (Access T.O.) that serves equity also forecloses precise cost accounting — meaning Toronto genuinely cannot answer, with its own data, how large the unfunded without-status population's shelter footprint actually is, a real gap for planning purposes even though the policy itself is defensible.
  • The federal National Shelter Study's own methodology excludes "immigrant and refugee shelters" from its national figures — meaning the true national comparator for Toronto's dedicated refugee-shelter system may be incomplete, a limitation on any Toronto-vs-national comparison this brief or its backgrounder makes.

Both sides draw on the same federal and leaf-promoted sources; this brief states the documented architecture and its gaps without asserting whether the current funding split is adequate.

Precedents

This leaf's own scope does not cover the exclusion-policy or litigation history (handed off to homelessness-refugee-claimant-exclusion) or general settlement-pressure precedents (handed off to newcomer-settlement-shelter-pressure); no independent non-Toronto municipal precedent specific to population-data/service-eligibility categorization by immigration status was identified or verified in this pass.

Upward Ask

research/issue-map.md's E4 row (newcomer-settlement-shelter-pressure) names the Owner as "Federal (immigration policy, settlement funding) with municipal service-delivery pressure" — directly relevant to this leaf's own subject, since the structural funding gap for the without-status population is, on this document's own analysis, a federal-jurisdiction gap. This leaf's promoted source does not identify a specific Toronto Council motion requesting a new federal funding mechanism for the without-status population specifically (as distinct from IHAP-level funding asks for refugee claimants, which belong to homelessness-federal-funding-architecture) — stated here as a gap in the documented advocacy record, not evidence no such ask exists.

Bottom line

Toronto's shelter data can track refugee claimants with real precision and connects them to a real federal funding mechanism (IHAP) — but by the same sanctuary-policy design, it cannot and does not track the without-status population, for whom no federal funding mechanism exists at all, a structural gap a new federal policy (Bill C-12) may be about to widen in a way nobody has yet quantified for Toronto specifically.

Uncertainties

  • Whether the national shelter study's exclusion of dedicated immigrant/refugee shelters affects the reliability of any Toronto-vs-national comparison in this brief.
  • The Toronto-specific scale of Bill C-12's impact — how many of the 30,000 national procedural-fairness-letter recipients are in Toronto, and what shelter-demand effect (if any) follows — is not established in this pass.
  • Whether any Toronto-specific data exists on international-student or temporary-foreign-worker shelter use distinct from the general population was not found and may not exist.
  • All NEW-2026-NC-# findings in this brief are directly quoted from primary or leaf-promoted sources but have not yet been run through this project's formal claim-verification/ledger-registration pipeline — treat as ⚠️ VERIFY-equivalent pending that pass.
