Rental Market & Tenant Protections — Toronto

DRAFT Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005 (CSD), 3520 (CD — single-tier city, both codes identify the same municipality) · Issue: rental-market-tenant-protections · Data as of: 2026-07-13 (live-discovery findings) / 2026-07-12–13 (formally registered claims)

Backgrounder: rental-market-tenant-protections · Issue: rental-market-tenant-protections Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005 (CSD), 3520 (CD — single-tier city, both codes identify the same municipality) Version: v1.1 · Date: 2026-07-13 · Status: DRAFT Data as of: 2026-07-13 (live-discovery findings) / 2026-07-12–13 (formally registered claims)

TL;DR

Toronto tenants sit inside a system where the largest levers — the rent-increase guideline, the post-2018 rent-control exemption, and the Landlord and Tenant Board itself — are entirely provincial [CL-0284, CL-0285], while the City holds a narrower but real toolkit: its own Rental Renovation Licence Bylaw (in force since July 31, 2025) [NEW-5, backgrounder], the Rent Bank and Eviction Prevention in the Community program (funded to help nearly 4,500 households in the 2026 budget) [NEW-6], and a standing, repeated set of formal asks to the Province (2023, 2024, and again in November 2025) that the largest structural fixes remain unmet [NEW-3]. Toronto's own vacancy rate (2.3%) sits below the commonly cited 3% healthy threshold, and vacant units in the city rent for $512–$1,089 more than occupied units of the same type [NEW-2] — the dollar-denominated shape of the displacement incentive this whole issue turns on.

FOR (tenant-protection direction)

  • Toronto's Rental Renovation Licence Bylaw, in force since July 31, 2025, requires landlords to obtain a City licence before an N13 (renovation) eviction, with tenant-notification, temporary-accommodation-or-compensation, and return-rent provisions built in [NEW-5, backgrounder].
  • The City's 2026 budget nearly doubled Rent Bank funding since 2023 (to $10.8 million, helping 2,800 families) and added EPIC/Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit support for 1,100 more households, for a combined total of nearly 4,500 households kept housed [NEW-6, backgrounder].
  • City Council has formally and repeatedly (2023, 2024, November 2025) asked the Province to restore rent control on post-2018 units and to invest in LTB adjudicator capacity — a documented, standing municipal position, not a one-off [NEW-3, backgrounder].
  • The LTB's own active caseload has fallen 26% from its December 2023 peak (53,057 → 41,465), independently confirmed by a third-party review of the same Annual Report [CL-0279; NEW-4, backgrounder] — real, if partial, progress on the provincial side.

AGAINST (counter-evidence / limits)

  • The rent-increase guideline exemption for units first occupied after November 15, 2018 remains fully in force and unchanged by any City action, since the City has no authority over it [CL-0285].
  • Bill 60 (introduced October 2025, debate limited by a time-allocation motion passed November 6, 2025, passed and received Royal Assent November 27, 2025 — now Chapter 14, Statutes of Ontario, 2025 [backgrounder NEW-7]) reduces tenant compensation for own-use evictions, shortens arrears-filing and appeal windows, and restricts tenants' ability to raise new issues at a hearing — all provincial changes the City opposed and that became law regardless [NEW-3, NEW-7, backgrounder].
  • Despite the caseload reduction, LTB hearings in 2024-25 still took roughly three times as long as in 2018, and per-adjudicator productivity fell from 950 to somewhere in a 382-528 range of resolved-via-hearing cases even as headcount more than doubled [NEW-4, NEW-8, backgrounder] — the range, not a single number, reflects a independently re-verified finding (2026-07-14) that Tribunals Ontario silently revised its own Annual Report's case-disposition data after tabling, with no changelog and no response to Tribunal Watch Ontario's written request for an explanation. The productivity decline holds under either figure. The City's own preferred fix (more adjudicator capacity) has already been tried at scale by the Province with disappointing productivity results either way, a genuine complication for the City's own advocacy ask.
  • The City's own staff report states existing eviction-prevention programs (EPIC, Rent Bank) are "already over-subscribed" even before accounting for Bill 60's additional demand, which is no longer merely likely — Bill 60 is now in force [NEW-3, NEW-7, backgrounder] — the 2026 funding increase, while real, is not represented by the City itself as sufficient.

Symmetry note: both sides here draw on 4 points each, and both draw substantially on the same underlying City of Toronto staff report — this is a genuine feature of the evidence (the City's own analysis documents both its own actions and the limits/pressures on them in one document) rather than a sign of lopsided sourcing.

Precedents

  • Hamilton and New Westminster, BC — renoviction by-laws. Cited in the page’s inherited master briefing as the precedent Toronto's own Rental Renovation Licence Bylaw was modeled on. our verification track update, 2026-07-14 (see backgrounder NEW-9, "Open questions" section): Hamilton now has one year of outcome data (N13 notices 119→23, 2024→2025, per WebSearch-synthesized CBC News coverage not yet independently confirmed by direct fetch — ⚠️ still being checked), but licence uptake was modest (7 applications, 1 issued). New Westminster's own bylaw is not a current precedent — it was repealed in 2021 after a provincial Residential Tenancy Act change made it inoperative, confirmed via direct fetch of the City's own 2021 news release — so any comparison to it should be understood as historical (2018-2021), not live.
  • Toronto's own July 2025 Rental Renovation Licence Bylaw is itself now a precedent other Ontario municipalities could examine — it is the first Ontario example in this project's evidence base of a renoviction licence layered directly onto the provincial N13 process [NEW-5, backgrounder].
  • Mississauga's Rental Housing Protection By-law 0121-2018 — a differently structured municipal tool (triggered by vacancy-rate and rent-level thresholds, aimed at conversion/demolition rather than renovation-eviction specifically) [CL-0514–CL-0517] — is a real Ontario comparator for what a different design of municipal rental-stock protection looks like, distinct from Toronto's approach.
  • Toronto's own bylaw still lacks outcome data (displacement prevented, bad-faith applications denied); Hamilton's N13-drop figure above is the closest comparator evidence available anywhere in this project's corpus, but remains ⚠️ still being checked pending direct-source confirmation, and New Westminster's is a legally-tested design precedent only, not a live outcome precedent.

Upward Ask

this library's issue index assigns the following owner(s) to this issue: Provincial (Residential Tenancies Act, LTB); municipal role limited (rental licensing where adopted).

Because the Owner column names a non-municipal level, this section records formal council asks already made toward that level. this library's municipal-asks table currently has no rows matching this jurisdiction and issue slug — a gap this brief can partly close from the backgrounder's own live-discovery findings, pending formal entry into that CSV by whoever maintains it:

  • 2023-11-08/09 (EX9.3): City Council adopted "Generational Transformation of Toronto's Housing System" and, within it, requested the Government of Ontario re-introduce rent control to cover units occupied after November 15, 2018 [backgrounder NEW-3].
  • 2024-06-26 (PH13.8): City Council adopted the City's Rental Housing Supply Program and reiterated the same request — amend the RTA and/or related regulations to re-introduce rent control for post-2018 units [NEW-3].
  • 2025-11-12/13/14 (EX27.1): City Council formally opposed Bill 60's RTA changes and reiterated three requests together: restore rent control on post-2018 homes; invest in LTB adjudicator resources to speed up hearings; and invest in incentives for new purpose-built rental construction [NEW-3]. The same Council term also directed City staff to submit the Bill 60 impact analysis to Ontario's Regulatory Registry as part of the Province's own (now-closed, November 22, 2025 deadline) consultation on select Bill 60 provisions [NEW-3]. Bill 60 nonetheless proceeded to Royal Assent on November 27, 2025, roughly two weeks after Council's opposition motion [backgrounder NEW-7] — the City's opposition was recorded but did not stop passage.

Three formal, dated council asks toward the Province on this issue in roughly 24 months is the count as of this brief's date — states this as a fact about the state of advocacy, not a call to action, per the Upward Ask template's own discipline.

Bottom line

For Toronto, the single most defensible synthesis sentence: the City has one real, newly in-force municipal tool (the Rental Renovation Licence Bylaw) and a growing but self-described "over-subscribed" set of eviction-prevention supports, while the two structural fixes it has now asked the Province for three times in two years — restoring rent control on post-2018 units and adding real LTB capacity — remain unmet, and the Province's most recent legislative move (Bill 60, now in force since Royal Assent on November 27, 2025) runs in the opposite direction on several specific provisions the City itself flagged as reducing tenant protection.

  • Resolved (2026-07-13): the 2026 provincial rent-increase guideline (2.1%) was flagged ⚠️ still being checked in the prior pass because only a secondary source could be confirmed; it is now independently confirmed directly against ontario.ca's own historical guideline table (a re-fetch attempt this review succeeded where the prior one was JavaScript-gated) [backgrounder NEW-1, now verified].
  • Correction (2026-07-13): Bill 60's legislative status is now confirmed — Royal Assent November 27, 2025, Chapter 14, Statutes of Ontario, 2025 [backgrounder NEW-7]. What remains unconfirmed is whether any specific provision was amended between the November 10, 2025 staff report (describing the bill as introduced) and the final enacted text, and the coming-into-force date of individual provisions not triggered by Royal Assent itself [backgrounder NEW-7, ⚠️ still being checked].
  • Toronto Rental Renovation Licence Bylaw's specific fee and penalty figures rest on secondary news coverage, not the bylaw's own primary fee schedule, in this review [backgrounder NEW-5, ⚠️ still being checked].
  • No outcome data (licences approved/denied, fines issued) exists yet for Toronto's own renoviction bylaw — Council's own comprehensive review isn't due until 2027.
  • Tribunal Watch Ontario's own account flags that the Tribunals Ontario 2024-25 Annual Report's data was apparently altered after public tabling with no disclosed authority — every LTB performance figure sourced to that report (via formally registered claims or NEW-4) carries this same integrity flag [backgrounder NEW-4].
