The Community Safety Perception Gap — Toronto

DRAFT Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Issue: community-safety-perception-gap · Data as of: 2026-07-14 (mining a merge-group spine document plus this pass's live discovery)

Backgrounder: community-safety-perception-gap · File: research/backgrounders/community-safety-perception-gap.md · Issue: community-safety-perception-gap Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Status: DRAFT · v1.0 · 2026-07-14 Data as of: 2026-07-14 (mining a merge-group spine document plus this pass's live discovery)

This is a topic where the core empirical finding (a real perception gap) is well-evidenced, but where the comparison figures on the "actual safety" side of that gap are themselves inconsistently reported across otherwise-credible sources — a genuine data-quality problem this brief states plainly rather than smoothing over. FOR and AGAINST below represent the strongest institutional case that the gap reflects a real communication/data problem worth fixing, and the strongest case for caution about acting on any single perception survey or trend figure, respectively.

TL;DR

A December 2025 survey by Liaison Strategies, a Canadian polling firm, found 76% of Toronto residents believe crime in Canada has increased over the past year, and 72% believe homicides specifically are rising in Toronto — even as multiple sources report Toronto's actual 2025 homicide count and auto-theft rate both declined substantially (exact figures vary by source: homicides reported as either 39 or 42 for the year, auto-theft declines reported between roughly 24% and 34%). The same pattern holds in neighbouring Peel and York Regions, where residents perceive rising homicide rates despite regionally-reported declines of similar or larger magnitude. Separately, Toronto's Community Crisis Service (TCCS), a non-police crisis-response program, was found in its original 2022 third-party pilot evaluation to divert 78% of relevant 911 calls without police involvement — a figure still being cited for the program's much larger current scale, without confirmation this pass could independently verify that a comparable evaluation has been repeated as the program grew roughly tenfold in reported budget.

FOR (the strongest case that this is a real, addressable data/communication problem)

  • The perception-gap finding itself rests on a directly and independently fetched, dated, methodologically transparent survey (IVR polling, named margin of error, named firm with stated election-prediction accuracy credentials) — not a vague impression or an unsourced claim.
  • The gap is not unique to Toronto within this leaf's own data: Peel and York Region residents show the same pattern, with Peel Regional Police reporting only 10 homicide occurrences through October 2025 against 19 for all of 2024, while a majority of Brampton and Mississauga residents still perceived homicides as rising — suggesting a regional, not Toronto-specific, phenomenon that a purely local explanation would not fully account for.
  • The City's own public-facing safety-ranking citation page was found, on direct fetch, to display nine-year-old data (2017 rankings) rather than any current figure — a concrete, fixable example of exactly the kind of stale public communication that could plausibly contribute to residents lacking access to current, authoritative safety data.
  • TCCS's original evaluation used a genuinely independent, credible third-party evaluator (CAMH-affiliated), which is a stronger methodological standard than the self-reported program metrics documented in this repo's sibling gun-gang-youth-violence-prevention backgrounder — suggesting the City has already demonstrated it can commission credible, independent safety-program evaluation when it chooses to.

AGAINST (the strongest case for caution before acting on any single figure)

  • This leaf's own discovery could not resolve the exact 2025 Toronto homicide count (conflicting reports of 39 and 42) or the exact auto-theft decline percentage (conflicting reports between roughly 24% and 34%) — meaning the "actual safety" side of the perception-gap comparison is itself not settled with the precision the survey's framing implies, a limitation any policy response should account for rather than treat the specific percentages as fixed facts.
  • The commonly-cited Economist Safe Cities Index ranking for Toronto is genuinely unclear from this pass's discovery: the City's own citation page is nine years stale, and a search-surfaced alternative figure (Toronto 6th globally, "safest in North America") is internally inconsistent with a separately-cited Calgary 5th-place figure in the same result — this specific comparator should not be treated as settled without a direct fetch of the EIU's own current report.
  • TCCS's headline 78% diversion-rate figure is confirmed only for a specific, much smaller six-month pilot window in 2022 — its persistence in more recent reporting, at a program roughly ten times larger in budget and newly expanding into transit environments the original evaluation did not cover, has not been independently re-confirmed in this leaf's sources, and should not be assumed automatically transferable to the current, much larger program.
  • No source in this leaf's discovery independently analyzes why the perception gap exists in the Toronto-area context specifically (media coverage patterns, social-media amplification, visible-disorder effects, or genuine localized experience of specific incidents) — the survey documents the gap's existence and scale convincingly, but this brief does not have independently verified causal evidence to draw on, and any policy response premised on a specific causal story about why the gap exists would be reasoning ahead of this leaf's actual evidence base.

Costs

No source in this leaf's discovery provides a specific cost figure for conducting or commissioning safety-perception polling at the scale of the Liaison Strategies survey. TCCS's own reported budget figures ($31.7 million 2025, $35.3 million proposed 2026) are carried at VERIFY status, not independently fetched from a primary City budget document this pass. The original 2022 TCCS pilot evaluation's own specific cost was not located in any source this pass.

Precedents

  • TCCS's original 2022 third-party pilot evaluation: a credible, independently-commissioned evaluation model, confirmed directly from the City's own primary release. Outcome: confirmed for its original scope (six months, four pilot areas); not independently re-confirmed at the program's current, much larger scale.
  • The Economist Intelligence Unit's Safe Cities Index: an international, standardized comparator ranking mechanism across 60 major cities. Outcome: methodology confirmed via a 2017 archival fetch; current (2024-era) Toronto rank not independently confirmed this pass, flagged rather than assumed.
  • CAHOOTS (Eugene, Oregon), commonly cited as an originating model for the "right responder" concept: named as a real, checkable comparator program, but not independently fetched or evaluated this pass.

Upward Ask

No formal Toronto City Council resolution specifically demanding a standing, current public safety-data dashboard, or a recurring independent TCCS re-evaluation at current scale, was identified in this leaf's sources — a genuine gap, not a claim no such ask exists. Both of this leaf's own recommendation cards (research/cards/community-safety-perception-gap.md) propose such asks as municipal-authority actions rather than upward asks to another government level, since this leaf's sources show both actions falling within existing City authority.

Bottom line

A real, independently confirmed gap exists between Toronto-area residents' perception of rising crime and multiple, if imprecisely reconciled, reports of substantial 2025 declines in homicide and auto theft — a pattern that replicates across neighbouring Peel and York Regions and is therefore unlikely to be a purely Toronto-specific artifact; at the same time, the City's own public safety-data communication (an EIU ranking citation page displaying nine-year-old data) and this leaf's own inability to pin down a single authoritative current homicide or auto-theft figure across otherwise-credible sources suggest the "actual safety" side of this comparison deserves the same scrutiny and clarity the perception side has already received from independent polling.

Uncertainties

  • ⚠️ VERIFY: Toronto's exact 2025 homicide count (39 vs. 42 across sources) and exact auto-theft decline percentage (roughly 24%–34% across sources) — not independently resolved from the Toronto Police Service's own primary data portal this pass.
  • ⚠️ VERIFY: Toronto's current (2024 or later) Economist Safe Cities Index rank — the City's own citation page is stale (2017 data), and a search-surfaced alternative figure is internally inconsistent.
  • ⚠️ VERIFY: TCCS's current-era (2025–2026) budget, staffing, and diversion-rate figures, and whether any evaluation more recent than the original 2022–2023 pilot report exists.
  • Genuinely unresolved: why the perception gap exists in the Toronto-area context specifically — no causal analysis was independently verified in this leaf's sources.
  • This brief does not cover policing budgets/staffing (community-safety-crime-policing), complaint/oversight questions (police-accountability-oversight), or gun/gang violence-prevention program funding specifically (gun-gang-youth-violence-prevention), though the homicide/shooting figures discussed here are adjacent to that leaf's own territory and are cited here only for the perception-gap comparison.
