Family Doctors and Primary Care — Toronto

DRAFT Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; CD and CSD codes coincide, Statistics Canada geographic code 3520005/3520) · Data as of: 2026-07-14 (Ontario Primary Care Action Plan January 2026 progress update; OCFP 2023-2024 releases; October 2025 University of Toronto reporting)

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Data as of: 2026-07-14 (Ontario Primary Care Action Plan January 2026 progress update; OCFP 2023-2024 releases; October 2025 University of Toronto reporting) Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; CD and CSD codes coincide, Statistics Canada geographic code 3520005/3520) · Provenance: carried-forward (carried forward from this page’s own sources master briefing) + New load-bearing findings (this review's source quote findings, cited NEW-2026-#, pending formal formal registration)

TL;DR

Ontario's own updated attachment methodology puts approximately 1.98 million people province-wide without a primary-care provider as of June 2025 — lower than the widely-cited 2.3-2.5 million figures and far below the 4.4 million "1 in 4 by 2026" forecast, which has not materialized [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2]. More than 516,000 Torontonians were without a family doctor as of a 2024 OCFP release, forecast to approach 1 million by 2026 [NEW-2026-3]; team-based primary-care access in parts of the GTA, including Scarborough, has been documented as low as ~6%, against a provincial average where nearly 75% of patients lack team-based support [NEW-2026-4]. The Ontario government's Primary Care Action Plan is a real, currently-active $2.1-billion-plus provincial program (rising toward $3.4 billion with 2026 Budget additions) that had already attached roughly 275,000 people to care in the first nine months of 2025, on track to meet or exceed its 300,000-person 2025-26 target [NEW-2026-6, NEW-2026-7]. Primary care itself is provincially funded and regulated; the City of Toronto's own levers are indirect — land/space for clinics, advocacy, and integration with municipal services.

FOR (the provincial program is real and Toronto has concrete, documented levers)

  • The Ontario Primary Care Action Plan is not aspirational — it is a funded, currently-operating program with independently reported, dated results: 78 new/expanded primary care teams already serving 490,000+ people as of the 2024 tranche, and ~275,000 additional people attached January-September 2025 [NEW-2026-6].
  • A concrete, currently-operating Toronto-area case study already demonstrates the hospital/academic-partnership model the master briefing recommends generally: Humber River Health's Schulich Family Medicine Teaching Unit is on track to serve 10,000 previously-unattached northwest-Toronto residents by 2026 [NEW-2026-5].
  • The Scarborough team-based-care figure this page’s master briefing had flagged as unconfirmed ([confirm]) is now directly source-confirmed to a named OCFP release citing INSPIRE-PHC data [NEW-2026-4] — a genuine strengthening of this page’s evidentiary basis, not a new finding invalidating the old one.
  • The province's own Primary Care Action Plan funding-targeting criteria already explicitly prioritize "the highest number of people not attached to primary care" by postal code [NEW-2026-6] — a structural opening for Toronto-specific advocacy naming Scarborough and northwest Toronto by name, since both are independently documented as high-need.

AGAINST (the gap remains large, its exact size is contested, and Toronto-specific confirmation is thin)

  • Even on the province's own newest, smaller figure, 1.98 million Ontarians remained unattached as of June 2025 [NEW-2026-1] — a large, unresolved gap regardless of which of the three circulating province-wide figures (1.98M, 2.3-2.5M, 4.4M-projected) is used.
  • No source located in this review provides a 2025-2026-dated, methodology-updated unattached-patient count specifically for Toronto — the ~1 million figure remains a 2024 OCFP forecast, not a directly re-confirmed current number.
  • No source located in this review confirms whether Scarborough or any other identified under-served Toronto neighbourhood is actually among the Primary Care Action Plan's funded 2025-26 or 2026-27 sites, despite the Plan's own targeting criteria making this plausible.
  • No City of Toronto-specific budget line, council motion, or land/space commitment supporting Community Health Centres or team-based clinics was located in this review — the master briefing's own recommended City lever (using municipal land/space) does not yet have a confirmed, named instance in the sources reviewed, distinct from the Humber River Health case study, which is a hospital/university partnership, not a City of Toronto program.

Both sides draw on real, cited figures from named primary sources; the AGAINST side leans more heavily on genuine data gaps this review's live-discovery could not close (Toronto-specific current figures, confirmed City-level action), while the FOR side leans more heavily on provincial program evidence and one concrete non-municipal Toronto case study — stated here as the honest shape of the asymmetry rather than smoothed over.

Precedents

  • Ontario Primary Care Action Plan (province-wide, 2025-): the leading current precedent for team-based-care expansion at scale, with independently reported attachment results already exceeding early targets [NEW-2026-6] — cited here as an outcome claim (real, dated results), not a bare "the province is doing something."
  • Humber River Health / Schulich Family Medicine Teaching Unit (northwest Toronto, 2023-): a real, named, currently-operating hospital-embedded referral-and-training model targeting 10,000 previously-unattached local patients by 2026 [NEW-2026-5] — the closest Toronto-specific precedent located in this review, though it is a hospital/university partnership, not a City of Toronto program.
  • The Netherlands (structural comparator, not a Toronto precedent): near-universal GP registration and gatekeeping, offered in the backgrounder's International context section as a design-principle comparator for Ontario's own 2029 universal-attachment goal, not as a directly transplantable program.

Bottom line

Ontario's family-doctor shortage is real, large, and only partially closing — even the province's own newest, smaller count leaves nearly 2 million people unattached — but it is also the subject of a genuinely substantial, currently-active provincial funding program with independently verified early results, not merely a promised future fix. Toronto and its under-served neighbourhoods, especially Scarborough and northwest Toronto, are independently documented as facing worse-than-average gaps, and at least one concrete, non-municipal Toronto-area model (Humber River Health's hospital-embedded clinic) is already demonstrating what a targeted local fix looks like at meaningful scale. What remains genuinely unconfirmed is whether the province's own funding is reaching Toronto's specific highest-need areas, and whether the City of Toronto has taken any of the concrete space/advocacy actions its own inherited briefing recommends — both real, checkable gaps this brief flags rather than assumes either way.

Uncertainties

  • Ontario's own unattached-patient count changed methodology between 2022-2023 (OCFP figures, ~2.3-2.5 million) and June 2025 (province's own INSPIRE-PHC-informed figure, ~1.98 million); the apparent improvement partly reflects counting-method changes, not only real attachment gains — not fully disentangled by any source reviewed in this review.
  • No 2025-2026-dated, methodology-updated unattached-patient count specific to Toronto was located; the ~1 million figure remains a 2024 forecast.
  • No source confirms or denies whether Scarborough or other identified Toronto under-served neighbourhoods are among the Primary Care Action Plan's actually-funded sites.
  • No City of Toronto-specific budget, motion, or land-use commitment on this issue was located in this review; this brief does not assert the City has taken no action, only that no source reviewed confirms specific action.
  • All NEW-2026-# findings in this brief are directly quoted from primary sources (except NEW-2026-7, NEW-2026-8, and NEW-2026-10, which are flagged in the backgrounder as search-summarized rather than directly primary-source-quoted, and carry correspondingly lower confidence) but have not yet been run through this project's formal claim-verification and registration process — treat as ⚠️ still-being-checked pending that pass.
