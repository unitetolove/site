Housing First: Evidence Base and Adoption Case — Toronto

DRAFT v1.1 Jurisdiction: Toronto, Ontario (single-tier city; CD/CSD codes coincide) · Data as of: 2026-07-13 (newest cited source: City of Toronto Winter Services Plan release, 2025-11-07)

Backgrounder: homelessness-housing-first-model (our research file for that page) · Data as of: 2026-07-13 (newest cited source: City of Toronto Winter Services Plan release, 2025-11-07) Jurisdiction: Toronto, Ontario (single-tier city; CD/CSD codes coincide) · Status: DRAFT v1.1 Provenance: carried-forward (this page’s carried-forward master briefing) and NEW (2026-07-13 live discovery, source quotes in the backgrounder).

Voice note: Toronto already paid for the answers — this work reads it all, integrates it, and shows the work, including the uncertainties.

TL;DR

Toronto has officially adopted Housing First as its stated philosophy but funds a shelter-dominant system: 78% of shelter users meet TSSS's own chronic-homelessness threshold, and the system produced 3,808 housing exits in 11 months of 2024 against a 12,000+ nightly census [this library's homelessness master synthesis]. The October 2025 point-in-time count showed a 21% decrease to approximately 12,180, but this page’s own inherited source attributes that decrease almost entirely to a falling refugee-claimant population, not to improvement in chronic, long-term homelessness, which "has not improved" on the same account [this library's prior executive synthesis]. The City's own November 2025 Winter Services Plan documents nearly 9,000 people nightly in the shelter system, about 330 encampments (down from 500+ a year earlier), and 3,800 shelter-to-permanent-housing moves in 2025 so far — "at risk without stable funding agreements between all three orders of government" in the City's own words [City of Toronto, 2025-11-07 release].

FOR

  • The world's largest Housing First randomized controlled trial ran partly in Toronto: the At Home/Chez Soi trial's Toronto arm enrolled 575 participants (MAP Centre, Unity Health Toronto), with the full trial's high-needs arm (~950 participants) showing 73% housed stably versus 31% under treatment-as-usual [this library's homelessness master synthesis; backgrounder "The decisive Canadian trial"]. [confirm exact figures against the primary MHCC report — flagged in this page’s own master briefing, not independently re-verified this review.]
  • Toronto's own Auditor General found emergency shelters cost 3x more than supportive housing and 7–10x more than subsidized housing or rental subsidies — a Toronto-specific, not merely trial-derived, cost argument for Housing First [this library's homelessness master synthesis].
  • Toronto is a Built for Zero Canada member community with an existing coordinated-access tool (STARS Common Assessment Tool) already referenced in BFZ Canada's national resources — meaning the infrastructure Medicine Hat used to reach functional zero is not being built from scratch here [this library's homelessness master synthesis].
  • Toronto's own 2020 Auditor General audit found that improving TCHC vacancy management by 50% would house approximately 2,200 more people and recover ~$7 million/year in currently wasted vacancy losses, with no new construction or program required [this library's homelessness master synthesis, REC 0] — see that page's recommendation cards a recommendation card for the full card.

AGAINST

  • Toronto's supportive- and affordable-housing supply is, on this page’s own framing, the binding constraint: "you cannot do Housing First without housing," and Toronto has a severe shortage of both [the inherited master briefing's §The strongest case AGAINST; backgrounder "Key tensions / tradeoffs"].
  • The City's own Homelessness Services Capital Infrastructure Strategy (HSCIS) — its primary current capital response — costs $421,875 per partitioned sleeping space versus $309,000 per private unit with kitchen and bathroom under the City's own Modular Housing Initiative, a 36% premium for an inferior product, even though the City separately frames HSCIS as saving up to $127/bed-night versus shelter-hotels [this library's homelessness master synthesis; City of Toronto, 2024-12-11 release]. Both figures are cited; this brief does not resolve which comparison should govern.
  • The Canada-Ontario Housing Benefit (COHB), which is provincially controlled, was effectively suspended April–October 2024 during a federal-provincial funding dispute, correlating with a 26.7% drop in housing exits from shelter that year (5,927 to 4,344) [this library's homelessness master synthesis] — a documented instance of a non-municipal funding lever directly constraining Toronto's own Housing First exit rate.
  • Two independent precedents this page cites as proof Housing First scales — Finland nationally and Medicine Hat, Alberta at city scale — both later reversed for structurally similar reasons (rents/housing costs outpacing sustained investment), a caution against treating scale-up as a one-time fix rather than a continuously-funded commitment [backgrounder "National-scale precedent: Finland," "City-scale precedent: Medicine Hat"].

Symmetry note: both sides draw on multiple independent sources; this is not assessed as a lopsided evidence base on either side.

Precedents

  • Houston, Texas: cut homelessness 63% since 2011 (roughly 8,500–8,950 to 3,325 by 2025) via Housing First and Coordinated Entry, achieved without additional municipal spending beyond existing federal allocations, with 90% of the 36,000+ people housed since 2012 retaining housing 2+ years [this library's homelessness master synthesis].
  • Medicine Hat, Alberta: achieved Canada's first functional-zero chronic homelessness on June 2, 2021, then lost that status by late 2021/early 2022 due to rising non-chronic homelessness from the local affordable-housing shortage — explicitly framed by this page’s sources as a housing-supply failure, not a Housing First model failure [this library's homelessness master synthesis].
  • Finland (national): cut shelter use 76% between 2008 and 2017, then saw long-term homelessness rise 29% in 2025 alone (to 1,306 individuals), confirmed directly against Varke's and Y-Säätiö's own primary statistics (corrected 2026-07-13 from a prior single-secondary-source citation), after cuts to housing-advice and social-security budgets [this library's homelessness master synthesis; Varke, "Homeless people 2025"; Y-Säätiö, "Record Rise in Homelessness in Finland in 2025"; both accessed 2026-07-13].
  • London, Ontario: has already achieved functional zero for veteran homelessness specifically, within the same Built for Zero Canada network Toronto belongs to [this library's homelessness master synthesis].

Because non-municipal levels control significant levers here — the COHB (provincial) and Reaching Home/federal housing-investment transfers — this section records what has already been formally identified as a demand in this page’s sources, not a new call to action: this page’s own master briefing names a specific set of provincial accountability demands (REC 16: increase OW to $1,200/month indexed to inflation; increase ODSP to close the $11,503 poverty gap; reinstate rent control for post-2018 units; enact a provincial no-discharge-to-homelessness mandate; legislate a provincial chronic-homelessness target with accountability) [this library's homelessness master synthesis, REC 16]. this library's municipal-asks table has not yet been checked against this specific jurisdiction/issue combination this review — flagged as an open item rather than asserted as empty or populated.

Bottom line

Toronto has the evidence — it ran part of the RCT that proved Housing First works, and its own Auditor General has independently confirmed the cost case — but has adopted Housing First as stated philosophy without funding it as the system's operating default, and the City's own most recent data (November 2025) states plainly that recent gains are "at risk without stable funding agreements between all three orders of government" [City of Toronto, 2025-11-07 release]. This is the single most defensible synthesis sentence this brief can state without exceeding a claim_type:recommendation framing it does not have the structure to carry here.

Uncertainties

  • The At Home/Chez Soi headline 73%/31% figures carry an unresolved [confirm] flag in this page’s own master briefing.
  • No L3-normalized, SGC-keyed fiscal data rows exist yet for this issue in this library's Toronto data layer — all cost figures above are backgrounder-inherited primary-source figures, not committed L3 CSV rows.
  • Whether Toronto's October 2025 PiT decrease is durable or will reverse if refugee-system pressure changes again is not addressed in either inherited source — flagged genuinely uncovered in the backgrounder.
  • The Medicine Hat 2025 governance transition (Community-Based Organization role change) was surfaced in this run's discovery but not captured with an exact quote or primary source — flagged, not asserted.
