The Hospital Discharge-to-Homelessness Cost Cycle — Toronto

DRAFT Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; Statistics Canada geographic code 3520005/3520), with national CIHI and provincial Ontario cohort-study context · Data as of: 2026-07-14

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 Backgrounder: our research file for that page · Data as of: 2026-07-14 Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; Statistics Canada geographic code 3520005/3520), with national CIHI and provincial Ontario cohort-study context · Provenance: carried-forward (carried forward from this page’s own sources document) + New load-bearing findings (this review's live-fetch findings)

TL;DR

People experiencing homelessness cost more than double the national average per hospitalization ($16,785 vs. $7,803, CIHI, 2022-2023) and are readmitted after psychiatric discharge at nearly double the rate of non-homeless patients (17.1% vs. 9.8% within 30 days) — figures independently confirmed via direct fetch of CIHI's own live data page in this review [NEW-HDC-1]. St. Michael's Hospital's Navigator Program, Toronto's highest-volume site for this population, has since expanded to a second confirmed Canadian site (St. Paul's Hospital, Vancouver) and a third planned (Montreal), together serving over 1,000 patients — a genuinely new, national-scale finding this page’s own June 2026 sources predate [NEW-HDC-4]. The underlying systemic driver, cited consistently with this project's own homelessness-population-specific-pathways leaf, remains unaddressed: "there is no provincial strategy for discharging people experiencing homelessness from acute care health settings" in Ontario [NEW-HDC-5].

FOR (a real, replicable, cost-effective intervention model exists)

  • CIHI's own national data confirms the underlying problem is real, quantified, and CIHI's own conclusion, not this project's inference: 93% of homeless-coded hospitalizations are admitted via emergency department, which CIHI itself states "suggest[s] inadequate access to primary care" [NEW-HDC-1].
  • The Navigator Program's core mechanism — an embedded, non-clinical Homeless Outreach Counsellor working with patients through discharge and follow-up — is independently confirmed via direct fetch of the MAP Centre for Urban Health Solutions' own program page, and has demonstrated real cross-jurisdictional replication: over 1,000 patients served across Toronto and Vancouver sites combined, with a Montreal launch confirmed as planned [NEW-HDC-3, NEW-HDC-4].
  • An NIHR-funded English economic evaluation independently corroborates the same underlying mechanism internationally: a housing-led, non-clinical step-down model was far more cost-effective (GBP 4,743 per QALY) than clinically-led alternatives (GBP 55,602), and cost-saving overall including service delivery costs.
  • CIHI's own March 2025 coding-guidance refinement — restricting Z59.0 coding to current homelessness only — shows active federal-level attention to improving the underlying data quality this whole leaf depends on [NEW-HDC-2].

AGAINST (the underlying policy gap has not closed, and the model's limits are real)

  • No provincial discharge strategy exists in Ontario, per a peer-reviewed Canadian analysis this project cites consistently across two leaves: "publicly available hospital policies or guidelines tailored to people experiencing homelessness are difficult to find" (direct-fetch confirmed quote). A companion Toronto-specific study (Jenkinson et al. 2021) attributes inadequate discharges to hospitals' efficiency/accountability pressures and shelters' own exclusion/eligibility criteria combining to leave people with nowhere to go — paraphrase, not verbatim quote, per independently adjudicated 2026-07-17, by a recorded judgment ruling [NEW-HDC-5].
  • The Navigator Program's own expansion, while real, has occurred at only one additional Canadian site (Vancouver) plus a planned third (Montreal) — no second Toronto hospital site has adopted the model despite Toronto's own documented concentration of homelessness-linked hospital use, per this review's live-discovery findings.
  • Whether the Navigator Program's own documented success factor (non-clinical staff with lived homelessness experience) generalizes to its new sites is not addressed by any source located in this review — expansion evidences a replication attempt, not confirmed outcome parity across sites.
  • CIHI's most recent available cost data remains 2022-2023, generated under a looser coding standard than now applies (per the March 2025 tightening) — meaning any future comparison of hospitalization trends needs to account for this definitional change rather than reading a future count shift as a pure trend.
  • The NIHR cost-effectiveness figures are England-specific and not directly transferable to an Ontario budget question; no Toronto- or Ontario-specific costed estimate exists for a provincially-scaled discharge-intervention program.

Both sides draw on the same directly-fetched primary sources; this brief states the asymmetry (the FOR case is strongest on the intervention model's own demonstrated replication and international corroboration, the AGAINST case is strongest on the persisting systemic policy gap the model exists to compensate for) without resolving whether site-level replication is an adequate substitute for provincial policy.

Precedents

England's NIHR-funded discharge/intermediate-care economic evaluation is the page’s strongest international precedent, with a quantified, independently-reviewable cost-effectiveness comparison across three service models. Within Canada, the Navigator Program's own expansion (Toronto → Vancouver → planned Montreal) functions as a domestic precedent for cross-jurisdictional replication of the same underlying model, independently confirmed in this review [NEW-HDC-4]. This document does not identify a non-Anglophone or non-Commonwealth international comparator with independently verified outcome figures within this review's scope.

Upward Ask

No this library's issue index row maps directly onto this page; the nearest related row (C2★, homelessness-encampments) names provincial and federal funding programs not specific to hospital discharge policy. This page’s own evidence names the Ministry of Health, not the Homelessness Prevention Program or Reaching Home, as the relevant non-municipal authority for a discharge-policy response — a distinct upward-ask target from this project's other Domain H leaves. No City council motion or formal upward ask specific to hospital discharge policy was identified in this review; a recommendation card's proposed Ministry of Health discharge-policy publication is framed as a new ask this project has not found evidence has already been formally made.

Bottom line

The hospital discharge-to-homelessness cycle is a well-quantified, genuinely preventable cost pattern with a real, internationally-corroborated, and now cross-Canada-replicating intervention model (Navigator) — but that model's own replication has occurred entirely outside Toronto since its origin there, and the underlying provincial policy gap the model exists to compensate for (no Ontario discharge strategy for homelessness specifically) remains open and unaddressed by any source reviewed in this review.

Uncertainties

  • CIHI's most recent hospitalization-cost data remains 2022-2023, predating the March 2025 coding-guidance tightening; no newer dataset exists to test whether the tightened definition changes the count or cost picture.
  • The "no provincial strategy" finding and its companion Toronto-specific qualitative-study quotes remain ⚠️ still being checked, inherited from homelessness-population-specific-pathways's own disclosed access limitation (a bot-check wall blocked direct primary-source fetch in that pass, not independently resolved in this review either).
  • Whether the Navigator Program's core facilitator (non-clinical staff with lived homelessness experience) generalizes across its now-multiple sites is not addressed by any source located in this review.
  • No Toronto- or Ontario-specific costed estimate exists for a provincially-scaled discharge-intervention program; the page’s own carried-forward documents flags this as absent, and this review did not close it.
  • No Cui Bono beneficiary was identified for this page in this review — a genuine empty result, since no source reviewed names an identifiable party profiting from discharge-to-homelessness persisting, distinct from the shelter-operator-contract Cui Bono category this project documents elsewhere.
