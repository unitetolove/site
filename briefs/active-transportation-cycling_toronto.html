Active Transportation and Cycling — Toronto

DRAFT Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005 (CSD), 3520 (CD — single-tier city, both codes identify the same municipality) · Issue: active-transportation-cycling · Data as of: 2026-07-14 (live-discovery findings) / 2026-07-12 (formally registered claims)

Backgrounder: active-transportation-cycling · Issue: active-transportation-cycling Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005 (CSD), 3520 (CD — single-tier city, both codes identify the same municipality) Version: v1.0 · Date: 2026-07-14 · Status: DRAFT Data as of: 2026-07-14 (live-discovery findings) / 2026-07-12 (formally registered claims)

TL;DR

Toronto's cycling network has grown to roughly 230 of a planned 500 kilometres of Major City-Wide Cycling Routes as of end-2024, with 100 more committed for 2025-2027 [backgrounder CL-0121, CL-0122]. The network's three most politically contested corridors — Bloor, Yonge, and University Avenue bike lanes — remain in place under a court injunction while Ontario's Court of Appeal considers the province's appeal of a Superior Court ruling that found the province's own internal evidence undermined its stated rationale for removing them [backgrounder NEW-5]. Toronto continues expanding the network elsewhere during this litigation window and has calculated that removing the three litigated corridors would itself cost the City nearly $50 million [backgrounder NEW-2].

FOR (the case for continued cycling-network investment)

  • Toronto's cycling network has grown steadily and continues to under a committed, funded multi-year plan: 230 km complete of a 500 km target, with 100 km more committed for 2025-2027, on top of a record $30 million single-year 2023 investment [backgrounder CL-0121, CL-0122, CL-80309].
  • Independent peer-reviewed research on Toronto specifically finds protected cycle tracks produce a measurable safety benefit both on the treated corridor and on nearby streets (a "safety halo" extending 151-550 metres) [backgrounder NEW-4].
  • A court found, based on the Ontario government's own internal Ministry of Transportation briefing, that the Ministry "does not hold any data on the performance of the identified three bike lanes to support a decision for removal" and that removal "may not have the desired goal of reducing congestion" [backgrounder NEW-5, direct quote] — the strongest form of evidence available in this project's framework, since it is the government's own words, disclosed through litigation, not an advocate's characterization.
  • The claim that University Avenue's bike lanes delay emergency response has not, per available public evidence, been corroborated by emergency services themselves; the City's own Auditor General instead attributes rising paramedic response times to hospital off-load delays and staffing shortages, and Sinai Health's own CEO wrote in support of the corridor's protected bike lanes in 2020 [backgrounder NEW-3].

AGAINST (counter-evidence / limits)

  • The Ontario government maintains its position on appeal and has not accepted the Superior Court's findings; a three-judge Court of Appeal panel heard the case January 28, 2026, with a decision still pending as of this brief's date, meaning the ultimate legal status of the three corridors remains genuinely unresolved [backgrounder NEW-5, NEW-6].
  • Ontario's Premier's public response to the original ruling — "they elect parties to make decisions – they don't elect judges" — signals the Province may not accept an adverse Court of Appeal outcome either, with legal commentary flagging the notwithstanding clause as a live possibility [backgrounder NEW-5].
  • Toronto's own bike-lane construction costs vary substantially by project (roughly $1.48 million/km to $3.75 million/km for two contemporaneous late-2025 proposals), and this review did not establish what specifically drives that 2.5x variance [backgrounder NEW-2].
  • No comprehensive Toronto-specific cost-benefit study of cycling infrastructure investment was located in this review — the evidentiary gap the court found on the Province's side has a real counterpart on the City's own side, where scattered project-level costs and a single removal-cost estimate stand in for a fuller accounting [backgrounder, Key tensions].

Symmetry note: both sides draw on four points; the FOR side rests more heavily on documented, court-tested evidence (the government's own admitted data gap), while the AGAINST side is weighted toward genuine unresolved uncertainty (the pending appeal, cost-variance, and evidence-gap questions) rather than an equally strong counter-case — this asymmetry reflects the state of the evidence located in this review, not an editorial choice, and is flagged rather than manufactured into false balance.

Precedents

  • Copenhagen, Denmark. Roughly 49% cycling mode share for work/school commutes, over 350 km of segregated cycle tracks, and a reported near-800M investment return on a ~300M outlay [backgrounder, International context] — the specific cost-benefit framing Toronto currently lacks a local equivalent of.
  • Amsterdam, Netherlands. 38% citywide cycling mode share, 500+ km of bike lanes, many physically protected, with protected-intersection designs giving cyclists signal priority [backgrounder, International context].
  • Toronto's own cycle-track safety record. Independent, Toronto-specific peer-reviewed research already documents a measurable safety benefit from protected infrastructure, including an area-wide "safety halo" effect [backgrounder NEW-4] — a real, local precedent for the safety case, distinct from the international mode-share comparators above.
  • Mississauga (179 km, 2017-2024) and Ottawa (2013 Cycling Plan, 8% morning-peak target by 2031). Both peer Ontario municipalities show comparable, ongoing multi-year cycling investment programs [CL-0126, CL-80311], indicating Toronto's approach is not an outlier among large Ontario cities.

Upward Ask

this library's issue index's Owner column for the adjacent mobility-congestion-transit slug names provincial authority over some mobility levers; this page’s own scope (cycling infrastructure specifically) sits primarily within municipal authority except where Bill 212 imposes a provincial approval requirement on new bike lanes removing a traffic lane, per the sibling mobility-congestion-transit backgrounder.

  • A formal, court-tested position already exists on one side of this issue, unusually for this project's Upward Ask sections: Cycle Toronto's Charter challenge produced a binding Superior Court ruling (under appeal) that the Province's own internal evidence contradicted its stated removal rationale [backgrounder NEW-5] — this is a stronger form of formal contestation than a Council resolution alone would represent, though it was brought by a civil-society advocacy organization and individual applicants, not by City Council itself.
  • No confirmed formal Council resolution consolidating the City's own position was located in this review covering both possible Court of Appeal outcomes (see a recommendation card, which proposes exactly this).

Bottom line

For Toronto, the single most defensible synthesis sentence: the City has continued building a genuinely growing cycling network with real, Toronto-specific safety evidence behind it, while its three most contested corridors remain legally protected only by an injunction pending an appeal whose outcome — and whose ultimate political resolution, given the Premier's public comments — remains unresolved as of this brief's date.

Uncertainties

  • The Ontario Court of Appeal's ruling on Bill 212 remained pending as of this brief's date; the ultimate legal status of the Bloor, Yonge, and University Avenue bike lanes is genuinely unresolved [backgrounder NEW-5, NEW-6].
  • Whether the Province would invoke the notwithstanding clause if it loses the appeal is speculative, flagged by legal commentary as a live possibility, not a confirmed intention [backgrounder NEW-5].
  • What specifically drives the 2.5x per-kilometre cost variance between recent Toronto bike-lane proposals is not established in this review [backgrounder NEW-2].
  • No comprehensive Toronto-specific cost-benefit study of cycling infrastructure was located; the Copenhagen ROI comparator's own methodology was not independently verified in this review [backgrounder, International context, ⚠️ still being checked].
  • 6 of 13 formally registered claims underlying this brief remain marked “still being checked”, not verified.
