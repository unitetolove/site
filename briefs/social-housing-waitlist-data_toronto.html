Social Housing Waitlist Data — Toronto

DRAFT — v1.1, 2026-07-13 Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; CD and CSD codes coincide) · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Data as of: 2026-07-13 (Q1 2026, the most current published quarter)

File: research/briefs/social-housing-waitlist-data_toronto.md · Status: DRAFT — v1.1, 2026-07-13 Backgrounder: research/backgrounders/social-housing-waitlist-data.md · Data as of: 2026-07-13 (Q1 2026, the most current published quarter) Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; CD and CSD codes coincide) · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Trust classes: INHERITED (from this leaf's promoted docs) and NEW (this session's direct primary-source fetch, 2026-07-13) — see backgrounder for full quote-context on each figure below.

This leaf's entire scope is Toronto's own centralized waitlist — there is no separate "localize a national/provincial figure" step here the way there is for issues drawn from research/issue-map.md; the backgrounder and this brief describe the same jurisdiction throughout.

TL;DR

Toronto's centralized RGI waiting list grew from 81,664 households (2020) to 105,115 households (2025), and stood at 104,851 as of Q1 2026 — the most current published figure, directly reconfirmed this session [backgrounder, "The trend"]. The figure counts households/applications, not individuals, despite a widely repeated 2024-2025 press description as "people" [backgrounder, Trap 1]. In 2025, 21,538 new applications were filed against only 2,838 households actually housed — roughly 7.6 inflow for every 1 housed, a 2.7% annual exit-to-housing rate [backgrounder, "Comparison to the supply pipeline"]. A 2026 reporting-methodology change (reactivated applications folded into the active total; "Cancelled" renamed "Removed from Waiting List") means the pre-2025 and 2025-onward series are not built on an identical counting basis [backgrounder, Trap 2].

FOR

(Reading "FOR" as: evidence supporting the case that the City's current waitlist administration and reform trajectory are working as intended.)

  • The 2022-adopted queue reform (50% randomized lottery / 50% longest-wait for buildings over 10 units) was a genuine, Council-adopted acknowledgment that the prior pure-seniority system underserved urgent, recently-arrived need — framed by the City itself as a fairness correction, not an external criticism [backgrounder, "Wait times, who gets housed, and by what rule"; INHERITED].
  • Seven named priority categories (led by survivors of domestic/family abuse and trafficking, per Ontario's Special Priority Policy) already skip the general queue, and Q1 2026 data shows 444 of 750 households housed that quarter came through a named priority category rather than the general list [backgrounder; NEW, City of Toronto Q1 2026 table].
  • The City's own 2026 reporting-methodology revision (clarifying "Removed from Waiting List" and folding reactivated applications into the active total) is a genuine improvement in definitional clarity over prior years' combined "New/reactivated" line [backgrounder, Trap 2; NEW].

AGAINST

  • The list grew 29% in five years (81,664→105,115) while the annual exit-to-housing rate sits at only about 2.7%, and 2025's inflow-to-housed ratio was roughly 7.6 to 1 — a system where demand growth substantially outpaces documented throughput [backgrounder, "Comparison to the supply pipeline"; INHERITED].
  • The City's own 2019 Auditor General report found that 95% of applicants (everyone outside the 5% prioritized) were managed strictly first-come-first-served, with waits exceeding 10 years, and identified $7 million in unit-vacancy losses and an estimated 2,200 additional people housable through better utilization of existing stock alone — a documented inefficiency independent of new-unit supply [backgrounder; NEW, Toronto Auditor General 2019, fetched 2026-07-13].
  • Even in the most current quarter (Q1 2026), the ordinary "General list" queue — not any priority category — still produced the single largest housed cohort (306 of 750, 41%), a data point that can be read either as the priority-queue reform not yet having shifted outcomes as much as its framing implies, or as expected given the reform's 50/50 design [backgrounder, "Key tensions/tradeoffs"; NEW].
  • Whether the 2022-adopted lottery/portal reform is fully operational, or still a described framework, remains unconfirmed as of this session's own check (2026-07-13), matching the same unresolved status the promoted source documents found as of October 2025 [backgrounder; INHERITED + NEW, both unresolved].

Symmetry note: FOR has three points, AGAINST has four; this reflects that the evidence itself documents an open reform (some elements adopted, implementation status partly unconfirmed) alongside consistently negative throughput figures, not a deliberate imbalance in what was searched for.

Costs

No City of Toronto fiscal-cost or per-unit figures specific to waitlist administration itself were located in this leaf's committed evidence (the L3 structured-data layer for this leaf has not yet been populated with FIR/CMHC rows, unlike the more developed housing-supply-affordability leaf). The one quantified cost figure available is the City's own 2019 Auditor General estimate of $7 million in annual vacancy losses tied to unit-utilization inefficiency [backgrounder; NEW] — a cost of underuse, not a program-administration cost. Card RC-SHWD-01 and RC-SHWD-02 (research/cards/social-housing-waitlist-data.md) each give order-of-magnitude cost ranges for specific proposed actions (structured data publishing; throughput-linked funding reporting), anchored to comparators rather than a cited City program-cost figure.

Precedents

No independent, out-of-Toronto precedent cases (another Ontario municipality's centralized-list reform, or another Canadian city's RGI-waitlist practice) have been mined for this leaf yet. The leaf's own promoted documents and this session's discovery pass are both Toronto-specific in their primary sourcing; a genuine precedents section would require a dedicated Lane D/M discovery pass against another jurisdiction's waitlist administration, not attempted here per this task's scope (deepen the existing leaf, not open a new comparative-jurisdiction line of research).

Upward Ask

This leaf's scope is centralized-waitlist administration, which is squarely within existing City of Toronto authority (Access to Housing, Housing Secretariat) — the City is not waiting on the Province or the Feds to administer its own list. The waitlist's growth rate, however, is downstream of RGI/social-housing supply funding, which does implicate the Province and the Feds (per housing-supply-affordability's own Owner column, not re-derived here). research/data/municipal_asks.csv was not checked in this pass for any existing formal Toronto Council ask specifically tied to waitlist throughput or reporting; Card RC-SHWD-02 proposes such a conditioning ask, but this brief does not assert that ask has already been formally made — it should be verified against the municipal-asks tracker before being cited as an existing position rather than a card-level proposal.

Bottom line

Toronto's centralized RGI waitlist is a large, well-documented, and directly City-quotable dataset whose headline growth (81,664→105,115 households, 2020-2025) is real and independently reconfirmed against the City's own live table — but two things must accompany any citation of it: the figure counts households/applications, not individuals, and the pace of new housing placements (2.7% of the list annually) is not keeping up with the pace of new applications (7.6 for every 1 housed in 2025).

Uncertainties

  • Whether the 2022-adopted lottery/portal queue reform is now fully operational is unconfirmed, both in the promoted source (as of October 2025) and in this session's own check (as of 2026-07-13).
  • Whether "Access to Housing" and "Housing Connections" name the same function or a formally distinct predecessor entity is unresolved — secondary sources only, not independently confirmed against a primary City/TCHC document.
  • The COHB program's relationship to centralized-waitlist figures (mutually exclusive enrolment or something else) is flagged by this leaf's own promoted documents and remains unresolved here.
  • No L3 structured cost/fiscal data specific to this leaf has been committed yet; the Costs section above is thin relative to what a fully joined L3 dataset would eventually support.
  • No out-of-Toronto precedent cases have been mined for this leaf; the Precedents section above states that gap rather than filling it with an uncited "other cities have done this" claim.
