Homelessness Economic ROI — Toronto

DRAFT Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; CD and CSD codes coincide, SGC 3520005 / 3520) · Data as of: figures below trace to the backbone documents (this library's prior synthesis document (Economic ROI), dated through July 2026) plus one 2026-01-13 provincial-scale source cited in the backgrounder.

Backgrounder: homelessness-economic-roi (research/backgrounders/homelessness-economic-roi.md) · Cards: research/cards/homelessness-economic-roi.md Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; CD and CSD codes coincide, SGC 3520005 / 3520) · Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.1 · Date: 2026-07-13 Data as of: figures below trace to the backbone documents (this library's prior synthesis document (Economic ROI), dated through July 2026) plus one 2026-01-13 provincial-scale source cited in the backgrounder.

TL;DR

Toronto's own audited numbers show that sheltering a chronically homeless person costs the City $49,640/year, while housing that same person through the Housing First (ACT) model costs $6,311/year net — a documented $43,329/year difference, audited on both sides rather than modelled [backgrounder, Part 3]. Applied to Toronto's estimated 12,026 chronically homeless population, that comparison alone implies roughly $521M/year in avoidable municipal shelter cost, before counting the federal and provincial savings that would also result [backgrounder, Part 3]. The City bears the shelter cost directly; the largest federal and provincial savings land at those other orders of government, which is the structural reason this document's evidence base gives for why investment has lagged the case for it [backgrounder, "Fiscal misalignment"].

FOR

  • The $43,329/year per-person savings from housing rather than sheltering rests on two independently audited figures (Toronto AG 2025 shelter cost; Latimer et al. 2020 net Housing First cost, re-confirmed by the backbone's own audit pass including a citation-DOI correction), not a single modelled projection [backgrounder, Part 3].
  • The prevention case shows the highest ROI ratio in the entire evidence base: a $2,000–$5,000 eviction-prevention intervention averting a full year of shelter cost implies a 10–25:1 return [backgrounder, Part 3].
  • A 2026-01-13 province-wide update independently corroborates, using different data and a different geographic scale, that municipalities are absorbing a growing and disproportionate share of system cost as homelessness outpaces funding growth — the same structural pattern Toronto's own figures show [backgrounder, "New this pass"].
  • A currently uncounted cost category strengthens the case further: applying the federal government's own required Value of a Statistical Life standard to Toronto's 59 documented 2024 shelter-resident deaths implies a $578.2M/year loss — larger than any other single line item in this cost case, and not yet folded into the City's public accounting [backgrounder, "A cost line usually left out entirely"; card RC-ROI-01].

AGAINST

  • The consolidated $1.14B/year total that includes Toronto's $563M municipal line is a single-point steady-state estimate; three of its four component lines (Provincial, Federal, Productivity) are single-model (Meta-methodology) figures not independently cross-validated, and citing the total without that caveat is flagged in the source material itself as the single most likely way a hostile fact-check could damage credibility [backgrounder, "The consolidated case"].
  • The City's own non-shelter municipal savings estimate (EMS, fire, bylaw, encampment response, totalling the gap between the $521M shelter line and the $563M full municipal total) has already been revised downward once, after an 8.1× ambulance-utilization figure and a $48M/year Toronto Police mental-health-call cost were both formally removed for failing independent verification — a caution that similar figures still in the estimate warrant scrutiny before being treated as settled [backgrounder, Scope warning; Part 3].
  • A 2026-01-13 province-wide finding complicates a simple "more funding solves this" reading: total system funding grew only 32.1% against a 49.1% rise in homelessness between 2021 and 2025, and within that growth, emergency-shelter spending rose 51.6% while community-housing spending fell 0.6% — meaning that even where funding did grow, it has not obviously flowed toward the housing-first side of the ledger this ROI case rests on, at least at the province-wide scale this finding covers [backgrounder, "New this pass"; "Key tensions"].

Precedents

  • The At Home/Chez Soi trial (the basis for the 69% cost-offset figure) ran in Toronto, Montreal, Moncton, Winnipeg, and Vancouver, and is the single largest Housing First trial informing this figure — Toronto is itself one of the trial sites, not an external comparator [backgrounder, Part 3].
  • A 2025 U.S.-based mathematical-modelling study (Rao & Brandeau, JAMA Network Open), led by a University of Toronto researcher who is, per the same reporting, seeking to expand the work into Toronto, found a housing-first intervention reduced total and fatal overdoses over five years by 11% and 9% respectively in a modelled population, at an incremental cost of US$26,800 per QALY gained. This is not yet a Toronto-specific empirical result and should not be cited as one [backgrounder, "New this pass"].
  • No other Ontario or Canadian municipality's own equivalent cost-comparison exercise (shelter cost vs. Housing First net cost, independently audited on both sides) has been identified in this leaf's evidence base as a direct precedent for Toronto's own figures — this is a genuine gap.

Upward Ask

Per research/issue-map.md's Owner framing for this issue (federal IHAP/CHT/IRCC exposure; provincial OHIP, corrections, OW/COHB exposure; municipal shelter and front-line service exposure), the largest identified return on investment sits with orders of government other than the City. Two of this leaf's recommendation cards state this directly as a demand rather than an assumption already granted: card RC-ROI-02 asks the Province to fund a targeted $500/month Housing Stability Supplement (~$72M/year for ~12,000 people) rather than a full OW rate increase (~$2.28B/year) whose resulting savings would land mostly outside provincial coffers; card RC-ROI-03 asks the federal government to fund a $500M Housing First bond repaid specifically from the IHAP and Canada Health Transfer savings this leaf's own modelling attributes to Ottawa. No formal council motion or resolution embodying either ask has been identified in research/data/municipal_asks.csv (not yet populated for this issue) as already made by Toronto or any other Ontario municipality — this section states what the evidence supports asking for, not what has already been asked.

Bottom line

Toronto's own audited numbers — not a projection — show a $43,329/year cost difference between sheltering and housing a chronically homeless person, and the City bears the larger identified cost exposure of any single order of government in this leaf's evidence base ($563M/year modelled municipal savings potential) while capturing a smaller share of the federal and provincial savings the same investment would also generate. This asymmetry, independently corroborated at the provincial scale by 2026 data showing municipalities absorbing a disproportionate share of system-cost growth, is this brief's most defensible synthesis point — stated as a fact about the documented fiscal structure, not a recommendation in this brief's own voice.

Uncertainties

  • The $1.14B/year consolidated total (across all four orders of government/productivity) is a single-point estimate; three of its four components are single-model figures not independently cross-validated — see "AGAINST" above.
  • No Toronto-specific data has been found confirming or denying whether the province-wide shelter-vs-housing spending-mix skew (shelters +51.6%, community housing −0.6%, 2021–2025) also describes Toronto's own recent spending pattern specifically.
  • The mortality-monetization figure ($578.2M/year) is arithmetically sound against an official federal standard but is not yet integrated into the City's own public accounting or into this document's consolidated totals — see card RC-ROI-01.
  • No L3 committed data row (FIR fiscal data, City-specific TSSS net-cost figures) has been joined into this brief; all figures trace to the L4 backgrounder's cited sources rather than a committed local CSV.
  • The federal bond and provincial supplement asks (Upward Ask section) are recommendation-shaped syntheses from the cards, not confirmed policy positions already adopted by any government — stated as such, not as settled fact.
