Gig Economy & Precarious-Work Labour Standards — Toronto

DRAFT Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; CD and CSD codes coincide) · Data as of: 2026-07-13 (DPWRA provisions and 2026 enforcement posture; StatsCan Dec 2025 platform-work data; City of Toronto PTC licensing page, modified Feb 2026)

Status: DRAFT · Version: v1.1 · Date: 2026-07-13 Backgrounder: research/backgrounders/gig-economy-precarious-work.md · Data as of: 2026-07-13 (DPWRA provisions and 2026 enforcement posture; StatsCan Dec 2025 platform-work data; City of Toronto PTC licensing page, modified Feb 2026) Jurisdiction: Toronto (single-tier city; CD and CSD codes coincide) · Trust classes: INHERITED (leaf-promoted this leaf's inherited master briefing (gig economy precarious work), cited as-is) + NEW-LOAD-BEARING (this pass's live-discovery findings, cited NEW-2026-#, pending formal ledger promotion)

TL;DR

Ontario's Digital Platform Workers' Rights Act (DPWRA) is now in its first full year of enforcement (2026), requiring minimum wage per work assignment, tiered penalties, and a Ministry of Labour complaint process — but its own provisions still exclude unpaid logged-in waiting time from that minimum-wage guarantee [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2; master briefing-INHERITED]. No published complaint or compliance data for the Act's first enforcement year was located in this pass. Nationally, roughly 667,000 Canadians did platform work in the 12 months to December 2025, led by delivery (272,000) and personal transport (184,000) [NEW-2026-3]; Ontario had 80,000+ active Uber/Lyft drivers as of December 2024, the most recent figure available [inherited from the master briefing]. Labour standards for this workforce are entirely provincial; Toronto's only direct lever is vehicle-for-hire (PTC) licensing, which the City already uses to set training, screening, and vehicle-age conditions [NEW-2026-4].

FOR (the case that the DPWRA is real, meaningful progress)

  • The Act's obligations are concrete and enforceable regardless of a worker's ESA employee status: per-assignment minimum wage, a 24-hour information requirement, a two-weeks'-notice-plus-written-reason requirement before removing platform access, a reprisal prohibition, and a three-year record-retention duty on operators [NEW-2026-1].
  • Penalties are real and tiered, including quasi-criminal fines up to $500,000 for repeat corporate offences — not a symbolic enforcement structure [NEW-2026-1].
  • The Act closes a prior legal vacuum: before July 2025, gig worker protection in Ontario depended on individual ESA misclassification complaints, as in the 2022 Uber Eats courier case, rather than a general statutory floor [NEW-2026-5].
  • The Act applies across the whole platform-work sector "regardless of whether the worker is classified as an employee," per independent legal-compliance commentary — meaning platforms cannot contract around it through classification alone [NEW-2026-2].

AGAINST (the case that the gap this leaf's inherited briefing identified remains real and unaddressed)

  • The Act's own minimum-wage requirement is per work assignment, not per logged-in hour — confirmed directly from the Act's provisions in this pass, not just from advocacy critique [NEW-2026-1]. Workers Action Centre's critique that many gig workers still earn below minimum wage across total logged-in time remains uncontradicted by anything found in this pass [inherited from the master briefing].
  • No published Ministry of Labour data on DPWRA complaint volume, penalties issued, or compliance rates for 2025-2026 — the Act's first enforcement year — was located in this pass, despite this leaf's inherited briefing naming monitoring implementation as the critical open test [inherited from the master briefing].
  • The Act provides no path to EI, CPP, or WSIB coverage — confirmed as still absent in this pass's review of the Act's provisions [NEW-2026-1; master briefing-INHERITED].
  • British Columbia has separately legislated on app-based gig work, meaning Ontario's specific "third way" model is not becoming a national standard but one piece of a forming provincial patchwork — itself evidence the reclassification question remains contested rather than settled [NEW-2026-2].
  • Toronto's own available municipal lever — PTC licensing — governs training, screening, and vehicle standards, not wages or benefits; the City has no current authority to close the engaged-time gap directly [NEW-2026-4].

Both sides draw on real, cited facts; this brief states the asymmetry (the FOR case leans on the Act's specific enforceable text and penalty structure, the AGAINST case leans on the same text's own scope limits and the absence of first-year compliance data) without adjudicating which side the evidence favours on balance, consistent with this leaf's binding rule to state facts and jurisdiction rather than take a position on reclassification.

Precedents

The inherited master briefing documents several non-Toronto precedents directly relevant to this leaf, cited there rather than re-researched here: Spain's Riders' Law (2021, presumption of employment, upheld by Spain's Supreme Court); the UK Supreme Court's Uber BV v Aslam (2021, "worker" status entitling minimum wage, holiday pay, pension contributions); the EU Platform Work Directive (2024, presumption of employment, implementation from 2026); California's AB5/Proposition 22 (2019-2020, $225M spent by platforms to reverse reclassification via ballot initiative); and Australia's Fair Work Commission 2024 amendments extending minimum standards to "employee-like" gig workers [inherited from the master briefing]. This pass's own live discovery adds one Ontario-specific precedent not previously in this leaf: the 2022 Ontario Ministry of Labour Employment Standards Officer ruling finding a specific Uber Eats courier was owed wages under the ESA, predating and distinct from the DPWRA's later "third way" approach [NEW-2026-5]. No independently-sourced non-Ontario Canadian municipal precedent for a licensing-conditioned compliance-disclosure approach (the mechanism this leaf's RC-GIG-01 card proposes) was identified in this pass — stated as a gap rather than an absence of comparable programs elsewhere.

Upward Ask

This leaf's own jurisdiction discipline is unambiguous: gig-worker labour standards (minimum wage, engaged-time definition, EI/CPP/WSIB coverage) are entirely provincial (ESA, DPWRA) and federal (EI, CPP) matters — the City of Toronto does not control any of them directly [NEW-2026-1, NEW-2026-2; master briefing-INHERITED]. research/data/municipal_asks.csv was not checked against a row specific to this issue in this pass — a genuine gap, not confirmed either way. The clearest already-documented instance of institutional pushback on gig-worker classification in Ontario is the CUPW-backed 2022 ESA complaint that produced the Uber Eats courier ruling, and CUPW/Gig Workers United's ongoing OLRB organizing activity [NEW-2026-5, NEW-2026-6] — labour-relations and complaint action, not a City Council motion, and this brief does not present it as equivalent to a formal municipal ask. No Toronto City Council motion specifically requesting the province close the DPWRA's engaged-time gap, extend EI/CPP/WSIB to platform workers, or publish DPWRA compliance data was identified in this pass. The two cards accompanying this brief (RC-GIG-01, RC-GIG-02) are both scoped to what the City can do unilaterally through its existing PTC licensing and public-realm authority, precisely because no confirmed upward-ask precedent specific to this issue was found.

Bottom line

Toronto sits downstream of a provincial law that is real, specific, and newly enforceable — and that, by its own drafted terms, still leaves the exact gap this leaf's inherited briefing identified as its central shortfall. The City has no authority to close that gap directly; its own genuine lever, vehicle-for-hire licensing, can add visibility and infrastructure at the margins but cannot substitute for provincial reform or federal EI/CPP action.

Uncertainties

  • Whether the DPWRA's 2026 first enforcement year is producing the predicted sub-minimum-wage-on-total-logged-in-time outcome, or something different, is not addressed by any complaint/compliance data located in this pass — genuinely unknown as of 2026-07-13.
  • No 2025-2026 Ontario- or Toronto-specific platform-workforce count was located; the national StatsCan figure (667,000) and the inherited December 2024 Ontario rideshare figure (80,000+) use different scopes and are not directly comparable [NEW-2026-3; master briefing-INHERITED].
