Vacant Home Tax Effectiveness — Backgrounder — Toronto

DRAFT v1.0 Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Issue: vacant-home-tax-effectiveness · Data as of: 2026-07-14 (backgrounder mined from a spine document with its own June 2026 research date; no live-discovery refresh run this pass)

Backgrounder: vacant-home-tax-effectiveness · File: research/backgrounders/vacant-home-tax-effectiveness.md · Issue: vacant-home-tax-effectiveness Jurisdiction: Toronto · SGC code(s): 3520005, 3520 · Status: DRAFT v1.0 Data as of: 2026-07-14 (backgrounder mined from a spine document with its own June 2026 research date; no live-discovery refresh run this pass)

Toronto is a single-tier city; its CD (3520) and CSD (3520005) codes both identify the same municipality.

TL;DR

This brief localizes the vacant-home-tax-effectiveness backgrounder for Toronto. Toronto's VHT, in effect since the 2022 tax year, raised approximately $56.5 million (2022) and $50.6 million (2023) at a 1% rate before Council raised the rate to 3% following a badly botched 2024 declaration cycle — a default-vacant design that wrongly flagged roughly 167,000 homes (over 20% of the city's residential properties), generating 120,000+ complaints and approximately 108,000 reversed charges. Vancouver's more extensively evaluated Empty Homes Tax offers the best available evidence on what a VHT can and cannot do: it measurably cuts vacancy (an independent study found ~5,355 fewer vacant units, 2016–2021) but has no measurable effect on average rents — a finding this brief does not know to be true or false for Toronto specifically, since no equivalent independent Toronto study exists in this leaf's evidence base. The upstream backgrounder is marked DRAFT v1.0.

FOR

Evidence supporting the case that Toronto's VHT is a worthwhile tool:

  • The VHT targets Toronto's specific "buy-and-leave" financialized-housing problem directly, is close to self-funding, and Vancouver's comparable tax has generated real revenue (~$194M since 2017, ~$142M to affordable housing) validating the tool's basic fiscal logic [backgrounder, "Revenue"].
  • The best available independent evidence (Vancouver, via the C.D. Howe/Caracciolo & Miglino study) confirms a real, causally-identified vacancy-reduction effect (~5,355 fewer vacant units, 2016–2021) without suppressing new construction [backgrounder, "Does a VHT reduce vacancy?"].
  • Toronto has already responded to its own 2024 failure with concrete process fixes (earlier portal opening, extended deadlines, a dedicated help team, automatic opt-out for ~48,000 believed-occupied homes) [backgrounder, "Toronto's 2024 administrative failure"].

AGAINST

Evidence complicating the "the VHT is working" reading:

  • Toronto's 2024 declaration cycle was a documented administrative failure: a default-vacant design wrongly flagged over 20% of the city's residential properties, an error of a scale that directly harmed ordinary occupied-home owners, particularly (per the inherited source's own equity analysis) seniors, non-English speakers, estates, and people with medical absences [backgrounder, "Toronto's 2024 administrative failure," "Key tensions / tradeoffs"].
  • The same independent study validating the vacancy-reduction effect in Vancouver also found no measurable effect on average rents — the tool's own best evidence base does not support treating it as an affordability fix, only a vacancy-and-revenue measure [backgrounder, "What the evidence does and doesn't support"].
  • Toronto's own revenue services state they "cannot confirm whether properties identified as vacant have been rented" — meaning even the City's own "units returned to use" claims are inferred, not observed [backgrounder, "Measurement opacity and gaming risk"].
  • A specific, plausible gaming channel — units shifting to short-term rental use to escape the VHT while remaining unavailable as long-term housing — is flagged by the inherited source as a real risk with "thin and badly needed" evidence, not yet established either way [backgrounder, same section].

The backgrounder itself carries the relevant figures directly (all INHERITED from the leaf's spine document): Toronto's ~$56.5M (2022) and ~$50.6M (2023) VHT revenue at the 1% rate; a projected (but explicitly [confirm]-flagged, ceiling-not-central-estimate) ~$105M/year at the current 3% rate; and an uncosted ([confirm]-flagged, roughly $3–6M estimated) administration cost.

Precedents

  • Vancouver, Canada (EHT, 2017–): the best-evaluated comparator — ~67% decline in declared vacant homes, ~$194M raised (~$142M to affordable housing), an independently-validated ~1.5 percentage point vacancy reduction, but no measured rent effect [backgrounder, "Best global comparators"].
  • British Columbia Speculation and Vacancy Tax (provincial, 2018–): layered above Vancouver's municipal EHT — named as a model for senior-government scaling, current rates/coverage flagged [confirm] [backgrounder, same section].
  • France (taxe sur les logements vacants, 1999–) and Melbourne/Victoria, Australia (Vacant Residential Land Tax): both named by the inherited source as longer-running comparators generally found modest in effect, both flagged [confirm] and not independently verified in this pass [backgrounder, same section].

Upward Ask

The VHT itself is entirely within municipal authority in Toronto's case (as a single-tier city setting its own property-tax-adjacent levy); the backgrounder's inherited comparator notes a provincial speculation-and-vacancy tax layered above Vancouver's municipal EHT, which is a distinct, senior-government-level instrument rather than something Toronto's own VHT depends on. No municipal ask record was found in research/data/municipal_asks.csv for this jurisdiction and issue.

Bottom line

For Toronto, the VHT is a real, revenue-generating tool with a documented 2024 administrative failure the City has since moved to fix, sitting on top of an evidence base (largely from Vancouver, since no equivalent independent Toronto study exists) that supports the tax as effective at reducing vacancy and raising money, but explicitly not effective at moving rents. The clearest locally-true synthesis: Toronto's VHT is worth keeping and fixing, not scrapping, but should not be presented to residents as an affordability solution — its own best evidence says it isn't one.
