Community Safety and Well-Being Plans — Toronto

DRAFT Jurisdiction: Toronto, single-tier city · Data as of: 2026-07-14 (core source: City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation," April 2025)

Backgrounder: research/backgrounders/community-safety-wellbeing-plans.md · Data as of: 2026-07-14 (core source: City of Toronto, "2025 Update on SafeTO Implementation," April 2025) Jurisdiction: Toronto, single-tier city · Status: DRAFT

TL;DR

Ontario law has required every municipality to adopt a Community Safety and Well-Being Plan since 2021, reviewed every four years, but the province provides no dedicated funding for implementing that requirement, per the City's own report [backgrounder, "Legislative basis"]. Toronto's plan, SafeTO, reports over 90% of its 26 actions complete or in progress after its first phase (2022-2024), including a mental-health crisis service that has handled more than 25,000 calls and resolved 78% without police involvement — but the same report discloses a 34% rise in gun violence in 2024, concentrated among youth and in six specific areas of the city [backgrounder, "SafeTO: Toronto's plan, Phase 1 outcomes"; "A documented setback alongside the reported progress"]. A key federal funding stream expires by March 2026 with no confirmed replacement.

FOR (case that SafeTO is working and should continue as designed)

  • The plan has produced concrete, measurable outputs: the Toronto Community Crisis Service (over 25,000 mental health crisis calls, 78% resolved without police involvement, a 4.5% decrease in mental-health-related 911 calls in 2023), the Community Crisis Response Program (1,844 critical incidents responded to at an 88% 48-hour response rate), and a Violence Prevention Toronto Office coordinating 60 multi-sector responses to violent incidents [backgrounder, "SafeTO: Toronto's plan, Phase 1 outcomes"].
  • The City's reported 2025 Summer Safety Plan is credited with a 40% reduction in gun violence alongside substantial youth-program participation (15,000+ youth), though this figure traces to a search-summary source rather than an independently confirmed primary evaluation [backgrounder, "A documented setback alongside the reported progress"].
  • The underlying model — a public-health, prevention-oriented approach to violence — has strong long-run international evidence behind it: Scotland's Violence Reduction Unit produced a 69% fall in weapon-carrying (2005-2016) and a 63% fall in hospital admissions from sharp-object assaults (2008-2015), and the model has since been replicated by over twenty Violence Reduction Units across England and Wales [backgrounder, "International context"].

AGAINST (case that SafeTO's progress claims and design need scrutiny)

  • The same report claiming 90%+ action completion discloses a 34% increase in gun violence in 2024 — the final year of the reporting period — disproportionately affecting youth aged 12-29 (58% of firearm-incident participants, 23% of the population) and concentrated in six areas holding just 22% of the city's population [backgrounder, "A documented setback alongside the reported progress"].
  • No independent (Auditor General-level or academic) evaluation of SafeTO specifically was located — unlike Toronto Paramedic Services, which received direct Auditor General scrutiny. The UK's own multi-decade experience with a comparable model (Community Safety Partnerships) found evaluation was frequently skipped or inconsistent, with no common effectiveness standard and over-reliance on raw crime-count data — suggesting this is a common failure mode for this model type generally, not necessarily a Toronto-specific shortcoming, but one that leaves SafeTO's own claimed progress harder to independently confirm [backgrounder, "Open questions / data gaps"; "International context"].
  • Funding is explicitly precarious: a $6.8 million federal contribution expires by March 2026, and the City's own report states Phase 2 funding "will be requested in the 2026 Budget and future Budget processes... subject to the City's financial resource capacity" — language stating available funding is not yet secured, not a confirmed commitment [backgrounder, "Funding scale and its stated fragility"].
  • The province's own mandate carries no dedicated implementation funding, per the City's repeated statement — though genuine, narrower provincial grant programs (the Safer and Vital Communities Grant, Mobile Crisis Response Team Enhancement Grant) do exist and should not be conflated with a claim that Ontario provides zero funding touching this space at all [backgrounder, "Key tensions / tradeoffs"].

Costs

Toronto has invested over $60.6 million gross ($55.7 million net) in community-led violence prevention and interruption programs since SafeTO's 2021 adoption, excluding administrative costs [backgrounder, "Funding scale and its stated fragility"]. Separately cited annual figures ($43 million approved for 2025, a reported $49 million annual investment referenced in 2026 coverage, including $5 million specifically for youth violence prevention) were not reconciled to the cumulative figure in this pass and should be read as drawn from different reporting windows rather than a single verified total. No L3 structured fiscal data (FIR, StatsCan) was joined against these figures this pass.

Precedents

Scotland's Violence Reduction Unit (launched 2005, expanded nationally 2006) is the best-evidenced international precedent for the public-health approach SafeTO's own framing explicitly adopts, with an 11-year outcome track record (69% fall in weapon-carrying, 63% fall in hospital admissions for sharp-object assaults) that has since been judged replicable at scale — over twenty comparable units now operate across England and Wales, backed by more than £200 million in funding [backgrounder, "International context"]. Within Ontario, comparator municipalities (London, Windsor-Essex, Peel, Hamilton, Waterloo Region, Ottawa) have all adopted their own CSWB plans under the same legislative requirement, with a cited (though not independently re-verified) academic review of 33 Ontario municipalities finding comprehensive, collaborative planning was the area of weakest actual practice across the sample, and fewer than half of examined initiatives showing "high potential for success" [backgrounder, "Other Ontario municipalities' plans"].

Bottom line

Toronto's own reporting shows SafeTO delivering real, specific program outputs in its first phase while the citywide outcome the plan most centrally targets — gun violence — moved in the wrong direction in the plan's final reported year; whether that reflects a lagging effect, a citywide trend the plan could not be expected to fully offset in three years, or a genuine design shortfall is not resolved by any source in this leaf, and no independent evaluation exists yet to adjudicate between those readings.

Uncertainties

Whether the 2025 Summer Safety Plan's reported 40% gun-violence reduction reflects a causal effect or a correlation stated in summary language that does not itself establish causation is unresolved — the underlying primary evaluation document was not independently fetched this pass [backgrounder, "A documented setback alongside the reported progress"]. Whether SafeTO's Phase 2 funding, including the federal grants expiring by March 2026, will be renewed is explicitly unconfirmed in the City's own report language. The relationship between SafeTO's cumulative and annual reported funding figures has not been reconciled. Whether any enforcement mechanism exists for Ontario municipalities that fail to adopt or review a CSWB plan on schedule, and what proportion of Ontario's 400+ municipalities currently have a compliant, up-to-date plan, are both genuinely uncovered questions this brief does not answer — see the backgrounder's "Open questions / data gaps" for the complete list before this brief is used publicly.
