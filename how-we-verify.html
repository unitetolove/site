How We Verify

Every claim on this site that carries a figure, a fact, or a quote is supposed to earn that place, not just occupy it. This page explains the shape of how that happens — the method, not the case files.

The loop

Publication here follows a fixed order, not a shortcut:

  1. What we owe. Before anything is written, the questions a page is meant to answer are written down first. What a page owes its reader is decided before drafting starts, not backfilled after.
  2. Drafting against sources. A draft is built directly against real, citable material — never written first and sourced later.
  3. Backwards verification. This is the hard gate. Before anything ships, every load-bearing claim in the draft is pulled back out, one at a time, and checked independently against its original source — not against the draft’s own summary of that source, and not by trusting a first pass at face value. If a source can’t be reached or confirmed, the claim is fixed, hedged, or removed — it does not ship unresolved.
  4. A record that survives the source moving or disappearing later. When a source is checked, a copy of what was actually read is kept alongside the claim, so a claim's backing doesn't quietly rot if the original page later changes or vanishes.
  5. Plain-language release. Only after that verification pass does a claim become eligible to appear in a public, plain-language page — never the other way around.
  6. Corrections propagate everywhere, automatically. If a claim is later found wrong or disputed, every place on this site that repeats it gets checked and updated — found by a systematic sweep across the whole site, not by relying on anyone’s memory of where it was used.

Independence is part of the design

Checking is never done only by whoever drafted the claim. A second, independent pass re-verifies sourced material before it’s trusted for publication — the same discipline a careful editor would apply to any writer, regardless of who or what produced the first draft. Being “checked once” and being “verified” are treated as two different things on purpose.

What our status words actually mean

You’ll see a small, consistent set of words across this site. Here is exactly what each one promises:

  • Sealed — every load-bearing claim in this page has been independently checked against its original source, with zero unresolved disagreements found. This is our highest bar, not a marketing word.
  • In verification — drafted against real sources and going through the same independent check described above right now. Some pages carry one or two specific open items — a source that wouldn’t load, a figure not yet confirmed a second way — and we say so rather than rounding up.
  • Draft — written, not yet through the verification pass. Treat anything marked draft as a starting point, not a finished claim.
  • Proposed / coming — planned or scoped, not built or drafted yet.

We would rather show you an honest “in verification” than a premature “sealed.” A visible drop in how much of the site is sealed, if that ever happens because something failed a re-check, is treated as the system working, not something to hide.

What happens when something turns out to be wrong

The short version lives on our corrections page. The relevant point here is that a correction is never a quiet edit. Once a claim is confirmed wrong or no longer solid, every page repeating it is found and updated together, the change is dated, and the old version stays visible with its history — not deleted, replaced.

See the method applied, end to end

The clearest worked example on this site: the “check our work” page of Why can’t Toronto move? — a claim-by-claim ledger with its receipts, the first-party measurement the report rests on, the hostile questions we asked our own numbers before publishing, and the dated re-check schedule. That page is what the method above looks like when a full report goes through it.

What this page is not

This is a description of method, not a log of specific findings, disputes, or any individual claim’s history — those live on the pages the claims actually appear on, and in the public corrections record. It also isn’t a description of the specific tools or internal workflow used to do any of this; that's an implementation detail that can change without changing the promise above. The promise is the point: a claim either traces to a real, checked source, or it says so on its face.
