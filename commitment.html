Our Commitment

Most projects ask you to trust them. This one asks you to check it. This page is the whole trust architecture in one place: the rules we bind ourselves to, the method every claim passes before it ships, the public door for telling us we’re wrong, and an honest account of how this was made — machines included. Every section below is something you can publicly hold us to. Version 1, published 2026‑08‑12; it will keep versioning in public, and daylight between these words and our conduct is a correction — file it at the door below.

What we promise

Draft proposal — not yet adopted. This page is staged for review and has not been published as a commitment. When it publishes, every rule on it becomes something you can publicly hold us to.

Most projects ask you to trust them. This one asks you to check it. These are the standing rules we bind ourselves to — written plainly, in one place, so that anyone, friendly or hostile, can measure what we do against what we said.

The truth laws

Every claim carries a receipt.

Every figure on this site traces to a public source or a computation we show. If a claim can't be traced, it doesn't publish. Receipts do the persuading; we don't.

Every number wears its date.

Facts age. A candidate count, a turnout figure, a rule about voting — each one is stamped with when it was true, and re-checked when the world moves. An undated number is treated as a wrong number.

What we don't know, we say — on the page, not in a footnote.

Unverified things are labeled unverified. Open questions are listed openly. We would rather show you a gap than paper over one.

Our coverage is not the world.

When we've only examined part of something, we say what we examined. A percentage describes our research unless every case was checked — we will never let "we haven't looked yet" masquerade as "it doesn't exist."

Corrections are public and welcome.

When we're wrong, the correction is published, dated, and left visible — and the person who caught it is thanked. Finding our mistakes is the system working, not the system failing. Here's the door.

One substance, many doors — never many stories.

The same facts may be framed for different readers, but every version traces to the same canonical substance. We do not tell one audience one thing and another audience another.

The identity law

The work is signed by a pseudonym so the ideas stand alone.

Everything here is written under the name the Unknown Soldier. That is deliberate: no reputation, no personality, no face asks for your trust — only work you can verify. If the arguments don't survive your checking, no name should rescue them.

The neutrality firewall

Nobody elected us — so we endorse nobody.

This project never endorses, ranks, or opposes any candidate. Ever.

Every candidate is served identically.

Question banks, research, briefs, the how-to-run kit — whatever we offer one candidate in a race, every candidate in that race gets, on identical terms, whatever their politics.

We tell anyone how to vote — never who.

Voting information here is neutral logistics from official sources. No turnout effort of ours may target by party, by candidate, or by how anyone is expected to vote.

Nothing to join, nothing replaced.

This project builds commons, not a membership organization. It feeds existing communities, organizations, and rooms — it does not compete with them, absorb them, or ask anyone to leave them.

The money rules

No donations are asked for — anywhere, from anyone.

You will not find a donate button on this site. Participation is the currency here.

Research money and election money never mix.

The funds behind this infrastructure pay for research and civic commons only. They do not and will not pay for election advertising for or against anyone. One purpose per account; no dollar crosses that line. (The full account structure goes to legal counsel for verification before this page publishes — that review is a gate we set for ourselves.)

If we ever sell anything, the margin is disclosed.

Should anything ever be offered for sale — a poster, a shirt — what it cost and what it earns will be printed next to the price.

Accounted to the dollar, in public.

The standing promise: spending is logged transaction by transaction, and the claims register publishes. Until the full public claims register page is live, the spending summary in How this was made is the interim record.

The dignity laws

The dual test — every page passes both or doesn't publish.

One: it survives a hostile fact-checker. Two: every person described in it can read it with their dignity intact. A page that fails either test is not finished.

No statistic may corner an identifiable person.

We never attach an aggregate claim to a group so small it describes one or two nameable people. A "statistic" about a class of one is a characterization, and we don't do it.

Safety beats completeness.

Where data could expose someone precarious — immigration status above all — we publish magnitudes, not counts, or nothing at all. No surface we build may put a vulnerable person at risk, no matter what it would prove.

The participation laws

Consent first, deletion built in.

Anything you give us — a signature, a submission, an idea — is collected with consent, used for what we said, and deletable on your word. Minors are welcomed to participate with a stated gate, never quietly harvested.

Receipts, not newsletters.

We report what happened — what was built, what was checked, what was corrected — rather than marketing to you. Measurement is aggregate-only: we count participation; we do not surveil participants.

In practice, plainly: every page on this site loads one small, aggregate-only visit counter — Cloudflare Web Analytics. What it reports, pooled across every visitor: which pages get read, what site referred you here, your country, and your device/browser/OS class. Per Cloudflare's own documentation, it does not collect or use visitors' personal data, does not log query strings, and does not track individual people across sites — and we set no cookie and store nothing on your device ourselves; the counter is the only script we run. Raw beacon data is kept seven days, then aggregated down and the detail is gone. There is no dashboard anywhere that shows you as a person — only counts. If that ever stops being true, that's a correction, filed the same way as any other: the corrections door.

Holding us to all of this

These rules run deeper than this page: behind them sit full written standards — an honesty editing rubric, a data-retention schedule, a cryptographic timestamping standard — that are internal today and publish as each is ruled ready. The page you're reading is the plain-language layer, and it is the binding one: if you catch daylight between these rules and anything this project does, that is a correction, and the corrections door is how you file it. How we verify

Every claim on this site that carries a figure, a fact, or a quote is supposed to earn that place, not just occupy it. This page explains the shape of how that happens — the method, not the case files.

The loop

Publication here follows a fixed order, not a shortcut:

  1. What we owe. Before anything is written, the questions a page is meant to answer are written down first. What a page owes its reader is decided before drafting starts, not backfilled after.
  2. Drafting against sources. A draft is built directly against real, citable material — never written first and sourced later.
  3. Backwards verification. This is the hard gate. Before anything ships, every load-bearing claim in the draft is pulled back out, one at a time, and checked independently against its original source. That check never relies on the draft's own summary of the source, and never trusts a first pass at face value. If a source can’t be reached or confirmed, the claim is fixed, hedged, or removed — it does not ship unresolved.
  4. A record that survives the source moving or disappearing later. When a source is checked, a copy of what was actually read is kept alongside the claim, so a claim's backing doesn't quietly rot if the original page later changes or vanishes.
  5. Plain-language release. Only after that verification pass does a claim become eligible to appear in a public, plain-language page — never the other way around.
  6. Corrections propagate everywhere, automatically. If a claim is later found wrong or disputed, every place on this site that repeats it gets checked and updated — found by a systematic sweep across the whole site, not by relying on anyone’s memory of where it was used.

Independence is part of the design

Checking is never done only by whoever drafted the claim. A second, independent pass re-verifies sourced material before it’s trusted for publication — the same discipline a careful editor would apply to any writer, regardless of who or what produced the first draft. Being “checked once” and being “verified” are treated as two different things on purpose.

What our status words actually mean

You’ll see a small, consistent set of words across this site. Here is exactly what each one promises:

  • Sealed — every load-bearing claim in this page has been independently checked against its original source, with zero unresolved disagreements found. This is our highest bar, not a marketing word.
  • In verification — drafted against real sources and going through the same independent check described above right now. Some pages carry one or two specific open items — a source that wouldn’t load, a figure not yet confirmed a second way — and we say so rather than rounding up.
  • Draft — written, not yet through the verification pass. Treat anything marked draft as a starting point, not a finished claim.
  • Proposed / coming — planned or scoped, not built or drafted yet.

We would rather show you an honest “in verification” than a premature “sealed.” A visible drop in how much of the site is sealed, if that ever happens because something failed a re-check, is treated as the system working, not something to hide.

What happens when something turns out to be wrong

The short version lives on our corrections page. The relevant point here is that a correction is never a quiet edit. Once a claim is confirmed wrong or no longer solid, every page repeating it is found and updated together, the change is dated, and the old version stays visible with its history — not deleted, replaced.

See the method applied, end to end

The clearest worked example on this site: the “check our work” page of Why can’t Toronto move? — a claim-by-claim claims register with its receipts, the first-party measurement the report rests on, the hostile questions we asked our own numbers before publishing, and the dated re-check schedule. That page is what the method above looks like when a full report goes through it.

What this page is not

This is a description of method, not a log of specific findings, disputes, or any individual claim’s history — those live on the pages the claims actually appear on, and in the public corrections record. It also isn’t a description of the specific tools or internal workflow used to do any of this; that's an implementation detail that can change without changing the promise above. The promise is the point: a claim either traces to a real, checked source, or it says so on its face.

Make it better — corrections, ideas, hands

This is an open invitation, and we mean it as loudly as a quiet library can. Everything on this site is a working draft of something bigger, built in public — and the fastest way it gets better is you telling us something we don’t know yet. This page is how.

What we’re asking for

All of it is welcome. Any of these makes the site better the day it arrives:

  • Corrections — a claim that’s wrong, a source that doesn’t hold, a figure that’s out of date. Include the claim (its ID if you have it — every figure on this site shows one on hover), what’s wrong, and the better source if you have one. No better source? Send it anyway; “this doesn’t hold” with a reason is plenty. You don’t need credentials. The evidence needs credentials.
  • Improvements — a clearer way to say something, a page that’s confusing, a path through the site that dead-ends. If it made you squint, tell us.
  • Ideas — something we should build, cover, ask, or try. The project grows by exactly this route.
  • Resources — a dataset, a report, a person’s published work, a tool, a precedent from another city. Receipts are our whole currency; bring us more.
  • Hands — if you want to help build, verify, translate, or organize, say so. There is real work here and it is genuinely open.

Send it

One channel, monitored, live today:

The email opens with a short template — the one thing we really need is which page you’re writing about, so please paste the page’s address in. Since you came here from another page on this site, we’ve pre-filled that page’s address into the email for you — check it’s the one you meant.

A one-click “suggest an improvement” button on every page is planned and not yet built — until then, this mailbox is the whole machine, and it works.

What happens to your note

It gets read — a person reads every note; nothing vanishes into an unwatched inbox. It gets verified — we check it against sources, with the same discipline we apply to our own claims, and then one of three things happens in the open: we fix it (the claim changes everywhere it appears, the old version stays visible with a dated correction), we show our work (if the claim holds, we publish why, with sources, so the next person with your doubt finds the answer waiting), or we downgrade it (sometimes the honest answer is “we’re no longer sure,” and the claim’s status changes to say exactly that). And you get credited, if you want credit — tell us the name to use, or stay anonymous; both are honored.

What never happens: silent edits, quiet deletions, or a claim that goes on being cited after it broke. Fixing things fast and logging the fix in the open isn’t damage control here — it’s the product working as designed.

If it’s serious

If you believe you’ve found something that doesn’t just weaken a claim but points at real wrongdoing by real institutions — stop. Don’t send it here first. Serious allegations deserve journalists, auditors, or authorities with the power and the duty to investigate them properly; we are neither, and we don’t play at being either. This library documents what’s published and verifiable. It is not a tip line.

A standing note on tone

Blunt is fine. You don’t have to wrap a correction in compliments — a correct stranger is worth a hundred agreeable ones, and we’d rather build with the first group. Bring what you’ve got; we’ll bring the gratitude and the follow-through. That’s the trade.

One more thing. Somewhere on this site right now there’s an error nobody has found yet, an idea nobody has sent yet, and a pair of hands we haven’t met. There always is — we built the whole system around that sentence staying permanently true. Which means this page isn’t fine print. It’s a help-wanted sign, and it’s always up.

Written with machines — the authorship disclosure

Yes. AI helped build this. A lot of it. Let’s talk about it plainly, because you deserve better than a disclosure buried in a footer.

Here is honestly how this work gets made.

One of your neighbours — one human being, with a small circle of friends at the edges — decides what this project cares about, what it will never do, and what gets published. Every value in it, every red line, every “we won’t touch that” came from a person. The questions came from people. The stubbornness came from people.

The machines do what machines are good at: they read faster than we ever could. They sweep thousands of documents, draft summaries, cross-check claims against sources, find the study we’d never have found, and argue with us at three in the morning. They also draft prose — including, we’ll say it before you do, early versions of pages like this one.

Now the part that actually matters, because “humans direct, AI drafts” is what everyone says. The question isn’t who typed it. The question is: why should you trust any of it?

Our answer is the same answer we give for everything: nothing here asks to be trusted because of who — or what — wrote it. Every load-bearing claim carries a receipt that leads to a source that existed before any machine summarized it. AI systems are famous for confidently making things up. We know. That failure mode is precisely why the receipts architecture exists: a claim either traces to a real, archived, checkable source, or it’s labeled unverified, or it doesn’t ship. The pipeline was designed by a person who assumes the machine is sometimes wrong — the same way this project assumes we are sometimes wrong. Distrust, verified, all the way down.

Think of it this way: if a human researcher with a brilliant reading speed and an occasional habit of misremembering worked here, you’d want every one of their claims checked against sources before publication. That is literally our process. The species of the researcher was never the load-bearing part.

Could we have done this without AI? A version of it — smaller, slower, and honestly, years too late to matter. A handful of unpaid neighbours cannot read a hundred thousand pages of municipal reports, audits, and studies. With these tools, they can — and the whole point of this project is that the tools of institutional-scale knowledge finally fit in a community’s hands. It would be strange to build a movement about that and be embarrassed by it. We’re not embarrassed. We’re careful — which is different, and harder, and documented.

What the machines don’t do: they don’t decide our values. They don’t approve publication. They don’t choose what the movement asks for or who it stands with. And they don’t get blamed — if you find an error, it’s ours, the humans’, because signing the work means owning its mistakes. The corrections page doesn’t care where a mistake was born; neither should we.

You’ll meet people who say AI involvement discredits all of this automatically. Ask them the only question that matters: is the claim true, and how would we know? We’ve made that checkable. Most of what you’re asked to believe every day — by institutions with far bigger budgets than ours — has not.

The whole story — who runs this, how, and what it actually cost

Updated July 28, 2026. An earlier version of this page held a visible placeholder here, noting we hadn’t yet decided whether to name the specific AI systems and vendors used. That call is made, in three words: be completely honest. What follows is one story, told straight — the person, the controls, the money, and the month. All figures in Canadian dollars.

Start with the person, because everything else hangs off him. One human being directs, orchestrates, and controls all of it. To borrow from a certain gravel-voiced movie dad: he has a very particular set of skills. Skills acquired over a very long career. Not those skills — his are three-plus decades of living on the internet: finding things, reading things, checking things, building things, and refusing to log off. If a forgotten municipal report exists, he will find it, and he will cite it.

And the machines answer to him — not loosely, but by written constitution. Every instruction he gives is captured word-for-word in a permanent log, and an AI session’s first duty is to read those words back correctly before acting on them. Every ruling he makes becomes a numbered decision on a permanent record. Every substantive edit preserves a snapshot of the version before it, so the full history of every document stays inspectable forever. Every load-bearing claim needs a receipt. Every page faces adversarial review — hostile-reader personas hunting for the ways it could mislead — before it’s called ready. Automated checks sweep every batch of work for leaks and drift. And nothing goes public without his hand on the button. The machines here are brilliant and fast, and they are on a leash held by someone who has been on the internet longer than some of their training data.

Now the money, because it is the most instructive part. This person had not paid for a single AI tool in over two years. Then, on June 27, 2026, a friend lent him a Claude account — and bought him his own month of it as a gift. Everything on this site began in the thirty-one days that followed. But here is the number that matters more, because most of those payments landed late in the month: pro-rate each subscription by the days actually used by July 28 — and the AI actually consumed came to roughly CAD $330. A few hundred dollars. The rest was capacity for the month ahead, already bought. Everything you can read on this site — every page, every receipt, every guide — cost a few hundred dollars of machine time, directed by one unpaid human. This claims register updates as the story does; the dates on it tell you how current it is.

Around the paid seats, a rotating free bench did the bulk reading and cross-checking — a dozen or so free tiers from every major lab, each linked from this page’s live version with the date we last checked its terms, because free tiers change monthly and a stale claim about somebody else’s pricing is still a stale claim. Plus one already-owned gaming PC running free open-weight models around the clock as a tireless local research assistant. The paid seats do the judgment-heavy work: drafting, verification passes, orchestration. Kimi is the deliberately different outside eye — a second family of models, so no single vendor’s blind spots become ours. The cheap and free tiers haul the bulk. Every tool’s output passes through the same receipts discipline — the vendor was never the load-bearing part either.

(Free tiers as checked July 28, 2026 — they change often; each link is the living source): Qwen (free chat app, Qwen3.7-Max/Plus previews), Z.ai (GLM-4.7-Flash), DeepSeek (V4-family chat), ChatGPT (GPT-5.5 Instant), Gemini (3.6 Flash, with a daily taste of 3.1 Pro), Perplexity (Sonar), Google’s Antigravity preview (rate-limited Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Sonnet/Opus 4.6, GPT-OSS 120B), Vibe by Mistral (Medium 3.5), Meta AI (Llama 4), Grok (light free tier), Suno (v4.5-all, for music), and Microsoft Copilot (GPT-5.1 Smart mode).

What did thirty-one days and a few hundred consumed dollars produce? A civic research library of 666 live pages — backgrounders, briefs, cards, and day-one implementation pages across every major issue, each carrying its verification status on its face. A living atlas of 765 free-help entries across 54 categories, from crisis lines to free skating. A plain-language how-to-vote guide checked against the election regulation itself — a check that caught the City’s own published ID list omitting a document the regulation accepts, now flagged publicly. A candidate’s handbook for anyone deciding whether to stand in October. The verification machinery under all of it. And since that count was taken: the conversation itself opened — the door where a neighbour signs up, the standing offer that brings the author to any room in the city that invites him, and a poster pack any neighbour can print at home, every line of it traceable back to this same library. If you like numbers, the auto-generated tally that day: 5,950 files holding just over a million lines of text and code, 3,386 research and strategy documents, more than 150,000 rows and entries of structured data, 41,167 distinct external sources consulted, 93 numbered decisions, 112 logged working sessions, 74 written work orders between AI sessions — counted by a script precisely so the legend can’t outgrow the claims register, and re-run at launch.

The honest asterisks, before anyone else finds them: a friend’s gift started it; the free tiers are subsidized by their vendors; the gaming PC was already owned; quality varies by page and every page says so on its face — that is what the statuses are for. And none of it counts the human’s own time, which was enormous and unpaid.

Here is the message inside the numbers. One skilled person — unpaid, anonymous, directing machines with a written constitution and a few hundred dollars of compute — can now stand up a receipted civic research library at a scale that used to need an institute and a budget with commas in it. The machines didn’t bring the values, the questions, or the stubbornness. They made one stubborn person’s month go institutionally far. Now imagine a thousand of us, in every city, at this standard, with receipts. That is the actual invitation of this page.

Judge the work. Check the receipts. It’s the same invitation as ever. It doesn’t change with the typist. The door is on the home page. It takes thirty seconds.
