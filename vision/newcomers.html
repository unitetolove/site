You are needed here

Whatever your status — landed, waiting, on a permit, still deciding — the door here is open, and nothing on this page pretends otherwise.

0. What this is (and isn’t)

This is not an organization. There’s no membership, no dues, nothing to sign up for beyond a name and a way to reach you. It begins as one person’s synthesis of thousands of conversations — many of them with newcomers themselves — offered alongside a library of verified civic information anyone can check.

The ballot has a citizenship requirement. This conversation does not. Whatever your status — landed, waiting, on a permit, still deciding — the door here is open, and nothing below pretends otherwise.

1. The honest numbers on who can vote — and who’s welcomed anyway

Toronto doesn’t hide this, and neither will we. As of the 2021 Census, of Toronto’s 2.79 million residents, about 1.87 million were adult Canadian citizens — eligible to vote by age and citizenship alike. Roughly 430,000 more were adults excluded from voting solely because of citizenship status — permanent or temporary residents. About 462,000 were children under 18. That is the law as it stands, stated as a number, not a grievance and not a euphemism.

And immigrant Toronto is not the same as excluded Toronto: at least ~400,000 of Toronto’s own electors are foreign-born citizens — people who came from somewhere else and now hold the ballot themselves. Immigrant Toronto both votes and carries the excluded. Beyond the city line, more people live in the surrounding GTA than inside Toronto — they work, drive, and build here daily with no ballot at all. The election is Toronto’s; the conversation is regional, and the world’s diasporas are watching.

So here is the actual invitation, stated plainly: the running public count of everyone who has joined this conversation includes, counted proudly, the people who can’t vote. Voters and non-voters. Residents and the excluded. The GTA worker with no Toronto ballot, the student on a permit, the person watching from Manila or Lagos or Kyiv who loves this city without living in it yet. Worldwide welcome, counted publicly — and the count always leads with Toronto’s own number first, never buries it in a bigger one. The ballot is one door into this city’s future. It was never the only one.

If you’re under 18, you sign up through a parent — same minimal data, same right to withdraw, stated here at the door, not buried in a settings page.

2. What we’re actually asking — and what we’re not

You don’t have to become an activist. You don’t have to join a party, attend a meeting, or prove your loyalty to anyone. Nobody pays to participate, and there are no donation asks — not at the door, not by email, not ever. The blueprints are free forever.

The first ask is the only mandatory one, and it is small on purpose: sign up for the conversation. Thirty seconds — a name and a way to reach you. Your signature moves a public number the whole city can see, and when the room opens, you’re already in it.

And the conversation itself doesn’t have to happen in a room somebody else picked, or in English. Part of what’s being built between now and the vote — a target, never a claim, of ten thousand conversations across the city in three months — is exactly this: your own room, your own circle, your own language, on your own terms. A conversation your community is already having counts the moment it sends back what it heard: a vision, a complaint, or a name worth encouraging toward candidacy. Nothing is convened inside any community without an invitation from inside it — this project offers a kit and a place for what your community already says to be counted; it never shows up uninvited with a meeting of its own.

Everything past the first ask is optional, and every rung returns something back to you — not a newsletter, a receipt: what your contribution became, and the next smallest thing you could do if you want it. Nothing here is a queue you join and then wait in silence.

On gatherings, stated as a promise, not a hope: kids are always welcome, food is shared, and no gathering under this banner ever charges admission — that’s structural, forever, not a launch-period courtesy. Fare and childcare support arrive the moment the merch margin or a sponsor covers them, publicly, when that’s true — we’re not promising what isn’t funded yet.

And on money, to the dollar: if you ever see something for sale under this banner — a shirt, a pin, a poster — that’s optional merch, sold at a disclosed margin, and every dollar of that margin funds the AI tools and the printed posters, accounted publicly. Nobody involved is paid yet, including the people asking. Your presence is never a revenue line for anyone.

3. Before we ask anything of you: the credential wall

Here is where honesty has to come before invitation, not after it.

The credentialed newcomer driving a cab is not a metaphor in this city; it is a measured pattern. Internationally trained professionals — doctors, engineers, nurses, teachers — work far below their training at a stark, measured rate compared to those credentialed here. That is a hospital’s worth of doctors and a faculty’s worth of engineers, held behind paperwork by a city that needs exactly what they already know how to do.

The students who were sold a path believed, reasonably, in a study-here, work-here, belong-here pathway that policy has since narrowed under them. Whatever their futures hold from here, while they are in this city they are skill, energy, and heart — ready to build, needed now, not someday.

So before this page asks you for a single hour of your time, here is what this project puts on the public agenda first: the credential-recognition fight is advocacy this project carries before it asks anything back. That is the order, stated as an order: the barriers get named and pushed on first; the ask for your energy comes after, not instead of it. If that sounds backwards from every “get involved” pitch you’ve heard before, it’s meant to. Most of those pitches ask first and fix later, if ever. This one doesn’t get to ask first.

And the fight has a file, not just a feeling. The library beneath this page carries a dedicated backgrounder — “Newcomer Success & Credential Recognition” — tracking, with every figure’s verification status marked on its face, Ontario’s fair-access and labour-mobility legislation, bridging-program funding, and the levers the City itself holds as employer, procurer, and convener. Organizations already doing this work — TRIEC and COSTI among them — are named there as evidence of the infrastructure that already exists, not as affiliations.

4. The untapped city — and you are already in it

This city is full of people who want to serve and have never been asked. That’s not a slogan aimed at newcomers alone — Canadian-born youth who did everything they were told and still stepped into a world without the jobs they were promised belong in the same sentence, carrying the same weight, needed for the same reasons. But some of it is newcomer-specific, and it’s worth naming directly:

  • The diasporas as the world’s bridge. Toronto holds people who are, in this project’s own observed experience, extraordinary at community, resilience, and raising families — and who are also the world’s best-positioned global support network for everything this city proves works. The direction is stated exactly as CORE states it, and it matters which way the arrow points: improve home from here, or bring the solutions home with you — to help the communities you came from. Nobody here is presumed to be leaving. Many of you are already home; this is the family being raised.
  • The elders with decades of mastery, and nobody asking. If that’s you, or someone in your building, your temple, your mosque, your church, your gurdwara — the invitation names you specifically, not as a demographic category but as a person with something the city needs.
  • The most universal story this project has heard, from thousands of newcomers themselves: one of the deepest human urges is to go out while young — to learn, earn, explore, adventure — and then to come home and raise a family. Toronto is where much of the world goes out to. The work this project asks for is never to appraise anyone’s worth like a resource on a ledger; it is to ask for service and return it in kind, with recognition and reciprocity, never extraction. The city’s highest honour is to be of use, and there is a use for every single one of us.

5. The city already has what it needs — including you

Nearly every problem this city faces has been solved somewhere else already, often by cities with fewer resources than Toronto’s — catalogued, graded, with the receipts, in the library beneath this page. And 46.6% of this city’s residents are immigrants — which means, concretely: when this project says “study the world’s best ideas,” half the time the person who lived inside the relevant health system, school system, or governance system is already a transit ride away. That is not a talking point about newcomers; it is an honest description of who this city already is.

6. The homeland you carry — and the line this project holds

If your homeland has politics, conflicts, or grief that followed you here, this project is not the place those get relitigated, and that is a promise, not a dodge. No foreign money, ever, and no homeland conflicts imported — that’s charter law here. The diaspora bridge this project builds carries solutions, never disputes. What crosses the bridge is what worked here, going back to help where you’re from — not the argument from home, coming here to continue. That line holds the same way for every community, without exception, and it protects you as much as it protects the project.

And when solutions do travel, eventually, they travel on your calendar, not ours. The sporing — this project’s plan to carry Toronto’s proven solutions to other cities and communities, once Toronto has proven them here first — is designed to ride each community’s own new year: a Year of New Years, each regional launch timed to whatever new year that culture already keeps, carried by the Toronto diaspora that already celebrates it, offered as a gift on a day that already belongs to that community. Two guardrails bind this absolutely, in the project’s own words, and neither is a suggestion: community leads or it doesn’t happen — no launch, no event, no announcement without an invitation from inside that community, the same invitation-from-inside law that governs everything else on this page. And no calendar is a marketing surface — a culture’s festival dates, feast days, or new-year observances are never claimed, scheduled around, or used as a hook by this project; they stay with their keepers, always, the same way Indigenous ceremonies here stay on Indigenous terms and by Indigenous invitation only. This is not scheduled for a while yet, and it is not a promise about your specific community or festival — it is the design rule that will govern it, stated now, before it ever reaches anyone’s door.

7. Who’s actually running this, and who’s watching the money

Nobody elected the person who started this, and nothing here pretends otherwise. Everything is offered, never imposed; legitimacy belongs to what the crowd itself decides. The person behind this project wants no money, no title, and no permanent role — the stated goal is to become unnecessary and hand it off. Every artifact this project publishes discloses when AI tools were used in making it — that disclosure is binding, not optional. And the same rule that keeps homeland conflicts out keeps this project’s books honest: if you ever want to check who’s paying for what, section 2 above already told you — merch margin, disclosed, to the dollar, nothing else.

8. What this stands on, in one line each

Dignity, always — institutions get named with receipts; people never get scapegoated, newcomer or otherwise. Honest baselines — the numbers in section 1 above, stated straight, not softened for comfort or sharpened for outrage. Recognition before extraction — the credential fight comes before the ask, every time. Everyone invited — every language, every faith, every status, every stage of arrival; Indigenous wisdom held on its own terms, on this land, never spoken for. Service before status — however you help is the highest thing this project can say about you.

9. One link

uniteTOlove.ca. Thirty seconds. A name and a way to reach you. Whether you can vote or not, whether you’ve been here ten days or ten years — the count includes you, publicly, and the count always says so.

This page is currently published in English only. Translation into the languages this city actually speaks is a designed, funded path — not an afterthought — tracked under the project’s multilingual work; this page does not draft translations itself.
