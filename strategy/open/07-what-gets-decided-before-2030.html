What gets decided before 2030

People keep calling this the most important election in history. That’s a superlative, and our own rules say superlatives have to earn themselves — so here is the claim, itemized, with the receipts to check. Read the list and judge the stakes yourself.

Start with the arithmetic that makes a municipal ballot part of it at all: the council elected this October 26 serves a four-year term — into late 2030. Whatever gets decided before 2030, in every file below, this is the council (and the school boards, and the police board members) in the chairs while it happens. The pre-2030 window and the term you’re voting on are the same years. That’s not rhetoric; it’s the calendar.

Artificial intelligence. Canada still has no AI-specific statute in force: the proposed federal AI act died on the order paper when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025, and the national AI strategy launched in June 2026 routes the rules through targeted bills — privacy, online safety — rather than one framework law. So the decisions that actually bind arrive lower down, and sooner. Ontario’s public-sector digital-trust act is in force, its first regulations landed July 2026, and the AI-specific regulations are still being written — now. Toronto governs its own use of AI through a corporate assessment framework and staff guidance issued in 2025. And procurement is policy: Toronto’s police board already requires board approval before any high-risk AI system — facial recognition explicitly included — is bought or deployed. Every one of those regulations, approvals, and contracts lands years before any statute does, and the people deciding them are on this fall’s ballot or answer to boards appointed by the people who are.

Surveillance. Facial recognition in policing, automated licence-plate readers, algorithmic case management — each arrives as a procurement line item and a board vote, not as a referendum. This isn’t hypothetical here: Toronto’s police fleet already carries plate readers on hundreds of vehicles, scanning over a million plates a day, under policies the police board writes and revises. Once installed, surveillance infrastructure is nearly never uninstalled. The window in which a city decides what watching it will tolerate is before the systems are bought. That window, for this city, is this term.

Data. Who holds the city’s health data, school data, transit data, and on what terms. Canada’s privacy-law modernization — the last attempt died with the same prorogation; its successor was tabled in June 2026 and is before Parliament now, with rules reaching from children’s data to surveillance-based pricing. Open banking moved from promise to law in March 2026 and its first phase rolls out this year. And Ottawa said in January 2026 it will not build a national digital ID — which hands the identity question to provinces, cities, and vendors; which is to say, to procurement. These sound abstract until you notice they decide the default settings of daily life for a generation — and that the contracts implementing them get signed in the next four budget cycles.

Climate lock-in. Infrastructure built now runs for decades — power plants thirty to fifty years, buildings eighty or more — and the world’s climate accounting already shows committed emissions from existing fossil infrastructure consuming most of the remaining budget on its own. The global arithmetic for holding warming near agreed targets runs through cuts of roughly forty per cent this decade — and the decisions that meet or miss that line are disproportionately municipal and provincial: what gets built along transit corridors, whether retrofit programs scale, what power generation the province procures, which pipes and roads and plants get approved in the next four years. Toronto’s own strategy targets net zero by 2040 with interim 2030 goals, and its 2026–2030 action plan was adopted in late 2025; the council elected this fall either funds the path or doesn’t.

Safety and freedom. How this city treats its poorest residents — encampment policy, shelter standards, by-name accountability — is decided in this term’s budgets. So are the norms around protest, public space, and emergency powers, which have been moving fast everywhere. None of this waits for 2030 to become irreversible; it compounds annually.

Notice what this list is not. It’s not a prediction of doom, and it’s not a claim that one election decides everything. It’s an inventory of decisions actually scheduled — legislatively, contractually, budgetarily — inside one council term. “Most important in history” is a register you can argue with. “The decisions about AI, climate, surveillance, data, safety, and freedom being made before 2030 will be made by people chosen this fall, watched — or not — by us” is just the calendar again.

Which is the point of the whole strategy: the decade is being steered. The only question is whether it gets steered from the top of the pyramid by default, or from the base on purpose — by crowds that did their reading, in cities that learned to watch their own machinery. Steering from the base starts embarrassingly small: knowing what’s on the table, showing up prepared, and keeping receipts.
