Somewhere near you votes this fall

This project talks about Toronto because you have to prove a method somewhere, and our backyard came first. But here is the fact this page exists to hand you: on October 26, every municipality in Ontario votes — all of them, the same day. Four hundred and forty-four councils, plus school boards. If you live in Hamilton or Barrie or Thunder Bay or a township most maps skip, your ballot is as real as ours, your races are probably closer, and your council chamber is probably easier to fill.

And the honest secret of this whole strategy is that it was never Toronto-shaped. It’s a method, and methods travel:

The method, in five moves — all documented in the open playbook:

  1. Do the homework first. Map your races, your candidates, what’s actually decided in your town before 2030. Research is the part of this that looks hardest and is most systematized — and it’s the part we’ve packaged separately. The methods codex is the replication kit for the research itself: how to build a candidate atlas, a claims ledger, a verified library for your municipality, with the same receipts discipline we use. That kit is its own project — start at run this in your municipality, the methods codex’s own front door.
  2. Open one narrow door. One link, one counter, thirty seconds to sign. No membership, ever.
  3. Equip rooms you don’t run. Your legion hall, your gurdwara, your rink parents, your farmers’ market — they already gather. Hand them a conversation kit; a conversation counts when it returns a vision, a complaint, or a nomination.
  4. Ask every candidate the same question, publish every answer. The whole election architecture — records, never endorsements — is in piece three and works identically in a township of nine hundred as in a city of three million. Smaller, honestly, is easier: in many Ontario races, a dozen prepared residents at one all-candidates night IS the public discourse.
  5. Keep receipts, publish corrections, design your exit. From day one. It’s what makes you copyable instead of just busy.

A word sized to small places. You don’t need ten thousand conversations. Scale the arithmetic to your population and it gets friendlier fast — a town of ten thousand needs thirty-five committed people to match, proportionally, what the historical record says moves outcomes. One prepared book club is a civic force at that scale. Acclamations are the other opening: hundreds of Ontario seats go uncontested every cycle. If your councillor is running unopposed, the question bank still works — an acclaimed official who answered the town’s questions in writing is still on the record for four years.

Beyond Ontario: other provinces vote on their own calendars, and nothing here checks your postal code. The kits, the frameworks, the whole playbook are free to fork under open licenses — copy them, translate them, strip the Toronto out, and don’t ask permission, because that’s the design. If you build a version for your town, the only thing we’d love back — and it’s optional — is the same thing we publish: your receipts, so the next town starts from your proof instead of ours.

Somewhere near you votes this fall. The room where it gets decided is smaller than you think, and it has an empty chair in it.
