How to convene without an organization

Here is the whole method in one sentence: don’t organize a crowd — equip the people a crowd already trusts.

Toronto holds thousands of gatherings every week. Congregations, union locals, run crews, book clubs, diaspora associations, campus clubs, seniors’ lunches, group chats that have outlived three apartments. Every one of them is a room that already works: it has trust, a rhythm, a person who books the space. A new organization would compete with all of that for the same limited evenings. So we never built one. There is nothing to join, no roster, no dues, no chain of command — and this is not a modesty flourish, it’s the load-bearing design decision everything else sits on.

What exists instead is a set of free tools and one narrow front door.

The door. One link, thirty seconds, a name and a way to reach you. Signing moves a public counter and gives you a place in what opens next — never membership. The ballot has a citizenship requirement; the conversation doesn’t. Students, newcomers, commuters, the cousin abroad — all counted.

The kits. A conversation kit is a script, not a stage: a dinner-party version, a debate-in-a-box, an assembly-in-a-box, a classroom version, a complaint clinic. Each one is designed so a first-time host can run it the same week they find it, in their own name, under their own roof rules. You don’t ask us. You take it, change it, run it. A room that rebuilds a kit to fit itself is the system working, not drift.

The harvest. A conversation counts when it returns something: a vision, a complaint, or a nomination — one QR code, one form, two minutes at the end of the evening. This is the difference between a meeting and a brick in something larger. Ten thousand conversations that each return one harvested thing become a city-scale picture of what Toronto actually wants — assembled in the open, weighted by how many said it, with dissent recorded beside consensus.

The consent floor. Nothing is convened in any community without an invitation from inside it. Ever. The calendar bends; this rule doesn’t. And measurement never becomes surveillance: we count rooms, not people; doors knocked on a block, not who knocked them. The test for any proposed metric: would it embarrass or endanger one person if it leaked? Then it doesn’t exist in that form.

The receipts. Everything harvested comes back as something real — what your contribution became, and the smallest next thing to do. No newsletters. Receipts.

Why does this hold together without a headquarters? Because the coordination lives in the design, not in a command chain: the same simple kits, the same public counter, the same optional citywide goals, the same rule that every room keeps its own name. Five hundred rooms running the same open pattern in their own voice out-coordinate any headquarters — more reach, more trust, no single point of failure, nothing to capture.

The arithmetic that makes it a strategy and not a vibe: a thousand organizations hosting ten conversations each is ten thousand conversations. That’s the whole machine. It needs no permission, including ours.
