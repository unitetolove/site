A year, school-shaped

Every campaign calendar in the country is shaped like a countdown: work backward from voting day, spend everything, stop. We built ours on a different clock, and the difference is the strategy.

The year runs September to September, like a school year. Not because of any ballot — because that’s when the city actually changes rhythm. The first week of September is the closest thing Toronto has to a collective deep breath: schools and campuses return, congregations come back from summer, leagues restart, workplaces refill, group chats wake up. Every network in the city is simultaneously open for exactly one week. If you want to invite a whole city into a conversation, that’s the week the invitation lands everywhere at once — one link, ten thousand posters and orientation tables and group chats.

August is the setup month. Great years are won before they start: the research library opened, the kits printed, the rooms booked, the counter live, the candidate field settled — so that when the city takes its September breath, the machine is already standing. Homework first, always. A crowd that has done its reading is very hard to refuse.

Voting day is the mid-sprint scoreboard, not the finish line. This is the load-bearing reframe. By the time Toronto votes in late October, the sprint is six weeks old: a public plan synthesized from thousands of conversations, commitments locked while the weather is kind, proofs landed every week. Whoever wins inherits a city already in motion — and a published record of what they committed to do about it. The election matters enormously; it just doesn’t get to be the story. The story is a city learning to take care of itself, and it doesn’t pause for the count. Why would a city wait for a ballot to start?

Then the calendar keeps going, because governing does. November is where crowd decisions meet machinery: the new council sworn in with the people’s plan and the adoption record on every desk, first motions already drafted. Winter is for the committee rooms and the warm rooms — the unglamorous months where commitments either hold their dates or don’t. Spring surfaces the work where everyone can see it. Summer audits it honestly, failures in the same typeface as wins. And the following September, the second ignition — which nobody has to light, because last year’s participants are this year’s hosts.

December is for rest, on purpose, designed in from the start. Burnout is the most consistently documented killer of volunteer efforts, and a calendar that never exhales is a burnout machine with a mission statement. One month, no scoreboards, no asks. The gatherings don’t stop; they just stop counting. A movement that can rest without dissolving is the proof it’s alive. Anyone can sprint; the city that learns to breathe is the one worth copying.

If your city’s rhythm is different — different school year, different election date, different festival season — reshape the calendar around your collective deep breath. The principle travels even where the dates don’t: start when every network opens at once, treat the ballot as a checkpoint, audit in public, and put the rest month in before anyone needs it.
