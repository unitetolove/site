How to tell the real thing

Everything on this shelf is free to copy, and we mean it — including by people we’d wince at. That’s the price of open, and we pay it gladly. But it creates one real problem, and it belongs to you, the reader: sooner or later something will show up in your city wearing this costume — “records not endorsements,” “receipts,” “nothing to join” — and you’ll need to know whether it’s the real practice or advocacy in a trench coat.

We can’t police that, and we won’t try: there is no official fork, no certification, no licence to revoke. What we can do is hand you the tells. The genuine article has properties that are expensive to fake and cheap to check — that’s the whole design. Ask these seven questions of any group running this playbook. Including us. Especially us.

1. Does the corrections door actually work? Don’t read their claims — file a correction. A real implementation acknowledges it, rules on it visibly, and fixes every page carrying the error. A costume has a contact form and a silence.

2. Is the candidate treatment checkably identical? Not “do they sound neutral” — can you verify that every candidate in every race got the same materials, the same questions, the same rendering? A real record shows every answer beside every silence, and never ranks. If some candidates seem to get warmer weather, you’re reading an endorsement engine with better manners.

3. Where does the money go — and does anyone ask you for it? The real practice has no membership fee, no donation button on the civic infrastructure, and publishes what things cost when it spends. A costume monetizes trust; the practice refuses to, structurally, so there’s nothing to buy and nothing to capture.

4. Are targets labeled targets? Read their numbers. A real implementation says “goal” when it means goal and “count” when it means count, and its counter would survive an auditor. If the aspiration is wearing the statistic’s clothes, everything else is negotiable too.

5. Do they publish their failures in the same typeface? Ask what went wrong last quarter. The real thing has a written answer, dated, public, unflinching. A brand has a highlight reel.

6. Who gets convened, and who asked? The practice never convenes anyone’s community from outside — it waits for the invitation from inside, every time, no exceptions for deadlines. And it counts rooms, not people: if you find a roster tracking individuals’ participation, you’ve found the surveillance the design exists to prevent.

7. Is the exit designed? Ask who takes over, and when, and what happens if the founder disappears tomorrow. The real practice has answers in writing — named succession, roles that train their replacements, a steward whose stated ambition is to become unnecessary. A personality project has a face where the exit plan should be.

Notice what’s not on this list: tone, polish, size, or whether you agree with what their crowd decided. A real implementation can be scrappy, small, and reach conclusions you dislike — the tells are about how power is held, not what it concludes. And notice that every tell is checkable by an outsider in an afternoon. That’s not an accident; that’s the operating system.

If a group near you passes all seven: that’s the real thing, whoever they are, whatever they call it — they owe us nothing, not even attribution. If it fails two or more: you’ve met the costume. Say so, kindly, with receipts. That’s the practice too.
