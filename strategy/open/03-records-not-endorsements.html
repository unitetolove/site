Records, not endorsements

Every election, the same machinery grinds into motion: endorsements, rankings, report cards, star candidates. We built something different, and it’s stronger. Here is the architecture, free to copy.

The rule underneath: a commons endorses no one and serves everyone identically. Nobody elected us. The moment a shared civic resource picks favourites, it stops being shared — half the city correctly writes it off, and every trailing candidate gets a fair reason to call the whole thing partisan. Most civic projects treat neutrality as a legal nuisance. Treat it instead as the design constraint that forces you to build the stronger machine.

Step one: the crowd makes a plan. Not a manifesto from a back room — a synthesis of thousands of harvested conversations, published with receipts, weighted by how many said each thing, dissent recorded beside consensus.

Step two: every candidate gets the same package. Every candidate, every race, identically — the research library, the question bank, the crowd’s plan. Incumbent or challenger, front-runner or first-timer. No exceptions, forever. This is not generosity; it’s what makes step three unanswerable.

Step three: one identical question. Will you commit to implementing the people’s plan — and which parts? That’s it. Not “do you deserve our endorsement.” A public commitment, in their own words, on the record.

Step four: publish the record. Candidate by candidate, in their own words, beside every silence. Never a ranking, never a score, never a verdict from us. Whoever adopts, is the suite — the “candidates of the movement” assemble themselves by their own public commitments. The crowd supports the plan; candidates earn the crowd by adopting it.

Why this beats endorsement: an endorsement is somebody’s opinion. An adoption record is the candidate’s own commitment — weighable at the ballot box, reviewable all term. When the term starts, the record comes off the shelf: what was promised, what was tabled, what moved, what was quietly dropped. Accountability doesn’t need anger. It needs a filing system and a city that reads.

The same pattern extends past promises to capacity. Instead of scoring candidates ourselves, publish a demonstration record: an open challenge — say, kilometres of sidewalk cleaned, or listening sessions held with published what-we-heard notes — with the receipt standard defined identically for everyone. Candidates publish their own receipts; we record what they submit, identically formatted, never summed, never ranked. The crowd and the press do the comparing. That’s their seat, not ours — and saying so out loud is the whole point.

Two honest cautions for anyone copying this. First, the neutrality is only credible if it’s absolute — one asymmetric favour and years of discipline evaporate; audit yourself harder than your critics do. Second, know your local election law. Publishing identical records of candidates’ own public commitments is a different legal animal from campaigning for the ones you like, and the line varies by jurisdiction — walk it with proper advice, not vibes.
